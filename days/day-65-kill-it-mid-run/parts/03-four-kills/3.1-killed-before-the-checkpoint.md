# 3.1 — K1: killed after the work, before the checkpoint

## One-line answer

A crash between doing a stage and recording it means the stage runs again on resume, which is
correct and is not free: the run log shows the research request paid for twice and the whole run
costs five provider requests instead of four.

## The story

You are applying for something online and the form is long. Twenty minutes of typing. Previous
addresses, dates you have to look up, a reference number off a letter in the other room.

You get to the bottom, and the browser dies. Not the internet — the browser, the whole window, gone.

You open it again and go back to the page and it is empty. Everything you typed is gone, because it
was only ever in the box on the screen. The twenty minutes were real, the typing was real, and none
of it was anywhere that survives.

So you type it all again. It is annoying, but it is not *wrong*: the form is still blank, so filling
it in twice produces one filled-in form. The cost is the twenty minutes, and the twenty minutes are
genuinely gone.

## The idea in plain language

K1 kills the process after a stage has done its work and before that work has been written down.

The stage in this run is **research** — the step that looks through the archive for a related
ticket. In a real system it costs a provider request, which is the thing this curriculum counts as
money. The sequence is:

1. research does the work and the request is paid for;
2. the cost is written to the log;
3. **the process dies here;**
4. what research produced is written to the log.

Because step 4 never happened, the resumed process has no record of what research found. It has no
choice but to run research again. That is the right behaviour and it is worth being precise about
why: the alternative — skipping a stage whose result you do not have — would leave the run
continuing with a hole in it, which is far worse than paying twice.

The property this demonstrates has a name, and it comes up in every conversation about durable
systems. **At-least-once** means every step is guaranteed to run, and may run more than once.
Its opposite, at-most-once, guarantees no step runs twice and accepts that some may not run at all.
You do not get to have both, and at-least-once is the one you want for work that is safe to repeat.

The word that decides which stages are safe is **idempotent**: an operation is idempotent if doing
it twice leaves the world the same as doing it once. Research is idempotent — it reads, it changes
nothing. Closing a ticket is not, and that difference is why part 3.4 exists.

## Why Sutra needs it

This is the kill that makes the phase's cost real rather than theoretical.

Day 60 established that a resumed run reinstates completed stages instead of re-running them, and
that sounds like durability is free. It is not. Every crash costs whatever the in-flight stage had
already spent, and on a free-tier quota measured in requests per day, that is the number the budget
criterion in part 5.4 has to include. A resume that is described as "picking up where it left off"
hides the fact that "where it left off" is always a little behind where it actually got to.

## The mechanism

```bash
cd days/day-65-kill-it-mid-run/lab
uv run python drill.py --kill K1
```

**Line by line:**

- `--kill K1` selects the `during-research` point from the drill's `KILLS` table, so the process
  dies after the research request has been charged and before its result is recorded.
- The drill resets the state directory first, so what you see is one run from a clean floor rather
  than the residue of an earlier experiment.
- Three child processes run in sequence: the one that gets killed, the one that resumes and parks,
  and the one that resumes after the approval and closes the ticket.

Measured on 2026-09-05:

```text
=== K1: killed mid-stage, after the work, before the checkpoint ===
start  k1: ticket 4610
  intake    ran        ticket=4610 text=Signed out mid-form. The customer fill...
  classify  ran        label=auth
  [killed at during-research]
  -> exit 137   [killed]
resume k1: 6 events on disk, stages done ['intake', 'classify']
  intake    reinstated
  classify  reinstated
  research  ran        neighbour=4588
  draft     ran        draft=Ticket 4610: classified auth. See tick...
  review    ran        verdict=needs-approval rubric=v1
  parked    awaiting appr-k1
  spent     3 requests
  -> exit 2   [parks]
  appr-k1 approved by operator-a
resume k1: 13 events on disk, stages done ['intake', 'classify', 'research', 'draft', 'review']
  intake    reinstated
  classify  reinstated
  research  reinstated
  draft     reinstated
  review    reinstated
  close     applied (key k1:appr-k1)
  spent     0 requests
  -> exit 0   [recovered]
  closes for this intent: 1
```

Three things in that output are the whole part.

**`6 events on disk, stages done ['intake', 'classify']`.** The resuming process knows nothing except
what it read from the file. Research is not in `done`, so it will run. The number six is worth
holding on to: the killed process wrote six lines, and the sixth is the research cost with no
research result after it.

**`research ran neighbour=4588`** on the resume, not `reinstated`. The stage happened twice.

**`spent 3 requests`** on the second process. Research, draft and review — three stages, three
requests. The classify request from the first process is not counted here because this process did
not make it, which is exactly why the cost had to be written to the log rather than tallied in
memory.

The proof that the request was paid for twice is in the log itself:

```bash
cat state/runs/k1.log
```

**Line by line:**

- No flags and no script. The evidence for the whole part is six lines of a plain text file, which is
  the argument for one JSON object per line rather than a binary format.


```text
{"cost": 1, "kind": "spent", "stage": "classify"}
{"kind": "stage", "produced": {"label": "auth"}, "stage": "classify"}
{"cost": 1, "kind": "spent", "stage": "research"}
{"cost": 1, "kind": "spent", "stage": "research"}
{"kind": "stage", "produced": {"neighbour": "4588"}, "stage": "research"}
```

Two `spent` lines for research and one `stage` line. Every other stage has one of each. That pair is
the crash, and it is the only place in the system where the price of durability is visible.

## When it breaks

The failure is a resume that decides it can skip the stage anyway, and it is more tempting than it
sounds.

Somebody notices that research is slow and expensive, and that the run has a `neighbour` field in
its state from... somewhere. Maybe a cache. Maybe a previous run of the same ticket. So the resume
gains a shortcut: if `neighbour` is already set, do not run research.

Now kill the process at K1 with a stale `neighbour` from a different ticket in the state, and the
run continues with a plausible, wrong value and never mentions it. There is no error. The draft
cites the wrong prior ticket, the reviewer approves it because it reads fine, and the customer gets
an answer pointing at somebody else's problem.

The version of this you can produce on purpose is milder and makes the same point. Kill *before* the
work instead of after it:

```bash
uv run python -c "import _store; _store.reset()"
uv run python runner.py start 4610 --run-id m1 --kill-at research; echo "exit: $?"
uv run python -c "import _store; print('spend:', _store.spend('m1'))"
```

**Line by line:**

- `--kill-at research` fires `_die` ahead of the stage function rather than after it.
- `_store.spend` totals the durable cost lines, so the difference between the two kills is a number
  rather than an impression.

```text
start  m1: ticket 4610
  intake    ran        ticket=4610 text=Signed out mid-form. The customer fill...
  classify  ran        label=auth
  [killed at research]
exit: 137
spend: 1
```

`spend: 1` against the `2` you get from `during-research`. Same stage, same exit code, one request
of difference — and if the drill had used this kill point, the extra cost of a crash would never
have appeared and part 5.4's budget would be understated by exactly one request per crash.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead.** They make the expensive stage's result cacheable by input
rather than trying to avoid re-running it. If research is keyed by the ticket text, the second call
after a crash is a cache hit and costs nothing, and correctness does not depend on the crash landing
anywhere in particular. That is the honest version of the shortcut above: the check is on the
*input*, not on whether some field happens to be populated.

**What degrades at scale.** The cost of at-least-once is proportional to how long your stages are.
Four short stages lose almost nothing to a crash. One stage that takes a minute and makes twelve
calls loses all twelve, every time, and the mitigation is to make the stage smaller rather than to
make the resume cleverer. Teams usually discover this the first time a nightly job is killed by a
deploy at the ninety per cent mark.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic.** Under load, crashes are not evenly
distributed across stages — they cluster on the slow ones, because the slow ones are where the
process spends its time and where the memory pressure builds. So the stage that costs the most to
repeat is also the stage most likely to be interrupted, and the average cost of a crash in
production is worse than the average cost in a drill.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"You say the resume is free. Your own log has two
cost lines for the same stage, so it is not free — it is one request per crash, and at your crash
rate that is a number you can put in the budget. Put it there."*

**The interview question:** *"Your job crashed halfway through. What runs again?"* The answer that
lands names the boundary rather than the stage: *"Everything after the last durable checkpoint, which
means the stage that was in flight runs again in full. That is at-least-once, and it is only safe
because the stages before the write are all reads. The moment a stage sends something, at-least-once
stops being an acceptable default and needs a key."*

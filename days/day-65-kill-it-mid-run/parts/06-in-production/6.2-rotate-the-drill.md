# 6.2 — Rotate the drill

## One-line answer

A gate that runs the same four kills for ever stops measuring durability and starts measuring
compliance with four kills, so the criteria are a list you add rows to — and part 1.1's warning, that
a gate which finds nothing has not looked, is a standing instruction rather than an observation about
today.

## The story

Everyone learning to drive in the town takes the test on the same route. Out past the retail park,
the tricky right turn by the school, three roundabouts, reverse round the corner on the quiet street
behind the church.

So that is what the instructors teach. Not driving — the route. Which lane for the second roundabout,
where to look at the school turn, which corner is easy to reverse round. Pass rates go up. Everyone
is pleased.

And the drivers are genuinely better at those roundabouts than at any others, which is a real and
completely useless skill, because after the test they will never drive that route again with anyone
watching.

Nobody cheated. The examiner tests exactly what she is supposed to test, honestly, every time. The
route just stopped being a **sample** of driving and became the definition of it.

## The idea in plain language

A drill measures durability by sampling it. Four kills at four moments stand in for every moment a
process could stop, and they are a good sample because part 2.3 chose them at the boundaries where
something changes hands.

They are still a sample. The moment they become permanent, two things happen, and both are gradual.

**The system is optimised for the sample.** Nobody does this dishonestly. A developer finds a
durability bug, fixes it, and checks the fix by running the drill — so the fix is shaped by the four
kills. Repeat for two years and the code is specifically robust at four points and unexamined
everywhere else.

**The sample stops matching the system.** The graph gains stages. The write moves. A cache appears in
front of the ledger. The four kill points were defined against a five-stage graph with one effect, and
they keep passing while the thing they describe drifts away underneath them.

The response is not to distrust the drill. It is to treat the criteria as a **list you add to**, and
the source of new rows is the same in every mature system: every real incident becomes a criterion.
Something broke in a way the gate did not predict; the gate now predicts it.

That is why part 1.2 insisted a criterion is a command with an exit code, and why the seven criteria
in section 5 are seven separate files rather than one script with seven functions. A list of
independent commands is a list you can append to without touching what is already there.

## Why Sutra needs it

This gate runs again. Phase 10 closes with *injection attempts contained; quota router live*, Phase 12
is entirely about evals, and Phase 13 adds tracing and deployment — and each of those phases will
inherit these seven criteria plus its own. If the seven ossify, everything after them inherits a gate
that measures 2026's failure modes.

There is also a specific hostage to fortune in this phase. Part 3.3 and part 3.4 both named a failure
this instrument cannot reach: two processes resuming the same run, with no lease. That is a known gap
with no criterion, which means it is exactly the kind of thing that gets forgotten. Writing it into
the day is the weakest possible mitigation and is better than nothing; adding a criterion when the
system grows a second worker is the real one.

## The mechanism

The criteria are a list, and adding to it is one row:

```python
CRITERIA = [
    ("1 it resumes, once", ["drill.py"], HERE),
    ("2 the gate cannot be bypassed", ["bypass.py"], HERE),
    ("3 the trail answers from rows", ["trail.py"], HERE),
    ("4 the budget is measured", ["budget.py"], HERE),
    ("5 the freshness check", ["fresh.py"], HERE),
    ("6 every day is written", ["written.py"], HERE),
    ("7 no open IDs in the phase", ["ids.py"], HERE),
]
```

**Line by line:**

- The structure is what makes rotation cheap: a **label, a command, a directory**. A new criterion is
  a new file and one row, and nothing existing has to be understood, let alone edited.
- Each is a separate process, so a new criterion cannot break an old one by sharing state with it —
  which is the property that lets somebody add a row confidently on the day after an incident, which
  is the only day they will be motivated to.
- The gate reports **every** failing label rather than stopping at the first, so a list that grows to
  fifteen still produces one actionable report instead of fifteen sequential discoveries.

And the kill points are data too, which is what makes the sample extensible:

```python
KILLS = {
    "K1": ("during-research", "killed mid-stage, after the work, before the checkpoint"),
    "K2": ("after-draft", "killed after a checkpoint, before the gate is filed"),
    "K3": ("parked", "killed while parked, waiting for the human"),
    "K4": ("window", "killed after the decision, inside the effect window"),
}
```

**Line by line:**

- The dictionary carries the point **and the question only that kill can answer**. The second element
  is not a comment; it is what stops somebody deleting a kill for being slow without first
  understanding what coverage they are dropping — part 5.1's narrowing failure.
- Adding `K5` is one entry plus a `--kill-at` label the runner recognises. That is deliberately the
  smallest possible amount of work, because the moment to add a kill is the morning after an incident,
  when everyone is tired.

The obvious next rows for this instrument, each corresponding to a gap this day has named rather than
solved:

| Row | The failure it would catch | Named in |
| --- | --- | --- |
| two resumers, same run | interleaved logs, double close under concurrency | 3.3, 3.4 |
| resume across a code change | checkpoint written by a graph that no longer exists | 3.3, and Day 60 |
| approval that never arrives | a parked run with no timeout and no owner | 3.3 |
| the drain that exceeds its grace period | the systematic hard kill on every deploy | 6.1 |

## When it breaks

The failure is not that somebody refuses to add a criterion. It is that the drill becomes slow and
annoying, and gets **moved** rather than deleted — out of the pre-commit hook, into the nightly job,
into the weekly job, into a job that has been failing for a month and everyone knows it.

The tell is a gate whose red is routine. Once a criterion is known to be red for a reason nobody
intends to fix, the whole gate's output stops being read, and the eighth criterion — the one somebody
added after an incident — goes red six weeks later and nobody notices, because the gate has been red
since March.

This day has a live example, and it should be stated as one rather than left in a footnote. The
finding that `mcp==1.29.1` is pinned with no row in `docs/PACKAGES.md` was raised by **Day 52's phase
gate** and again by **Day 59's phase gate**, and criterion 5 raises it a third time in part 5.5. Three
gates, one unfixed defect. That is not a fact about a package version. It is the mechanism above,
caught early: a finding that survives repeated gates is training everyone to read that section of the
output as noise.

The rule that keeps a gate alive is therefore not about adding criteria at all. It is that **every red
either gets fixed or gets a decision**, written down, with a name on it — *we accept this, because*.
An accepted finding stops being red and becomes a recorded exception, and the gate goes back to
meaning something. A finding that is neither fixed nor accepted is the one that rots the gate.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead.** Chaos experiments are generated rather than enumerated: kill
at a random point, many times, and let coverage accumulate across runs. That converts the criterion
from *these four moments are safe* to *no failure observed in ten thousand random kills*, which is a
weaker claim per run and a much stronger one over time. The named kills stay, as regression tests for
failures that actually happened.

**What degrades at scale.** A growing criteria list gets slow, and slow is what gets it skipped. The
usual resolution is tiering: a fast subset on every commit, the full set on merge, the chaos
experiments on a schedule against a real environment. That is a sound trade as long as it is a
**stated** one — the failure is doing it silently, so that people believe the commit-time check is the
gate. Part 5.1 measured that exact deception: K1 alone is green on a system that closes tickets twice.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic.** Incidents you did not have. The criteria
list is, by construction, a list of failures you have already met or predicted, and the next one will
be neither. This is the honest limit of every gate, and the reason the *In production* section of
every part in this day names failures the drill cannot reach. A gate is a floor, not a ceiling, and a
team that treats a green gate as proof of durability has misread it in exactly the way part 1.1
warned about.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"We have had this same finding on three consecutive
gates. Either fix it this week or write down that we accept it and why, with your name on it. What we
are doing now is teaching everyone that the bottom of this report is optional."*

**The interview question:** *"Your chaos tests have passed every week for a year. What does that tell
you?"* The answer that shows experience is suspicious of it: *"Less than it looks like. It tells me
the specific failures I thought of a year ago are still handled, and the system has probably been
shaped to handle exactly those. It does not tell me the sample still matches the system — the graph
has changed, the write has probably moved, and the kill points were chosen against the old shape. So I
would want to know when a criterion was last added, and the healthiest answer is 'after the last
incident', because that is what a gate that is still alive looks like. I would also look for findings
that have been red for months, because those are the ones training people to stop reading the
output."*

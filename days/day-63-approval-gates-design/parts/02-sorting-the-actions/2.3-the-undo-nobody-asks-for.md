# 2.3 — The undo nobody asks for

## One-line answer

An undo is only worth what the alarm in front of it is worth: `reopen` is free and instant, and
three of Sutra's ten actions carry a `detect_hours` of **72 or more**, which makes their undos
theoretical rather than available.

## The story

You have a slow puncture.

It is the cheapest possible fault. Any roadside shop fixes it in a few minutes for a few rupees, and
you would happily have it done today if you knew.

You do not know. The tyre goes down by a very small amount each day, and a tyre that is slightly
soft looks exactly like a tyre. Nothing tells you. Three days later you come out in the morning
already late, and it is flat, and now the cheap repair is not the problem — the problem is that you
are standing in the lane with a flat tyre at the moment you cannot afford one.

The repair was never expensive. The repair was never *available*, because availability of a repair
is the repair plus the knowing, and you only had one of the two.

## The idea in plain language

The previous two parts established that an action can be sorted by whether it can be undone
([2.1](2.1-reversible-and-the-cost-of-the-undo.md)) and by who it touches
([2.2](2.2-blast-radius-counted-in-people.md)). Both of those assume something neither of them
states: that somebody **finds out**.

**Detection time** is how long, in the worst realistic case, before anybody notices that a
particular action was wrong. It is the third axis, and it is the one that decides whether the first
two mean anything, because:

> An undo behind a slow alarm is not a control. It is a thing you could have done.

The reason this axis is so easy to leave out is that it is the only one of the three that is not a
property of your code. Reversibility is in your codebase. Radius is in your data model. Detection
time is a property of **somebody else's behaviour** — whether the morning shift reads the note,
whether the paged colleague asks why, whether the customer can be bothered to write back — and
there is nothing in your repository to look it up from.

So it has to be written down deliberately, and it has to be written down as a **mechanism** rather
than a duration. "We'd notice quickly" is a feeling. "The morning shift reads the note on the same
ticket" is a mechanism, and once it is written as a mechanism you can ask the useful question: does
that mechanism actually exist, and does it run?

There is a phrase for what the gap buys you, and it is worth naming because it is what the whole
sorting exercise is trying to minimise:

- **The exposure window** is the time between a wrong action happening and anybody knowing. During
  the window the wrong thing is simply true, and everything downstream that reads it treats it as
  true.

## Why Sutra needs it

`close_ticket` looks like the easiest row in the table to leave ungated. It is state-reversible,
`reopen` has existed since Day 37, and it costs nothing. A reasonable engineer sorting on
[2.1](2.1-reversible-and-the-cost-of-the-undo.md)'s narrow column and glancing at the cheap undo
would put it in the free half without much argument.

This part is what stops that, and it is the argument that finally settles the row in
[3.2](../03-the-policy-table/3.2-the-table-and-the-argument.md): the undo is free and the alarm in
front of it takes three days and frequently never rings at all.

## The mechanism

Each action's undo, paired with the time before anybody asks for it:

```bash
cd days/day-63-approval-gates-design/lab
uv run python visible.py
```

**Line by line:**

- `detect_hours` is a field on the inventory, and it is the only field there that is an estimate
  rather than a lookup. It is the worst realistic case, not the average, because a control is sized
  by its bad days.
- `who_notices()` is a separate function returning a **sentence naming the mechanism**, not a
  number. The number without the mechanism is unarguable and therefore unreviewable; with the
  sentence, a reviewer can say "the morning shift does not read those notes any more" and the
  estimate can be corrected.
- The script prints the gate alongside, so the correlation between a long window and a gate is
  visible in the same table rather than asserted afterwards.

Measured on 2026-09-05:

```text
  action                  detected in   undo         gate       who notices
  ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  lookup_ticket           -             -            never      nothing to notice
  search_kb               -             -            never      nothing to notice
  search_tickets          -             -            never      nothing to notice
  load_memory             -             -            never      nothing to notice
  record_triage           0.5h          overwrite    never      the morning shift reads the note on the same ticket
  reindex_start           1h            re-run       never      the next search returns nonsense and somebody says so
  escalate_to_engineer    0.2h          -            threshold  the colleague replies asking why they were paged
  close_ticket            72h           reopen       always     the customer writes back, if they bother
  post_reply              72h           -            always     the customer writes back, if they bother
  refund_order            168h          -            always     the monthly reconciliation, or nobody

  actions nobody notices within a day: 3
    close_ticket           undo = reopen     but nobody asks for it for 72h
    post_reply             undo = -          but nobody asks for it for 72h
    refund_order           undo = -          but nobody asks for it for 168h

  `reopen` is a real undo and it is free. It is also never called, because
  the signal that would call it takes three days to arrive and usually never does.
```

The table sorts itself, and it is worth noticing that nobody arranged it to.

Look at the `who notices` column top to bottom. The fast rows are noticed by **us** — the morning
shift, the next search, the paged colleague — and every one of them is ungated. The slow rows are
noticed by **the customer**, and every one of them is gated. The detection mechanism and the blast
radius are the same fact seen from two ends: an effect that stays inside the company is noticed by
somebody inside the company, quickly, because they are looking at the same screens you are.

Now `escalate_to_engineer`, which is the sharpest row here. Its undo column is `-`: there is no
undo at all, you cannot un-wake a person. By [2.1](2.1-reversible-and-the-cost-of-the-undo.md)'s
axis alone it is the most irreversible thing in the list. And its `detect_hours` is **0.2**,
because the colleague you woke replies within twelve minutes asking why.

That combination — no undo, instant detection — is why it is the one row in the table gated by a
condition instead of a verdict. Fast detection does not repair the harm, but it does bound it: the
mistake is caught while it is one page to one person, rather than compounding. An action with no
undo and a three-day window would be a different matter entirely, and that is `post_reply`, which is
gated `always` with no argument.

And then the last two lines, which are the honest ones. `reopen` works. `reopen` is free. There is
no evidence in this batch or any other that `reopen` has ever been called on a ticket the agent
closed, and there would not be, because the only thing that would call it is a customer writing
back — and a customer whose problem was closed without being solved mostly does not write back.
They just do not come back at all, and that is a signal your system does not receive.

## When it breaks

The failure is a detection mechanism that is written down and does not run.

Here is the concrete version, and it is not hypothetical for Sutra. `record_triage` is ungated, and
the entire argument for that is the sentence *"the morning shift reads the note on the same ticket"*
— half an hour of exposure, then somebody sees the wrong severity and fixes it. That sentence is
the load-bearing member of the decision.

Then the morning shift starts trusting the agent. Not through a decision, and not on any particular
day: the notes have been right for six weeks, the queue is long, and reading the agent's summary
carefully stops being how anybody spends a Tuesday. The mechanism is still written in the table. The
`0.5h` is still in the field. Nothing was edited, nothing failed, and the actual exposure window for
a wrong triage note is now however long it takes for the mistake to surface somewhere else —
which, given that these notes are indexed and retrieved
([2.2](2.2-blast-radius-counted-in-people.md)'s break), may be weeks and may be never.

Two of the six wrong writes in the recorded batch are exactly this: `ticket:4607` with a P1 filed as
P3, and `ticket:4618` filed as new when it duplicates `ticket:4610`. The policy waves both through
on the strength of that half-hour, and if the half-hour is fiction then the policy is resting on it.

The tell that you are in this failure is that **nobody has fixed anything in a while**. A detection
mechanism that works produces corrections, at some steady rate, and those corrections are visible.
If the notes have not been corrected by a human in two months, either the agent is perfect or nobody
is reading, and one of those is much more likely than the other. That check is cheap and almost
nobody runs it.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead.** They make the detection mechanism produce a **countable
event**, and then they count it. Not "the morning shift reads the note" but a correction rate: how
many agent-written triage notes were edited by a human last week? That number is the health of the
mechanism, it costs nothing to collect, and when it falls to zero it has told you that an ungated
row lost its justification without anybody touching the policy file.

**What degrades at scale.** Detection mechanisms that rely on a person noticing something anomalous
degrade with volume, and they degrade faster than the volume grows, because noticing is a comparison
against what is normal and more traffic makes anything look normal. A colleague paged once a week
asks why. A colleague paged four times a night stops asking, and the `detect_hours: 0.2` claim on
`escalate_to_engineer` — the entire reason that row is allowed a threshold rather than a hard gate —
quietly becomes as long as the other windows.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic.** The customer signal is not just slow; it
is **biased**, and the bias points the wrong way. Customers who write back are the ones still
engaged. The ones whose ticket was wrongly closed and who concluded the company does not read
tickets do not write back at all, so the wrongly-closed tickets you learn about are a sample that
systematically excludes the worst outcomes. Your detection rate looks like a measurement of your
error rate and it is a measurement of your customers' patience.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"detect_hours 0.5 on record_triage, mechanism 'the
morning shift reads the note'. When did a human last edit one of these notes? If the answer is 'I
don't know' or 'a while ago', this row's exposure window is not thirty minutes and the reason it is
ungated has evaporated. Put a counter on it before we ship the policy."*

**The interview question:** *"Your agent can do something wrong and there is a cheap undo. Is that
enough?"* An honest answer: *"No, because an undo is only as good as the alarm in front of it. In
our system reopening a ticket is one line and free, and the only thing that triggers it is a
customer writing back, which takes about three days when it happens at all — so the wrong closes we
find out about are a biased sample of the ones we did, biased towards customers who still care.
That is why closing is gated even though it is technically reversible. Meanwhile paging a colleague
has no undo whatsoever and is gated by a condition rather than always, because the colleague replies
in twelve minutes and that bounds the damage. The number I want written next to every action is not
'is there an undo' but 'what makes anybody ask for it, and how long does that take'."*

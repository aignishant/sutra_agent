# 2.4 — Defeated by its own volume

## One-line answer

Frequency is not a safety property and must never enter the decision about whether an action is
dangerous — but it decides whether the gate you designed is the gate that actually runs, so it is
measured separately and kept visible: 41 approvals a night, 9, or 11, from the same batch.

## The story

The smoke alarm in the kitchen goes off when you fry onions.

It is not broken. It is doing precisely what it was built to do, and frying onions genuinely
produces smoke. It goes off on Monday, and Wednesday, and twice on Sunday when there are guests.

You know how this ends, because it is how it ends in every house. Somebody stands on a chair with a
tea towel and waves at it, and that works for a while, and then one evening somebody stands on the
chair and takes the battery out instead. Nobody decides that the house no longer needs a smoke
alarm. The battery is on the shelf and everybody means to put it back.

The alarm was defeated by being right too often about something that did not matter. It will be
silent for the one fire, and the reason will not be that anybody stopped caring about fires.

## The idea in plain language

The previous three parts sorted actions by properties of the **action**: can it be undone, who does
it touch, when would anybody find out. Those are the axes that decide whether an action is
dangerous.

Frequency is not one of them. How often something happens says nothing whatsoever about how bad it
is when it goes wrong, and an action that moves money is exactly as serious on its five-hundredth
occurrence as its first.

This matters enough to be written into the code. In `_policy.py`, the function that proposes a gate
from the facts — `suggest()` — deliberately does not read the frequency, and says why in its
docstring:

> *Frequency is deliberately NOT in here. Volume is a reason to redesign an action, never a reason
> to stop gating a dangerous one, and a scoring function that let it in would quietly trade safety
> for convenience.*

That is the trap this part exists to name. Once frequency is an input to the danger score, a
sufficiently common action can score its way out of being gated, and the resulting table is
indistinguishable from a table where somebody thought about risk. The trade got made, and it got
made invisibly, by arithmetic.

So frequency is measured **separately**, and it answers a different question:

- The danger axes answer: *should this be gated?*
- Frequency answers: *will the gate you designed survive contact with the shift?*

Both are real questions. Keeping them apart is what lets you notice when the answers conflict —
because when they do, the correct response is not to relax the gate. It is to **redesign the
action** so that fewer of them reach the gate: a classifier confident enough to handle the easy
cases, a batching change, a narrower tool. Those are engineering problems, and they only get worked
on if the conflict stays visible instead of being resolved by a weight in a formula.

## Why Sutra needs it

[1.1](../01-the-gate-that-means-nothing/1.1-a-control-that-fires-on-everything.md) showed that a
41-item queue catches fewer mistakes than an 11-item one. It did not say where 41 came from.

This part is where the counts are established, per action, from the record — and where the three
candidate policies get their queue depths. Those depths are what
[6.4](../06-gates-that-are-decoration/6.4-the-sweep-nobody-runs.md) sweeps against attention, and
what the hub's §6 request budget is denominated in. Without the counts, "the queue is too long" is
another adjective.

## The mechanism

Three candidate policies, counted against the same night:

```bash
cd days/day-63-approval-gates-design/lab
uv run python volume.py
```

**Line by line:**

- `CANDIDATES` holds three predicates in the order teams actually propose them: gate every write,
  gate only what a customer sees, and the day's policy. The third is `None` in the dict and handled
  by `asked_by_policy()`, because it is the only one with a threshold and therefore the only one
  that needs to read a step's severity rather than just its name.
- The per-action counts come from `Action.per_shift`, which is a **property that counts the record**
  rather than a stored number. It was a stored number for one draft of this lab, and two of the ten
  values were already wrong against the batch the first time the file ran. A hand-typed count that
  can silently disagree with the thing it counts is the same class of defect this whole day is
  about, so it is now derived and cannot drift.
- The reads are printed at the end deliberately, to keep the write counts in proportion.

Measured on 2026-09-05:

```text
  what fired, per night
    lookup_ticket           27   gate: never
    search_kb               27   gate: never
    search_tickets          27   gate: never
    load_memory             27   gate: never
    record_triage           27   gate: never
    reindex_start            1   gate: never
    escalate_to_engineer     4   gate: threshold
    close_ticket             9   gate: always

  approvals asked, over 1 night(s)
    every write                      41   (41 per night)
    every customer-visible write      9   (9 per night)
    POLICY                           11   (11 per night)

  reads in the same batch: 108 - none of them gated, and that is most of the work
```

Three numbers, and the interesting comparison is not the one people expect.

**41 against 11** is the comparison from [1.1](../01-the-gate-that-means-nothing/1.1-a-control-that-fires-on-everything.md),
and it is settled: 41 is a queue nobody finishes.

**9 against 11** is the one worth arguing about. The middle candidate — gate only what a customer
sees — asks for two fewer approvals a night and is the more tempting policy for exactly that reason.
The two it drops are the escalations at P2 and P3, and dropping them means nobody is ever asked
before a colleague is woken for something that could have waited until morning. Two approvals a
night is what that costs, and the policy pays it, because
[2.3](2.3-the-undo-nobody-asks-for.md) established that paging has no undo at all.

The point of putting the three side by side is that the difference between them is **two items**.
Not two hundred. When the numbers are this close, arguing about which policy is more "practical" is
not an argument about workload — it is an argument about whether waking a colleague is a thing you
would like somebody to have decided.

Then the last line. **One hundred and eight reads, none of them gated**, against forty-one writes.
Most of what the agent does cannot be wrong in any way a human could catch, and a design that starts
from "the agent does a lot, so approving all of it is impossible" has misread its own system. The
volume problem is a problem about a quarter of the work.

Now the number that predicts the future:

```bash
uv run python volume.py --batches 5
```

**Line by line:**

- `--batches 5` multiplies the queue by five nights, which is what one person on a weekly rota
  actually faces. Nothing about the batch changes; only the horizon does.

```text
  approvals asked, over 5 night(s)
    every write                     205   (41 per night)
    every customer-visible write     45   (9 per night)
    POLICY                           55   (11 per night)
```

Two hundred and five is not a queue. It is a full-time job that nobody has been hired for, and it is
the number that gets the battery taken out of the alarm.

## When it breaks

The characteristic failure is not a policy that is too strict. It is a policy that is too strict
**and then quietly stops being enforced**, with no commit that says so.

The sequence is always the same and there is no villain in it. The gate ships covering every write.
The queue is long. Somebody adds a "bulk approve" button for the internal notes, because approving
twenty-seven identical notes one at a time is absurd and they are right. The button gets used for
the notes, and then for the notes and the reindex, and then for whatever is on the screen, because
the screen is a list and the button approves the list.

Nothing in the codebase records this. The policy file still says every write is gated. The audit
log still shows a human approving every write. Both are true. The catch rate is zero and there is no
artefact anywhere in the system that would tell you.

The tell is a **timing distribution**, and it is worth knowing because it is the only cheap
instrument for this failure. Approvals answered by a human who read something have a spread of
response times — some fast, some slow, a few very slow where they went and checked. Approvals
answered by somebody clearing a screen arrive in bursts of near-identical timestamps. You do not
need to interpret anything subtle: eleven approvals within the same second is not eleven decisions.

Sutra will not meet this on the recorded batch, because eleven items is a queue a person finishes.
It meets it the night the classifier regresses and the queue is four hundred, which is
[2.2](2.2-blast-radius-counted-in-people.md)'s incident seen from the approver's side.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead.** They put the expected queue depth **in the policy table, as
a column**, next to the verdict. A gate whose row says `always, ~9/night` is a design somebody can
review. A gate whose row says `always` is a design whose operational cost is discovered by the
person on the rota, several weeks after the decision, with no way to trace it back to the meeting
where it was made. It costs one column and it is the difference between a policy and a wish.

**What degrades at scale.** The gated proportion is what matters, not the absolute count, and it is
usually stable — which is the bad news, because it means queue depth grows linearly with traffic
forever. Nine closes a night at current volume is ninety at ten times, and there is no arrangement
of humans that reviews ninety customer-visible decisions a night properly. Long before that, the
design has to change: auto-close the confident cases and gate the rest, so the gate sees a
proportion that *falls* as the classifier improves rather than a count that rises with traffic.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic.** Approval load is bursty and the rota is
not. The batch does not spread its eleven approvals across the night; it files them between 02:00
and 03:00 and they sit until 09:30 — which is
[5.2](../05-when-nobody-answers/5.2-the-answer-nobody-gave.md)'s subject, and it means the queue a
person sees at the start of a shift is the whole night at once. Average queue depth is the number
everyone reports and peak queue depth is the number that decides whether anybody reads.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"How many of these will there be per night? The
policy has verdicts and no counts, so I cannot tell whether this is eleven approvals or four
hundred, and those are different designs with the same table. Add the measured per-night count as a
column — and if any single row is over about twenty, the fix is to make fewer of them happen, not
to loosen the row."*

**The interview question:** *"How do you stop an approval gate from becoming a rubber stamp?"* An
honest answer: *"By treating the queue depth as a design constraint and keeping it out of the risk
score. Frequency must never make a dangerous action look safe — our scoring function refuses to read
it, on purpose — but it decides whether the gate survives the shift, so it is measured separately
and written in the table as a column. Ours is eleven a night against forty-one for gate-everything.
When a row's count gets too high the fix is to reduce what reaches the gate, usually by making the
classifier confident enough to handle the easy cases, not by relaxing the row. And the instrument I
watch for the failure is the timing distribution on approvals, because eleven approvals inside one
second is one decision, not eleven, and nothing else in the system will tell you that."*

# 3.1 — The write inventory

## One-line answer

A policy is worth exactly as much as the inventory it was written against, so the inventory comes
first: ten actions, eight of them shipped, two of them rows written before the tool exists — and a
check that the count of actions and the count of rows are equal.

## The story

The shop takes stock on the last Sunday of the month.

Not from the register — from the shelves. Two people go through with a clipboard and count what is
actually there, aisle by aisle, including the box behind the ladder that nobody has moved since
March.

The owner already knows what he has. He is in the shop every day. Every month he is wrong about
something anyway: a line he stopped ordering that still has four units, a size he was certain he was
out of, a carton somebody accepted at the back door and never entered anywhere. None of it is
mysterious and none of it is anybody's fault. It is just that the picture in your head of a place
you know well is assembled from what you have thought about recently, and the shelf is assembled
from everything that ever happened.

The count is boring, and it takes a Sunday, and it is the only thing that turns the picture in his
head into a fact.

## The idea in plain language

An **inventory** is the list of every action the agent can take, gathered by looking rather than by
remembering.

That word — *looking* — is the whole content of this part. Everybody writing an approval policy
already has an inventory in their head, and it is assembled from the actions they have thought about
recently. The dangerous ones are in it, because they are the ones that prompted the policy. What is
missing is the action added on a Thursday for a reason unrelated to safety, the one behind a flag,
and the one that is a write only because somebody added a field to it last month.

The inventory is deliberately made of **facts and not verdicts**. For each action it records what
changes, whether an undo exists, who is affected, how long before anybody notices, and whether the
effect leaves the company. It records no opinion about gating at all.

Keeping those apart matters more than it looks. Facts about an action are stable — `close_ticket`
touches a customer whatever anybody decides about it — and verdicts change when the team changes, or
the rota changes, or an incident happens. Mixed into one table, a change of mind about a verdict
looks like a change of fact, and the next reader cannot tell which parts were measured and which
were argued. In the lab they are two files: `_actions.py` holds the facts, `_policy.py` holds the
decisions, and `_policy` imports `_actions` and never the other way round.

One rule about what belongs in the inventory, and it is the one that feels wrong the first time:

> **An action that does not exist yet still gets a row.**

Day 44 already did this. Its retry policy carries a row for `refund_order` — a tool no server Sutra
talks to offers — precisely so that
[the day somebody connects a payments server](../../../day-44-client-hardening/parts/01-what-may-be-repeated/1.3-the-line-through-every-call.md),
the policy already has an opinion. This is Principle 13, blast radius before capability, and the row
costs one line.

## Why Sutra needs it

[3.2](3.2-the-table-and-the-argument.md) writes a verdict per action, and
[3.4](3.4-the-tuesday-a-tool-arrives.md) is about what happens to an action with no verdict at all.
Both of those need a list that is complete, and "complete" needs a check rather than a feeling.

There is also a specific thing this inventory has to catch for Sutra. The curriculum has added tools
across fifteen phases — `lookup_ticket` on Day 15, `close_ticket` on Day 37, `load_memory` on Day 46,
`search_tickets` on Day 49 — and no single document has ever listed them together with what they do
to the world. Until this part, that list did not exist anywhere in the repository.

## The mechanism

Every action the agent can reach, with the day it arrived and whether it has shipped:

```bash
cd days/day-63-approval-gates-design/lab
uv run python inventory.py
```

**Line by line:**

- `INVENTORY` is a tuple of frozen `Action` dataclasses in `_actions.py`. Frozen because an
  inventory that code can edit at runtime is not an inventory; it is a cache.
- The `introduced` field names the day each capability arrived, so a reader can go and read what it
  was for. `0` means it has not arrived — those are the rows written ahead of the tool.
- The last four lines are the actual check: the set of action names, the set of policy row keys, and
  both differences. Printing `none` twice is the only output that means the inventory is complete.
  A policy with nine rows for ten actions is not a policy with a small gap; it is a policy with one
  action that runs unreviewed, and nobody decided that.

Measured on 2026-09-05:

```text
  action                  from    effect                      gate       ships?
  ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  lookup_ticket           day 15  reads one ticket            never      yes
  search_kb               day 15  reads the knowledge base    never      yes
  search_tickets          day 49  ranks the archive           never      yes
  load_memory             day 46  reads prior triage notes    never      yes
  record_triage           day 48  writes an internal note     never      yes
  reindex_start           day 49  rebuilds the index          never      yes
  escalate_to_engineer    day 62  pages a colleague           threshold  yes
  close_ticket            day 37  closes a customer ticket    always     yes
  post_reply              -       sends words to a customer   always     not yet
  refund_order            -       moves money                 always     not yet

  actions: 10   policy rows: 10
  actions with no row:  none
  rows with no action:  none
  rows written before the tool exists: ['post_reply', 'refund_order']
```

Read the `from` column first, because it is the argument for doing this at all.

The tools arrived on days 15, 37, 46, 48, 49 and 62 — six different days, spread across four phases,
each added for its own reason by somebody thinking about that day's subject. Nobody adding
`search_tickets` on Day 49 was thinking about approval policy; they were thinking about retrieval.
That is not a criticism, it is how systems are built, and it is exactly why the list has to be
gathered by looking.

Then the `ships?` column. Two rows say `not yet`, and they are the two most dangerous actions in the
table. `post_reply` sends words that cannot be recalled; `refund_order` moves money. Neither exists.
Both are already gated.

That ordering is the whole of Principle 13 in one table. The alternative — write the row when the
tool ships — sounds equivalent and is not, because the day a payments tool gets added is a day
somebody is trying to ship payments, and "we should discuss the approval policy" is a conversation
that loses to a deadline. The row written today costs one line and is not competing with anything.

Finally, the two totals. `actions: 10   policy rows: 10`, and both difference lists empty. That is a
check and not a claim, and it is worth noticing that it is the *only* thing in this output a script
could have got wrong on its own.

## When it breaks

The inventory breaks by being written once.

It is correct on the day it is written — genuinely correct, the counts match, the differences are
empty — and it decays, because the codebase keeps moving and the file does not. Every tool added
after today is a row that should exist and does not, and the check that would catch it only runs if
somebody runs it.

This is the same failure Day 40 met from the other side. There, an MCP server changed under a client
that had already agreed to a tool list, and
[a tool appeared on a Tuesday](../../../day-40-filtering-and-allowlists/parts/03-when-the-server-changes/3.1-the-tuesday-a-tool-appeared.md).
The lesson was the same: the list you agreed to is only a control while somebody is comparing it to
reality.

There is a second and more interesting break, and Sutra is already exposed to it. The inventory is
keyed on **tool names**, and an action's effect can change without its name changing. `record_triage`
writes an internal note; if somebody adds a `status` field to it, the same name now closes tickets,
and the inventory still says `writes an internal note` with radius `us`. Nothing in this file would
notice. That is [6.1](../06-gates-that-are-decoration/6.1-the-road-the-gate-is-not-on.md), and it is
the reason the failure lab exists.

The honest limitation of the mechanism as written: `inventory.py` compares the policy against
`_actions.py`, which is a hand-maintained list. It proves the policy covers the inventory. It cannot
prove the inventory covers the codebase, because nothing here reads the codebase. A real version
derives the action list from the registered toolset at runtime, and the hub's build brief asks for
exactly that.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead.** They generate the inventory from the **live toolset** rather
than maintaining a list, and they make the completeness check a test that fails the build. The shape
is: enumerate the tools actually registered on the agent, diff against the policy keys, fail on any
difference in either direction. Once that test exists, adding a tool without classifying it is a red
build rather than a silent gap, and the conversation happens at the right moment — in the pull
request that adds the tool.

**What degrades at scale.** Inventories go stale in proportion to how many people can add a tool.
With one author it is a discipline problem and discipline works. With six teams and a plugin system,
the number of tools is not a number anybody knows, and a hand-maintained list is not late — it is
fiction. Sutra will hit a version of this on Day 68, where an MCP toolset supplies tools the repo
never declared.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic.** A tool can be in the inventory, classified,
gated correctly, and never fire — and an ungated one can quietly become the busiest write in the
system. The inventory says nothing about this, because it lists capability rather than use. That is
what `per_shift` is for, and it is why [2.4](../02-sorting-the-actions/2.4-defeated-by-its-own-volume.md)
counts from the record rather than from the list. An inventory without usage counts tells you what
could happen; the pair tells you what does.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Good list, and it matches the policy today. What
makes it still match in November? If the answer is that somebody remembers to update it, this is
documentation and not a control. Derive it from the registered toolset and fail the build on a
mismatch — otherwise the first tool added by someone who has not read this file is invisible."*

**The interview question:** *"Where do you start when designing approvals for an agent?"* An honest
answer: *"With the inventory, and I gather it by looking rather than remembering, because the list
in my head is the actions I have recently worried about. Ours came out at ten, added across six
different days over four phases, and nobody adding a retrieval tool in phase seven was thinking
about approval policy. I keep the facts and the verdicts in separate files so a change of mind does
not look like a change of fact, I include rows for tools that do not exist yet — we gate a refund
tool nobody has built, because the day somebody builds it they will be busy shipping payments — and
I make the completeness check a build failure rather than a habit. A hand-maintained list is
accurate on the day it is written and fiction three months later."*

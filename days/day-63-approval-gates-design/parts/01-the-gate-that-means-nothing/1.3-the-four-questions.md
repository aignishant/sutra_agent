# 1.3 — The four questions

## One-line answer

"`close_ticket` needs approval" is a gate specification that answers one of four required questions,
and each of the other three, left unanswered, is a specific failure with a name that this day
reproduces.

## The story

You take a scooter to a workshop and the mechanic writes a job card. It says:

> *Servicing.*

That is all it says. You come back in the evening and the bill is four times what you expected,
because "servicing" turned out to include two new tyres. You did not ask for tyres. The mechanic
says you agreed to servicing and tyres are part of servicing, and he is not lying — he genuinely
thinks so.

Neither of you did anything dishonest. The job card had one word on it, and one word cannot hold
the four things you both needed written down: **what** work exactly, **what it would cost**, **who
was allowed to authorise extra work** when the mechanic found something, and **what he should do**
if he could not reach you.

A better workshop's job card has four lines instead of one. It is not more bureaucratic. It is the
same job, written down at the size of the actual agreement.

## The idea in plain language

Most approval gates are specified in a sentence in a design document: *"customer-visible actions
require human approval."* That sentence feels complete. It is not, and the gap is not vagueness that
could be tightened later — it is three whole questions that were never asked.

A gate specification has to answer four:

1. **Which action, and on which arguments?** Not the tool name — the *effect*. If two code paths
   produce the same effect and the gate names only one of them, the other is an open road.
2. **What exactly is the approver shown?** Not "the request". The specific bytes. If the gate
   records a pointer to something that can change, the thing approved and the thing executed can
   differ.
3. **Who may answer, and who may not?** A signature from the party that proposed the action is not
   a check on that party. Two signatures on the same evidence are one signature, twice.
4. **What happens when the answer is no?** If a rejection merely ends one attempt, the system can
   re-ask until it finds a yes, and then "rejected" was never a state the system could be in.

The reason these four and not five or three: each one, unanswered, is a **different** failure with a
different fix, and this day reproduces all four. That is the test for whether a checklist is real —
every item has to correspond to something that actually goes wrong.

## Why Sutra needs it

Tomorrow, Day 64, implements the gate. The implementation reads a specification, and whatever the
specification leaves out becomes whatever was easiest to code — which is the reason the plan splits
design and build across two days at all. A gate you design while building is a gate shaped like the
convenient path.

So this part is the shape of the artefact Day 63 hands Day 64. Sections 3, 4 and 5 fill in the four
answers for Sutra's real actions; this part is the checklist that says when they are done.

## The mechanism

`four.py` is a checker. Hand it a gate specification and it reports which questions it has not
answered and what each hole costs:

```bash
cd days/day-63-approval-gates-design/lab
uv run python four.py
```

**Line by line:**

- `QUESTIONS` is a dict of four entries, each holding the question and the failure its absence
  produces. Keeping the failure next to the question is the point: a checklist item with no named
  consequence is an item people skip.
- `SPECS` holds three real specifications in the order teams actually write them — the sentence
  somebody puts in a design doc, the version that survives one review, and the one that answers
  everything.
- `gaps(spec)` is four lines and it is the whole checker: it returns the keys of `QUESTIONS` that
  the specification has no entry for. There is no cleverness here because there does not need to
  be; the value is in having written the four down at all.

Measured on 2026-09-05:

```text
'the sentence': answers 1/4
    which action, and on which arguments       close_ticket
    what exactly the approver is shown         -- UNANSWERED: the approval binds to a pointer, and the referent moves before it executes
    who may answer, and who may not            -- UNANSWERED: the party that proposed the action is also the party that blessed it
    what happens when the answer is no         -- UNANSWERED: a rejection is indistinguishable from a timeout, so the run retries until it gets a yes

'the ticket-shaped one': answers 3/4
    which action, and on which arguments       close_ticket, any arguments
    what exactly the approver is shown         the ticket id
    who may answer, and who may not            anyone on the support rota
    what happens when the answer is no         -- UNANSWERED: a rejection is indistinguishable from a timeout, so the run retries until it gets a yes

'the specification': answers 4/4
    which action, and on which arguments       close_ticket, pinned to the exact resolution text and ticket id
    what exactly the approver is shown         the drafted reply, the ticket, and the diff of what will change
    who may answer, and who may not            a rota member who is not the run's requester
    what happens when the answer is no         recorded as rejected with a reason; the run ends, it does not retry
```

The middle specification is the interesting one, and it is the one most real systems ship.

It scores 3 out of 4, and every one of its three answers is *present but weak*. "close_ticket, any
arguments" answers the question with the tool name, which [1.2](1.2-a-permission-is-not-a-gate.md)
just showed is a permission. "the ticket id" answers the evidence question with a pointer, which is
[4.1](../04-what-the-approver-sees/4.1-approving-bytes-not-pointers.md)'s failure exactly. "anyone
on the support rota" answers *who* without answering *who may not*, which is how the proposer ends
up in the pool.

So 3 out of 4 is not 75% of a gate. It is a gate with three thin answers and one missing one, and
the checker deliberately does not grade quality — it only reports absence. Grading the answers is
what sections 3 to 5 do, one question at a time.

## When it breaks

The characteristic failure is not writing a bad specification. It is writing a good one and then
discovering that the fourth question was answered by the framework, in a direction nobody chose.

Here is the shape. Nobody writes "on rejection, retry". What they write is a graph where the
approval node returns a value, and the calling node treats a falsy value the way it treats every
other falsy value — as a step that did not succeed, and therefore as a step to try again. The
retry logic was written on a different day, for a different reason, by somebody thinking about
timeouts. It is correct code. It converts a rejection into a delay.

The tell is in the log, and it is worth knowing the shape of:

```text
02:31  approval requested  close_ticket ticket:4623  -> rota-1
02:34  approval rejected   close_ticket ticket:4623  reason: linked bug still open
02:34  approval requested  close_ticket ticket:4623  -> rota-2
02:41  approval rejected   close_ticket ticket:4623  reason: customer has not replied
02:41  approval requested  close_ticket ticket:4623  -> rota-3
02:42  approval granted    close_ticket ticket:4623
```

Every line of that is true and the system behaved exactly as written. There is no error anywhere in
it. [6.3](../06-gates-that-are-decoration/6.3-ask-until-somebody-says-yes.md) runs this and counts
what it costs.

The second break is subtler and it is a *documentation* failure rather than a code one: the
specification answers all four, and then a new tool ships and nobody re-asks question one for it.
The specification is still complete for the actions it was written against, and incomplete for the
system. That is [3.4](../03-the-policy-table/3.4-the-tuesday-a-tool-arrives.md).

## In production

**What a professional writes instead.** They write the four answers as a **table with the action as
the key**, not as prose, because prose lets you answer three questions for one action and lose track
of which. When the specification is a table, a missing answer is a blank cell and a blank cell is
visible from across the room. That is the format section 3 produces.

**What degrades at scale.** Questions one and three degrade with the size of the codebase; two and
four degrade with the age of it. As the system grows, new effects appear and question one goes
stale — which is the failure Day 40 already met with MCP servers, where
[a tool appeared on a Tuesday](../../../day-40-filtering-and-allowlists/parts/03-when-the-server-changes/3.1-the-tuesday-a-tool-appeared.md).
As the system ages, the payload shape drifts and the thing the approver is shown stops matching the
thing that executes, without anybody editing the gate.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic.** Question four is invisible until the day
somebody rejects something. In testing, approvals are approved — that is what makes a test pass —
so the rejection path is frequently the least-exercised code in the system, and the first real
rejection is when you learn what it does. Write a test that rejects. It is the single highest-value
test in this whole subject and almost nobody has one.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"The design says customer-visible actions require
approval. Three questions I cannot answer from this document: what exactly does the approver see,
who is not allowed to be the approver, and what happens on a no. If the answer to the last one is
'the node returns false', please trace what the caller does with false, because I think it retries."*

**The interview question:** *"Walk me through designing an approval gate."* An honest answer:
*"Four questions, and I write the answers as a table keyed by action rather than as a paragraph.
Which effect — not which tool name, because two code paths can produce the same effect. What the
approver is shown, stored by value rather than as a pointer, so the thing approved is provably the
thing executed. Who may answer and, more importantly, who may not — the party that proposed it is
excluded. And what a no does, which is the one people skip: if a rejection only ends one attempt,
the system will re-file until somebody tired says yes, and every line of that log will look
legitimate. The test I always write is the rejection test, because approvals get approved in
testing and the reject path ships unexercised."*

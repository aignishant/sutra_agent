# 7.1 — Six things that must be true

## One-line answer

Phase 6 is green only when six independent conditions hold at once, the ten-rule audit satisfies part of
exactly one of them, and the other five are answered by a person reading and writing things down.

## The story

You have the train ticket on your phone and you are still not certain you are going to travel.

Four separate facts have to be true. The ticket has to be confirmed, which is not the same as booked — a
waitlisted ticket looks almost identical on the screen. The identity card in your bag has to be the one whose
number is on the ticket, because a different card with the same person on it is not accepted. The train has
to leave from the station you are standing in, and this city has three. And the coach has to be where the
display says, which is the one that goes wrong, because coach positions get reversed and the platform
announcement is the only thing that knows.

None of the four tells you anything about the other three. A confirmed ticket says nothing about which
station. The right station says nothing about the coach. So you check them one at a time, and you check the
coach last, on the platform, because it is the only one whose answer can change while you are standing there.

Nobody feels that any single one of the four is *the* condition. It is a list, and the list has to be walked.

## The idea in plain language

The plan's §15 says a phase is green only when six things are true. Six separate claims, each of which can be
false while the other five are true.

| # | The condition | The question it is actually asking |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | every day in Phase 6 has its `PROGRESS.md` row, gates green | did each day actually finish, and was that recorded honestly? |
| 2 | `./m trace` shows no open IDs from this phase or earlier | is anything the plan promised still unbuilt? |
| 3 | `./m check` passes on the whole repository | do the automated checks pass, right now, in front of you? |
| 4 | every day in the phase has a `parts/` directory | is every day actually *written*, not just committed? |
| 5 | the freshness re-checks pass | has the world outside the repository moved? |
| 6 | every deviation is recorded — an ADR, or a `CHANGELOG_PLAN.md` row | did anything change that the plan does not know about? |

Day 31's
[6.1 — Six things that must be true](../../../day-31-the-quality-gate/parts/06-the-phase-gate/6.1-six-things-that-must-be-true.md)
walked this list for Phase 4 and made the proportional argument: `./m check` is **one** of six, and it is the
only one a program can decide.

Today that proportion gets smaller, not larger, and it is worth being blunt about it. The ten-rule audit is
one stage inside condition 3, which is one condition of six. **This day's build is roughly one eighteenth of
the phase gate**, and the other seventeen eighteenths are reading, checking and writing down. The pull in the
other direction is strong precisely because the audit is the part that produces a satisfying line of output.

Phase 6 is Days 39 to 45: the database day, the allowlist, capabilities, the agent served over MCP, the
deploy-shaped server, client hardening, and today. Phase 7 — memory and retrieval — starts tomorrow and
builds directly on the servers this phase produced, which is what makes the gate load-bearing rather than
ceremonial. A phase declared green while MCP-21 is open hands Day 47 a durable-session day sitting on top of
a server that was never made stateless.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because the specific thing a gate protects against is a **backlog of quiet lies**, and this repository has a
documented history of exactly that.

Read `docs/PROGRESS.md` and the notes under it. Days 23 to 25 were committed together instead of one commit
per day, and their rows were reconstructed afterwards. Days 16 to 22 carry a ✅ that was optimistic, because
`./m check` had been red since Day 15 on a lint error nobody had run into. Days 29 to 41 carry ⚠️ in the gate
column and mean it. None of that is hidden, and that is the point — the ledger records what happened rather
than what should have happened, and the gate is where the accumulated ⚠️ either becomes a ✅ or becomes a
finding with a day number.

There is also a concrete reason today rather than tomorrow. Condition 2 is about **open IDs from this phase
or earlier**, and Phase 5 has several. Reading the committed `docs/TRACEABILITY.md` on 2026-09-05 shows
MCP-26 and MCP-32 (Day 32), MCP-27, MCP-28, MCP-30 and MCP-31 (Days 36 to 38) still marked open, alongside
this phase's own. That file is generated and it is stale — it is regenerated by `./m trace`, and the days
that close those IDs have been written since it was last run. **Reading a stale generated ledger and
concluding anything is its own small version of audit theatre**, so the condition is not "read the file", it
is "regenerate it and then read it".

## The mechanism

Each condition, with the command that answers it and what a red answer means. Work through it in order and
**write each answer down** — the writing is what makes the difference between checking and assuming.

```bash
# 1 - every Phase 6 day has a row, and what its gate column says
grep -n "^| 39 \|^| 4[0-5] " docs/PROGRESS.md

# 2 - regenerate first, then read. Never read a generated ledger you have not regenerated.
./m trace
grep -n "open" docs/TRACEABILITY.md | head -20

# 3 - the gate itself, whole repository
./m check; echo "exit: $?"

# 4 - every day in the phase is actually written
for n in 39 40 41 42 43 44 45; do
  d=$(ls -d days/day-$n-* 2>/dev/null | head -1)
  printf "day %s: %s parts\n" "$n" "$(find "$d/parts" -name '*.md' 2>/dev/null | wc -l)"
done

# 5 - the freshness re-checks: 7.2

# 6 - deviations recorded
git log --oneline -- docs/adr docs/CHANGELOG_PLAN.md | head
```

**Line by line:**

- Condition 1's `grep` uses a character class rather than seven separate patterns, and the trailing space in
  `"^| 39 "` matters: without it the pattern also matches day 390, which does not exist yet and will not, and
  the habit is what stops the same mistake on a three-digit day number elsewhere.
- Condition 2 runs `./m trace` **before** reading. The plan's §16 divides the ledgers into generated and
  hand-written, and `TRACEABILITY.md` is generated — reading it without regenerating tells you what was true
  the last time somebody ran the command.
- `grep -n "open"` after it, because the regenerated table is long and the only rows that matter are the ones
  that are not closed. `head -20` because if there are more than twenty, the answer is already no.
- Condition 3 is `./m check` on the **whole repository**, not `./m depth 45`. A phase gate is not a check of
  the day you happen to be writing; it is a check of everything, which is the only way a regression in Day 34
  gets caught on Day 45.
- `; echo "exit: $?"` because `./m check` prints `OK all green` on success and the number is what you record.
- Condition 4's loop resolves each day folder by number with a glob, which is why it survives a folder being
  renamed to a better slug — the plan's §17.2 rule that the number is the identity and the slug is a label.
- `find ... | wc -l` counts part files rather than checking that `parts/` exists, because an empty `parts/`
  directory would satisfy an existence test and is not a written day.
- Condition 6 asks git rather than asking you to remember. A phase in which nothing was amended shows an
  empty result, and an empty result is an answer worth writing down.

Measured on 2026-09-05, conditions 1 and 4:

```text
| 39 | 2026-09-05 | MCP-15, ADK-25, ADK-78 | 20 (+1 paper) | 8443fb2 | ⚠️ |
| 40 | 2026-09-05 | MCP-16, MCP-17, SEC-03 | 20 (+1 paper) | 1cc670e | ⚠️ |
| 41 | 2026-09-05 | MCP-18, MCP-19, MCP-29 | 19 | 71ff778 | ⚠️ |

day 39: 20 parts
day 40: 20 parts
day 41: 19 parts
day 42: 15 parts
day 43: 13 parts
day 44: 6 parts
day 45: 16 parts
```

Read those two blocks together, because separately each is reassuring and together they are the finding.

**Condition 4 is close to satisfied**: every day in the phase has a populated `parts/` directory. **Condition
1 is not**: there are three rows where there should be seven. Days 42 to 45 are written and unrowed, which is
the precise failure Day 31's 6.1 named — *"a day can be fully written with no row, which is a day somebody
forgot to finish"* — and it is why the two conditions are separate rather than one.

The gate's own tooling agrees. `./m brief 45` refuses:

```text
**STOP.** The last PROGRESS row is day 41, so N must be 42, not 45. Fix the ledger or the day number
before writing (P2).
```

That is condition 1 failing, reported by a command whose entire job is to notice it. The disposition is
**filed**, to the days themselves: each of 42, 43, 44 and 45 pastes its own row from its hub's §11, and the
gate cannot go green until all four are in.

And the three ⚠️ marks in the gate column are their own filed finding. They mean what Day 31 recorded on
2026-09-04: `./m check` is red on a `ruff` import-order error in `tests/test_persona.py`, which is the
learner's own code that no generated day may edit, and which one command fixes.

## When it breaks

**Condition 3 passes and condition 2 was never run.** The most common failure, because `./m check` is the
satisfying one. Every automated check is green, several IDs from Phase 5 are still open, and Phase 7 opens on
top of them. The IDs surface three phases later as a feature nobody built, and by then the day that was
supposed to build it is far behind.

**The ⚠️ is read as a ✅ because the day was good.** Days 39 to 41 are thorough days with twenty parts each,
and it is genuinely tempting to read the gate column as pessimistic. It is not: it is recording that
`./m check` was red when the row was written, and the whole value of the column is that it says so instead of
rounding up.

**Condition 5 is skipped because nothing looked different.** No command fails, because there is no command —
condition 5 is a *look*, and a look that does not happen leaves no trace at all. That is [7.2](7.2-the-freshness-recheck.md),
and it is the condition most likely to be quietly dropped on a day when the other five went well.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead:** the release checklist lives in the pull request template or as a
required checklist on the release issue, with the automated conditions rendered as status checks and the
human ones as explicit boxes that a named person ticks. The reason to separate them visually is precisely
this part's argument — a list where the automated and the human items look identical trains people to treat
the human ones as automatic.

**What degrades at scale:** the human conditions get delegated and then dropped. In a small team one person
checks all six; in a larger one, condition 5 belongs to a platform team, condition 6 to whoever writes the
architecture records, and condition 1 to nobody in particular, because it is everyone's. The conditions that
survive are the ones with a named owner, which is the same argument
[4.2](../04-the-policy/4.2-a-rule-with-one-owner.md) made about rules in a build.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic** is the gate that is run after the decision. The
release is scheduled, the work is done, and the checklist is walked on the morning of — at which point a red
condition is not information, it is an obstacle, and the pressure is to explain rather than to stop. Gates
that work are run early and repeatedly, so that a red condition arrives while there is still room to act on
it.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Three of these seven days have ledger rows and all seven
have parts. That's not a phase gate passing, that's four days that were written and never finished. I'd
rather see the gate held open for a day than see four rows backfilled next month from commit history —
we've done that once already and the note in the ledger explaining it is longer than the rows."*

**The interview question:** *"What's a phase gate, and why bother on a project with one developer?"* The
honest spoken answer: *"It's a short list of independent conditions that all have to be true before the next
phase starts — every unit of work recorded, nothing the plan promised still unbuilt, the automated checks
green across the whole repository, the work actually written rather than just committed, the outside world
re-checked, and every deviation written down. The thing I'd emphasise is the proportion: the automated check
is one of six, and it's the only one a program can decide, so most of a gate is a person reading and writing
answers down. Solo is where that matters most, not least — there's no colleague to notice your drift, so the
checklist plays the colleague. And the one rule that makes it real is that you write the answers, including
the boring ones, because a blank is evidence of nothing and 'no change' written down is evidence somebody
looked."*

# 6.2 — 💥 The registry that was empty

## One-line answer

An audit whose inventory comes from a list rather than from reality reports ten green rules on a repository
that reaches three servers nobody reviewed — because every rule was correct about the one server in
`REGISTRY`, and no rule asks whether `REGISTRY` is the whole truth.

## The story

You get to the airport, put the trolley down, and count the bags against the list in your phone.

Big suitcase. Cabin bag. The blue holdall. Three, and there are three on the trolley. Everything checks
out, and you feel the small settling that comes from a list agreeing with the world.

The two bags by the front door at home are not on the list. One of them is the one with the gifts in it,
packed on the last evening by somebody else, after the list was written. Nobody added it, because the list
was already finished and adding to a finished list feels like an admission that the list was wrong.

You find out in another city, and the check you did at the airport will have been completely correct. Three
bags on the list, three bags on the trolley, no errors anywhere. The count was never wrong. It just
answered *"do I have the bags I wrote down?"*, and the question you thought you were asking was *"do I have
my bags?"*

Those two questions have the same answer right up until somebody packs something after the list was
written, and that is the only situation in which anybody needs to ask.

## The idea in plain language

Every rule in this audit takes its inventory from `REGISTRY` in `sutra/mcp/filtering.py`. `A08` checks the
policies in it. `A10` checks that every entry has a ledger row and every row has an entry. Both are
correct, both are useful, and **neither of them asks whether `REGISTRY` lists every server Sutra actually
reaches.**

That gap is not theoretical, because nothing prevents a toolset being constructed outside the policy
module. Day 40's
[4.1 — One door, and a check there is one](../../../day-40-filtering-and-allowlists/parts/04-the-policy-module/4.1-one-door-and-a-check-there-is-one.md)
is precisely about it: the policy module is one door, and the value of one door depends entirely on there
not being a second. A server reached by code that never consults `REGISTRY` is invisible to `A08`,
invisible to `A10`, and reachable by the agent.

So the audit produces the strongest possible green — ten rules, zero findings — on a system where three
quarters of the servers were never audited at all.

This is the same family of failure as [6.1](6.1-green-because-the-path-did-not-exist.md), and the
difference is worth being precise about, because they are fixed differently:

| | 6.1 | 6.2 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What went wrong | a check could not find its target | every check found its target |
| What the audit examined | nothing | everything on the list |
| Why the green is false | the rules did not run | the **list** was incomplete |
| The fix | a missing target is a finding | derive the list from reality, not from a declaration |

`6.1` is a bug in the checks. `6.2` is a bug in the **scope**, and no amount of improving individual rules
touches it. An audit is never better than its inventory.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because by the end of Phase 6 the number of servers Sutra can reach is larger than the number anybody holds
in their head, and the ways to reach one have multiplied.

`sutra_mcp` is the obvious one. Day 39 adds a database lane and a first-party server evaluated against
hand-written tools. Addendum 02 puts a free web-search MCP server in place of paid grounding. Day 42
exposes the desk agent outward, which means some other host holds a policy about *Sutra*. Phase 5 also left
props behind — the deliberately broken server from Day 38, and the fake server that
`tests/test_mcp_failures.py` drives — and a prop's job description is being wrong in an instructive way,
which makes *"is it still contained?"* a live question rather than a formality.

The version of this day written for the v1 run named the same failure from the other direction. Its
inventory table had a row for a rogue lab prop marked **deliberately not in the registry**, and a checklist
row beside it asking whether the prop was still unlisted, still labelled, and still enforced by a test.
That is the shape of a good answer: the inventory is not the list of things you approved. It is the list of
things that exist, annotated with which ones you approved and which you deliberately did not.

## The mechanism

A fixture, built to be maximally healthy. One server in `REGISTRY` with a real allowlist, a matching row in
the ledger, a client that opens a connection per call with a deadline, a server module whose tool is
documented, annotated and raises, and an `m` whose `check)` target runs all three `tools.` stages.
Everything the ten rules ask for.

And one more file:

```python
# sutra/desk/toolsets.py - in the fixture, wired straight past the policy module
"""Wired straight to three servers. Never went near the registry."""

VENDOR_STATUS = "http://localhost:9001/mcp"
WEB_SEARCH = "http://localhost:9002/mcp"
TOOLBOX = "http://localhost:9003/mcp"
```

**Line by line:**

- Three endpoints, held as constants, in a module under `sutra/desk/` rather than `sutra/mcp/`. This is how
  it actually gets written: somebody needed a second server, the policy module felt like ceremony for a
  one-off, and the URL went where it was needed.
- **`A01` does not fire on this**, and that is worth understanding rather than assuming. `A01` looks for
  *imports* of a driver or an HTTP library. A module holding URL strings imports nothing forbidden, and the
  code that uses them lives elsewhere. The boundary check catches a second pipe; it does not catch a second
  address book.
- **`A08` does not fire either.** `REGISTRY` is populated and its one policy is correct. The rule is about
  the contents of the registry, and this file is not in the registry, which is the entire problem stated as
  a tautology.
- Nothing here is syntactically unusual. No shortcut, no swallow, no held connection, no secret. Every rule
  reads this file and correctly finds nothing to say.

Run the full audit against that fixture:

```bash
python -c "
from pathlib import Path
import audit_preview as a
findings = a.run_all(Path('.'))
for c in a.CHECKS:
    rows = [f for f in findings if f.check == c.id]
    print(('ok ' if not rows else 'RED'), c.id, c.name.ljust(20), c.rule)
print(f'{len(findings)} finding(s), over {len(a.CHECKS)} checks')
"
grep -rn "localhost:900" sutra/ | wc -l
grep -c "ServerPolicy(" sutra/mcp/filtering.py
```

**Line by line:**

- `a.run_all(Path('.'))` is called with the fixture root rather than through `main`, for the reason
  [1.1](../01-what-an-audit-is/1.1-the-list-is-written-before-you-look.md) gave: a check that takes its root
  as an argument can be pointed at a fixture, and a check that computes its own cannot.
- The loop reproduces `main`'s output format without the findings detail, because on this run there are no
  findings to show — which is the point being demonstrated.
- The two `grep` lines are the comparison the audit does not make. The first counts server endpoints named
  anywhere under `sutra/`; the second counts policies in the registry. **Two numbers that should match and
  do not** is the whole finding, and it takes two lines of shell that no rule in the module runs.
- **Zero model calls, no network.** Three servers are named and none of them is contacted.

**Ten green rules, zero findings, and three servers against one policy.** Nothing above is a false green in
the sense of [6.1](6.1-green-because-the-path-did-not-exist.md) — every rule ran, every target existed, every
judgement was right. The audit is telling the exact truth about the wrong set of servers.

The fix is not another rule of the same kind. It is a rule that **derives the inventory** rather than reading
it: scan for what looks like a server address anywhere under `sutra/`, compare with `REGISTRY`, and report
the difference. That is an eleventh check, it is genuinely harder to write well than the other ten — an
address can be a constant, an environment variable or a function argument — and it is the hub's largest
`TODO(me)` for exactly that reason.

## When it breaks

**The derived inventory is written and it over-reports.** A URL in a docstring, a test fixture, a commented
example — all get counted, and the eleventh check reports six unregistered servers where there are three.
This is the standard way a scope check dies: it is annoying before it is useful, somebody adds exclusions,
and the exclusions grow until the check is scanning nothing. The countermeasure is to scan a narrow
definition well — a URL passed to a known connector, rather than any string that looks like a URL — and
accept the misses.

**The inventory is complete and the props are miscounted.** Day 38's broken server and the fake in
`tests/test_mcp_failures.py` are servers, they are supposed to exist, and they are supposed **not** to be in
`REGISTRY`. A derived inventory reports them, correctly, forever. The answer is not to exclude the path; it
is to give a prop a row that says *deliberately unlisted*, so it is inventoried and its containment is a
checked property rather than an omission.

**Everything matches and the agent still reaches something unlisted.** The endpoint is in an environment
variable, read at runtime, and no string in the repository contains it. Nothing static can see this. The only
things that can are a test that asserts the set of connections opened during a run, and — in a real
deployment — egress control at the network layer, which is the version of this rule that cannot be argued
with because it is not enforced by the process being audited.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead:** the inventory is not derived from source at all, it is observed.
Service meshes and egress proxies record every outbound connection a workload makes, and the audit compares
that observed set with the declared one. That inverts the whole problem — instead of hoping the code names
every dependency, you watch what it actually talks to — and it is the only approach that survives dynamic
configuration. The static version stays useful as the fast, pre-deployment half.

**What degrades at scale:** the declared and the real drift apart continuously rather than occasionally. Each
individual addition is small and reasonable, nobody sets out to bypass the registry, and after a year the
registry describes the servers somebody thought about and the network describes the servers in use. Teams
that manage this well make the registry **load-bearing at runtime** — the connection cannot be opened unless
the policy lookup succeeds, which is Day 40's raising lookup — so bypassing it is a bigger act than
forgetting.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic** is the dependency that appears under load. A
fallback endpoint, a secondary region, a retry that goes to a different host. None of it is exercised in
development, all of it is in the code, and it means the *effective* inventory is larger during exactly the
incident where somebody needs to know what the system talks to. This is why observed inventories are
collected continuously rather than sampled.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"The audit is green and there are three MCP endpoints in
this diff that aren't in the registry. The rules are all fine — they're just answering a question about the
registry, and I'm asking a question about the system. Put these three in the policy module, or tell me in
writing why they're deliberately outside it, and either way I want the count of endpoints and the count of
policies to be the same number."*

**The interview question:** *"How do you know your inventory of dependencies is complete?"* The honest spoken
answer: *"I mostly don't, and that's the part I try to be explicit about. Any audit that reads a declared
list is answering 'is what I wrote down consistent?' rather than 'is what I wrote down everything?', and
those look identical until somebody adds a dependency without updating the list. So I do two things. I derive
the inventory from the code as well as reading the declaration, and compare the two — even a crude scan
catches the common case. And I make the declaration load-bearing at runtime, so a connection that isn't in
the registry fails rather than working, because a list you can bypass will be bypassed. Beyond that, the real
answer is observed rather than declared: egress logs tell you what the thing actually talked to, and that's
the only inventory that includes the fallback endpoint nobody exercises until an incident."*

# 5.1 — The stocktake

## One-line answer

An injection asks for one of five things — **exfiltrate, escalate, mislead, destroy, spend** — and
they are worth separating because each one needs a different leg of the trifecta and each one is
noticed by a different person.

## The story

A warehouse manager does a stocktake at the end of the year and finds the numbers do not match. She
expects the shortfall to be stock, because stock is what a warehouse worries about.

The stock is short, a little. But the count also turns up other things she was not counting. A pallet
of the wrong goods has been sitting in bay four for eight months, blocking a bay worth more than the
goods on it. Someone has been running the forklift charger all night for a year. Two of the shelving
bays have been labelled wrongly since the refit, so pickers walk an extra aisle on every order. And a
box of returns has been signed off as inspected by somebody who left in March.

Every one of those costs money. Not one of them is theft, and if she had gone in counting only theft
she would have found the small shortfall and stopped.

Losses come in kinds, and a count that knows only one kind finds only one kind.

## The idea in plain language

"Prompt injection" is one name for one mechanism, and the mechanism is put to at least five quite
different uses. They are worth separating because the defences differ, the *detection* differs, and —
most usefully — the person who eventually notices differs.

| What the injection asks for | What it needs | Who notices, and when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Exfiltrate** — send private data out | all three legs | usually nobody, until an outside report |
| **Escalate** — reach a capability the requester lacks | private + a privileged tool | an audit, much later |
| **Mislead** — make the answer wrong but plausible | untrusted only | the customer, if ever |
| **Destroy** — delete, overwrite, corrupt | a writing tool | operations, quickly |
| **Spend** — burn quota, time, money | untrusted only | the on-call engineer, same day |

Read the third column downwards, because it is the column nobody builds for. Two of the five —
mislead and spend — need **only the untrusted leg**. They work on an agent with no private data and
no outward channel at all, which means part
[4.4](../04-three-legs/4.4-closing-the-tap-nearest-the-outlet.md)'s recommended cut does nothing
against them.

That is not a reason to skip the cut. It is a reason to be precise about what the cut buys:
**removing the outward leg prevents the unrecoverable harms and leaves the recoverable ones
standing.** Exfiltration cannot be undone — data that left is gone. A wrong answer can be corrected,
a quota resets tomorrow, a deleted row restores from backup. That distinction is what makes the
outward leg worth more attention than the others, and it is also why "we closed the trifecta" is not
the same sentence as "we are fine".

Two of the five deserve a note now, because they are the ones this curriculum has already met from
another direction:

- **Destroy** overlaps almost entirely with Day 63's approval-gate design. An agent that cannot write
  without a human saying yes has already mitigated it, and Day 64 built the gate.
- **Spend** is the one this project feels most sharply, because Sutra's budget is denominated in
  requests per day rather than in money. Part
  [5.2](5.2-the-meter-somebody-else-is-feeding.md) prices it.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because the threat model has to enumerate harms before it can decide anything, and a threat model
that enumerates only exfiltration produces a plan that is wrong in a specific direction: it spends
everything on the outward leg and leaves the desk trivially disruptable.

It also fixes the shape of part [7.3](../07-writing-it-down/7.3-accept-mitigate-refuse.md)'s decision
table. Each of these five gets a row, and three of them get `mitigate` rather than `refuse` precisely
because they survive the cut that closes the trifecta.

## The mechanism

The five are declared in `model.py` as decisions rather than as a taxonomy for its own sake — a
harm with no decision attached is a worry, not a threat model:

```python
DECISIONS: tuple[tuple[str, str, str], ...] = (
    (
        "accept",
        "the model cannot separate instructions from data",
        "no defence removes this; every mitigation below assumes it",
    ),
    (
        "accept",
        "any archive row may be hostile",
        "a support desk that refuses stranger text is not a support desk",
    ),
    (
        "mitigate",
        "hostile text reaching the prompt",
        "provenance marking and guardrails - Day 67",
    ),
    (
        "mitigate",
        "a steered agent reaching a dangerous tool",
        "least privilege and an allowlist - Day 68",
    ),
    (
        "mitigate",
        "private data crossing a boundary",
        "data boundaries and synthetic data only - Day 69",
    ),
    (
        "mitigate",
        "quota drain through a forced loop",
        "the caps from Day 59, tightened; the router from Day 70",
    ),
    (
        "refuse",
        "an outbound fetch tool in the answering path",
        "the trifecta closes without it; nothing we need requires it",
    ),
    (
        "refuse",
        "real customer data in any environment this curriculum runs",
        "Addendum 02 and Day 69: synthetic only, and the free tier is the reason",
    ),
)
```

**Line by line:**

- Each row is `(verdict, what, why)`. The verdict is one of three words and the vocabulary is
  deliberately tiny: anything that is not accepted or refused is being mitigated by somebody, and
  that somebody has to be named.
- The two `accept` rows come first because everything below assumes them. An accepted risk that is
  not written down gets re-litigated every quarter by whoever joined most recently.
- Every `mitigate` row names **a day**. That is not decoration — `gate.py`'s
  `check_mitigations_have_owners()` fails the build if a mitigation has no owner, which is how a
  threat model avoids becoming a list of things somebody ought to do.
- The `refuse` rows are the only two places in this day where a capability is given up. Both are
  cheap here and would not be cheap in a product that needed the capability, which is the honest
  reason to make the decision now rather than after somebody has built on it.

Generate the artefact and read the decisions section:

```bash
cd days/day-66-injection-threat-model/lab
uv run python model.py --write
```

**Line by line:**

- `render()` builds the markdown from the same declarations `legs.py` and `channels.py` use, so the
  document cannot drift from the analysis.
- `--write` puts it at `lab/threat-model.md` with a "do not hand-edit" line at the top, because a
  generated document that somebody edits by hand is worse than no document.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
## Decisions

| decision | about | because |
| --- | --- | --- |
| accept | the model cannot separate instructions from data | no defence removes this; every mitigation below assumes it |
| accept | any archive row may be hostile | a support desk that refuses stranger text is not a support desk |

| mitigate | quota drain through a forced loop | the caps from Day 59, tightened; the router from Day 70 |
| refuse | an outbound fetch tool in the answering path | the trifecta closes without it; nothing we need requires it |
| refuse | real customer data in any environment this curriculum runs | Addendum 02 and Day 69: synthetic only, and the free tier is the reason |

## The attacker

Not a genius and not funded. Someone who can open a support ticket, has read one article
about prompt injection, and is willing to wait a week. Every entry point above is
reachable with a free account and a text box.
```

The attacker paragraph is deliberately unglamorous. A threat model written against a nation-state
produces a plan nobody can afford and therefore nobody executes; a threat model written against
somebody with a free account and a week produces a plan that gets done. Every measurement in this day
was produced by that attacker: a ticket, a wiki edit, and patience.

## When it breaks

The taxonomy breaks when a harm has no row, and the check for that is mechanical:

```bash
uv run python gate.py; echo "exit: $?"
```

**Line by line:**

- `check_mitigations_have_owners()` scans the `mitigate` rows for the string `Day` and fails on any
  that lacks it. It is a crude check and it catches the real failure, which is a mitigation deferred
  to nobody.
- The two failures shown are the trifecta finding and the unwritten module, both expected.

```text
PASS  threat model artefact exists
PASS  every entry point is covered
PASS  every mitigation names its day
FAIL  no path holds all three legs
      the trifecta closes at stage 'review' - see legs.py
FAIL  sutra/threat_model.py written
      sutra\threat_model.py not written yet (build brief, TODO(me))

findings: 2
exit: 1
```

Now break it on purpose, which is the only way to know the check is real:

```bash
uv run python -c "
import gate, model
model.DECISIONS = tuple(
    (v, w, 'we will sort it out later') if v == 'mitigate' else (v, w, y)
    for v, w, y in model.DECISIONS
)
print(gate.check_mitigations_have_owners())
"
```

**Line by line:**

- Replaces every `mitigate` row's reason with a sentence that names no day — which is exactly what a
  threat model degrades into when it is edited under time pressure.
- Calls the check directly and prints what it returns. An empty string means pass.

```text
mitigations with no day named: ['hostile text reaching the prompt', 'a steered agent reaching a dangerous tool', 'private data crossing a boundary', 'quota drain through a forced loop']
```

Four mitigations, no owners, caught. That is a test that can go red (Principle 11) for the reason
threat models actually rot.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead.** They tie each row to a **ticket**, not just a day, and they
review the accepted rows on a schedule. The two `accept` rows here are the dangerous ones long-term,
not because they are wrong but because they are only right *given* the mitigations below them: "any
archive row may be hostile" is acceptable while the outward leg is cut, and becomes unacceptable the
moment somebody adds an outbound tool. An accepted risk should record the conditions it was accepted
under.

**What degrades at scale.** The `mislead` harm, which is the one with no owner in this table and the
one hardest to give one. Exfiltration has a control, spending has a cap, destruction has an approval
gate — misleading has none, because a plausible wrong answer is indistinguishable from a plausible
right one at the point of production. At small volume a human reads the drafts. At large volume
nobody does, and the harm becomes invisible rather than absent.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic.** The five categories blur. A real incident is
usually a chain: a mislead makes an agent classify a ticket wrongly, which routes it to a queue with a
broader tool set, which is where the escalate happens, which enables the exfiltrate. The postmortem
argues about which category it was and the answer is all of them, in order, which is why the table's
value is in planning rather than in classification after the fact.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Good table, one gap: `mislead` isn't in it. We have
rows for the harms with obvious controls and nothing for the one where the agent just confidently says
something false because a ticket told it to. I don't think we have a control either, and that's fine —
but then it belongs in `accept` with an explicit note that we're relying on the human reviewing the
draft, so that when someone proposes auto-sending replies they have to come back to this row first."*

**The interview question:** *"What can an attacker actually achieve with prompt injection?"* An honest
answer: *"Five things, and they need different capabilities. Exfiltrating data needs all three legs
of the trifecta. Escalating needs a privileged tool. Destroying needs a write. But misleading and
spending need only that untrusted text reaches the model — no private data, no outward channel — so
they survive the fix that closes the trifecta. I'd separate them by recoverability rather than
severity: exfiltration is the one you can't undo, so it earns the structural control, and the others
get caps, gates and review. The one I'd flag as unowned is misleading, because a plausible wrong
answer looks exactly like a plausible right one at the moment it's produced."*

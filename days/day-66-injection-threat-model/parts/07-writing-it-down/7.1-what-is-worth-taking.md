# 7.1 — What is worth taking

## One-line answer

A threat model starts by naming, in plain words, **what you are protecting** and **where a stranger's
words get in** — five of each here — and the row that changes people's minds is the second asset:
the archive as a whole, because **bulk access is worth more than any single one of its rows**.

## The story

A woman who runs a corner shop is filling in an insurance form. The form wants a list of contents,
so she walks round with a pen and writes down what she can see: the chiller cabinets, the coffee
machine, the till, the shelving, the stock, the van outside.

Her accountant reads it and asks what she would do if the shop burned down on a Sunday night.

She says she would open again in a fortnight in the unit next door. She would buy new chillers. The
stock is replaceable. What she would not have is the ledger behind the counter — the one with the
account customers who run a tab, the standing orders for the two cafés she supplies, and the
telephone numbers of the three suppliers who give her credit.

It is a notebook worth about ninety pence. It is the only thing on the premises she could not rebuild
from anywhere else, and it is not on the form, because she wrote the form by looking at the room.

## The idea in plain language

Every section of this day so far has been about the attack. This section is about the **document**,
and a document has to start somewhere: with what is worth protecting and where a stranger's words get
in.

Both lists have a failure mode and it is the same one — writing them in language too abstract to
decide anything with. An asset list that says *"the system"* or *"customer data"* has not started,
because you cannot ask a useful question of it. You cannot ask whether removing a tool protects "the
system". You can ask whether it protects *the archive as a whole*.

So the rule for both lists is: **name the thing, and say who can touch it, in words a person who does
not work here would understand.**

The one idea in this part is the one the shopkeeper's accountant supplied. Look at the second asset
in the list below:

> **the archive as a whole** — 58 documents; bulk access is worth more than any single one

A single ticket body is worth almost nothing. Fifty-eight of them, together, are a customer list.
That is not a difference of degree; it is a different asset, with a different value, and it does not
appear anywhere in a list built by naming individual things. **Aggregation changes what data is
worth**, which is why access control granted per record — *this agent may read a ticket* — can be
correct on every individual decision and wrong in total. An agent that may read one ticket, fifty-
eight times, has read the customer list.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because every decision this day makes has to be *about* something, and the something has to be
written down before the decision, not after. Part
[5.1](../05-what-it-asks-for/5.1-the-stocktake.md) enumerated the five harms an injection asks for;
this part enumerates what those harms are aimed at, and part
[7.3](7.3-accept-mitigate-refuse.md) puts a verdict against each pairing.

There is a practical reason too, and it is the one that makes this part worth its own document rather
than a paragraph in the hub. The threat model is generated (part
[7.2](7.2-the-document-that-writes-itself.md)) and it is checked by `gate.py`, and one of the checks
is that **every entry point declared in the lab appears in the artefact**. That check is only worth
having if the entry-point list is honest — a list that quietly omits the awkward one turns a green
gate into a false assurance. So the list is where the honesty has to go in.

## The mechanism

Five assets, each with the sentence that says why it is on the list:

```python
# What we are protecting. Named plainly, because a threat model whose asset list says "the system"
# has not started.
ASSETS: tuple[tuple[str, str], ...] = (
    ("customer text", "every ticket body in the archive Days 49 and 50 indexed"),
    ("the archive as a whole", "58 documents; bulk access is worth more than any single one"),
    ("desk memory", "what Day 48 chose to keep, retrievable by any later question"),
    ("provider quota", "the free-tier requests the desk runs on (Addendum 02)"),
    ("the desk's reputation", "an answer sent to a customer carries the company's name"),
)
```

**Line by line:**

- Each row is `(name, what it is)`. The second element is not decoration: it is the sentence that
  lets somebody who was not in the room decide whether a proposed change affects this asset.
- `customer text` and `the archive as a whole` are **two rows on purpose**, and a reviewer will ask
  why. Because the controls differ: one row is protected by not disclosing a ticket, the other by not
  permitting bulk retrieval, and a threat model that has only the first will approve a change that
  loses the second.
- `provider quota` is an asset, which is unusual and is a consequence of Addendum 02. Where the
  budget is denominated in requests per day, the requests **are** the resource, and part
  [5.2](../05-what-it-asks-for/5.2-the-meter-somebody-else-is-feeding.md) measured what one sentence
  in one ticket costs it.
- `the desk's reputation` is the one people leave off because it is not data. It earns its row
  because it is what the `mislead` harm attacks, and because it is the asset with no technical
  control at all — which is a thing worth discovering while writing the list rather than during the
  incident.

Five entry points, each with the answer to *who can write this?*:

```python
# Where a stranger's bytes get in.
ENTRY_POINTS: tuple[tuple[str, str], ...] = (
    ("ticket body", "anyone who can open a ticket"),
    ("archive row", "anyone who has ever opened a ticket, retrieved later"),
    ("memory row", "the desk itself, writing down what a ticket said"),
    ("knowledge-base article", "an insider, or anyone who can edit the wiki"),
    ("tool result", "whatever the tool talked to"),
)
```

**Line by line:**

- The second column is deliberately a **person**, not a component. "Anyone who can open a ticket" is
  a sentence you can check against reality; "the intake API" is not.
- `archive row` and `ticket body` differ only in *when*: the same words, retrieved later, for a
  different question. That is the delay part
  [3.1](../03-the-slow-fuse/3.1-written-monday-read-thursday.md) is about, and separating the two
  rows is what stops a reviewer thinking the archive is a read-only store of things the desk wrote.
- `memory row` says the writer is **the desk itself**, which is the uncomfortable one: the system is
  an entry point into its own context, and part
  [6.3](../06-the-way-out/6.3-the-note-inside-the-back-cover.md) showed the delivery that follows.
- `knowledge-base article` is the insider door that part
  [2.3](../02-how-it-arrives/2.3-the-recipe-folder-in-the-staff-kitchen.md) measured as the most
  reliable route into the prompt. It is on the list even though it requires access, because "requires
  access" is not the same as "trusted".
- `tool result` is the row that is hardest to write and easiest to omit: its writer is *"whatever the
  tool talked to"*, which is a party the threat model cannot name. A list that leaves this out reads
  as complete and is not.

Render them:

```bash
cd days/day-66-injection-threat-model/lab
uv run python model.py
```

**Line by line:**

- `cd` first: the lab's scripts import their siblings by plain name.
- No `--write`, so this prints the document without touching `threat-model.md`. Reading before
  writing is the habit; part 7.2 is where the file gets written and why the file matters.

Measured on 2026-09-06 — the first two sections of the generated document:

Five and five, and the `58` in the second asset row is a real count of the archive Days 49 and 50
built, not a round number chosen to look plausible.

## When it breaks

Two ways, and they fail in opposite directions.

**The list is too abstract to decide anything.** Replace the second asset with *"customer data"* and
every row of the decision table in part [7.3](7.3-accept-mitigate-refuse.md) still parses and none of
them means anything. There is a quick test for this and it is worth applying to a real threat model:
take any proposed change — *add a tool that exports search results to CSV* — and ask which asset rows
it affects. If the answer is "customer data, I suppose", the list has not started. If the answer is
"the archive as a whole, directly, because export is bulk retrieval", it has.

**A door is missing.** The one that goes missing is `tool result`, and it goes missing for a reason
worth naming: its writer cannot be identified. Every other row ends in a person — a customer, a
colleague, the desk. That one ends in *"whatever the tool talked to"*, which is an unbounded set that
changes whenever somebody adds an integration. It is easier to leave out than to write, and leaving
it out makes the document read as finished.

`gate.py` catches the second failure and not the first, which is the honest limit of a mechanical
check on a document:

```bash
uv run python gate.py; echo "exit: $?"
```

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
PASS  threat model artefact exists
PASS  every entry point is covered
PASS  every mitigation names its day
FAIL  no path holds all three legs
      the trifecta closes at stage 'review' - see legs.py
FAIL  sutra/threat_model.py written
      sutra\threat_model.py not written yet (build brief, TODO(me))

findings: 2
exit: 1
```

`every entry point is covered` passes because `check_entry_points()` compares the declared list
against the text of the artefact. It will go red the moment somebody adds a door in `model.py` and
forgets to regenerate — which is the drift part 7.2 is about. What it cannot do is tell you a door
was never declared. **No check can find a list's missing row; only a person walking the system can.**
The two failures are the ones this day expects: the trifecta finding from section 4, and the
build-brief module that is yours to write.

## In production

**Who owns an asset list.** Somebody has to, by name, or it is nobody's. In practice it belongs with
whoever can say what the data is worth — which is rarely the team that wrote the service. The failure
this prevents is the one where engineers guess at value: a list written entirely by engineers
over-weights the things that are technically interesting and under-weights the ones that are
commercially fatal, and `the desk's reputation` is usually the row that goes missing.

**What degrades at scale, and in which direction.** Asset lists rot **by accretion**. Things get
added — a new store, a new export, a new integration — and nothing is ever removed, because removing
a row is a decision somebody could be blamed for and adding one is free. After two years the list is
long, everything on it is "high", and the prioritisation it was written to support has quietly
stopped working. The fix is not a better list; it is a review with a rule that every row must be
re-justified or dropped.

**What this becomes at a large organisation.** Data classification. The five plain rows above turn
into a scheme with tiers and handling rules, and the tiers are then attached to stores, fields and
pipelines so that controls can be derived rather than argued. That is the right destination and it
has a well-known failure: a classification scheme that nobody can apply without a meeting gets applied
by defaulting everything to the middle tier. The plain version above is worth keeping alongside it,
because it is the one a new engineer can read in a minute.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Good list. Two things. `the archive as a whole`
needs to say what bulk means in a number we can check — is it fifty rows, is it any query returning
more than k — because right now it's a principle and I'd like it to be a limit. And `tool result` has
no owner in the sense that matters: the writer is whatever the tool talked to, so every new
integration adds a party to this row without touching the document. Can we make adding a tool require
a line here?"*

**The interview question:** *"How do you start a threat model?"* An honest answer: *"With two lists, in
plain language: what we're protecting and where somebody else's words get in. I keep them concrete
enough to decide with — 'the archive as a whole, fifty-eight documents' rather than 'customer data' —
because the abstract version can't answer whether a proposed change matters. The row I always argue
for is the aggregate one: individual records are usually cheap and the whole set is the actual asset,
so per-record access control can be right every time and still lose the thing you cared about. And on
the entry points, the one that gets left off is the tool result, because you can't name who wrote it —
which is exactly why it belongs on the list."*

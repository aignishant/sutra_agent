# 7.3 — Accept, mitigate, refuse

## One-line answer

Every risk gets exactly one of three verdicts, and the verdict is the only part of a threat model
that does any work: an **accept** row must record the conditions it was accepted under, a
**mitigate** row must name an owner or it is a wish, and a **refuse** row gives up a capability while
giving it up is still cheap.

## The story

A car goes in for its annual test. It comes back with a sheet of paper divided into three parts, and
the owner reads it standing at the counter.

The top part is what was **done**: two tyres, a wiper blade, an oil change. Money has been spent and
the problems are gone.

The bottom part is what the garage would **not** let him drive away with. A brake pipe was corroded
past the point where anybody was prepared to sign it off. There was no negotiation about that one.

The middle part is the interesting one, and it is the longest: *advisories*. Slight play in a track
rod end. A tyre near the limit but legal. Corrosion starting on a sill, not structural yet. Every one
of those is a thing somebody looked at, decided not to fix today, and wrote down.

The middle part is only worth the paper it is on if two things are true of each line: somebody knows
who is going to look at it again, and somebody knows when. A list of advisories with no name and no
date is not a decision. It is a way of feeling that a decision has been made.

## The idea in plain language

By this point the day has produced a lot of findings. Sections 1 to 3 said how injection arrives,
section 4 found the trifecta closing at `review`, section 5 listed five harms, section 6 counted five
ways out. None of that is a threat model yet. It is a survey.

A threat model is the survey **plus a verdict on every line**, drawn from a vocabulary of exactly
three words:

| Verdict | What it means | What it obliges you to write down |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **accept** | We are not going to do anything about this | the conditions under which accepting is reasonable |
| **mitigate** | We are reducing it, and it will still be there | who is doing it, and when |
| **refuse** | We are giving up the capability that creates it | what we lose, and why that is affordable |

The vocabulary is deliberately tiny, and the reason is the middle row. **Anything that is not
accepted or refused is being mitigated by somebody, and that somebody has to be named.** A finding
with no verdict at all is the worst outcome of a threat modelling exercise, because it produces the
feeling of having addressed something without any of the consequences of addressing it.

Part [5.1](../05-what-it-asks-for/5.1-the-stocktake.md) already printed the eight rows this day
produces. This part is about what makes a row belong in each column, and what each column then owes.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because four earlier parts deferred a decision to this one, and a deferred decision with an address
is only honest if the address exists.

  can largely **accept** rather than mitigate, and that "an accepted risk has to be written down as
  accepted, with the argument, or somebody will spend a week defending against it."
- Parts [4.3](../04-three-legs/4.3-the-line-that-passes-it-along.md) and

- Part [1.2](../01-one-stream/1.2-the-fix-the-database-got.md) predicted that phrase blocklists fail;

  directions. That result has to land somewhere in the table rather than in a general feeling that
  filtering is unwise.
- Part [5.1](../05-what-it-asks-for/5.1-the-stocktake.md) listed five harms, gave three of them
  `mitigate`, and left **mislead** deliberately unowned — its own review comment argues that it
  belongs in `accept` with an explicit note. This part takes that position.

## The mechanism

The four rows that are not `mitigate` are where the decisions actually are:

```bash
cd days/day-66-injection-threat-model/lab
uv run python -c "
import model
for verdict, what, why in model.DECISIONS:
    if verdict != 'mitigate':
        print(f'{verdict:<8} {what}')
"
```

**Line by line:**

- The filter is `verdict != 'mitigate'` because the mitigate rows are the ones that hand work to
  another day. What is left is everything this day settles on its own — two things nobody is going to
  fix, and two capabilities being given up.
- `f'{verdict:<8}'` left-pads to a fixed width so the two columns line up; the `why` is dropped here
  on purpose, because the point of this run is to see how short the list of real decisions is.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
accept   the model cannot separate instructions from data
accept   any archive row may be hostile
refuse   an outbound fetch tool in the answering path
refuse   real customer data in any environment this curriculum runs
```

Four lines. That is the whole of what this day decides; the other four rows are things it schedules.

**The two `accept` rows come first in the tuple, and the ordering is not cosmetic.** Everything below
them assumes them. *The model cannot separate instructions from data* is section 1's finding, and no
defence in this curriculum removes it — part
[1.2](../01-one-stream/1.2-the-fix-the-database-got.md) showed why the SQL fix cannot be copied
across, because there is no second input to put the values in. *Any archive row may be hostile* is
section 2's, and refusing stranger text is not an option available to a support desk.

What makes those acceptable is not that they are unfixable. It is the **condition**: they are
acceptable *while the outward leg is cut*. That is the sentence an accepted risk has to carry, and it
is the one almost always missing. "We accept that any archive row may be hostile" is a different
statement from "we accept that any archive row may be hostile, given that nothing in the answering
path can send data outward" — and the second one turns into a red light the moment somebody proposes
an outbound tool, which is exactly the moment you want it to.

Where direct injection lands, as part 2.1 promised: **accept**. The person doing it already has
whatever access the system gives them, so the harm is bounded by their own permissions, and defending
against a user steering their own session costs real usability for no reduction in blast radius. The
condition on that acceptance is that the session's permissions really are the user's — which is
Day 68's subject and is why the row is `accept` here rather than `accept, closed`.

Where the blocklist result lands: **not** as a row of its own. A blocklist is not a risk; it is a
proposed mitigation, and part 6.1 measured it at 10 of 12 hostile variants caught for 4 of 5 honest
customers refused. So it appears as an argument inside the `mitigate` row for *hostile text reaching
the prompt* — whose named owner is Day 67 — and the argument is that the mitigation must be layered
and structural rather than a phrase list. A threat model that carried a row saying "blocklists are
bad" would be recording an opinion instead of a decision.

Where **mislead** lands, and this is the row part 5.1 left open: **accept, with the condition
written**. There is no control for it. Exfiltration has a structural fix, spending has a cap,
destruction has an approval gate; a plausible wrong answer is indistinguishable from a plausible right
one at the moment it is produced. Writing it as `mitigate` would be dishonest, because there is
nothing to schedule and no day to name. So it is accepted, and the condition is stated: *accepted
while a human reads the draft before it is sent* — Day 62's human in the loop, built as Day 64's
approval gate. That phrasing does the work part 5.1's reviewer asked for. When somebody proposes
auto-sending replies, they have to come back to this row first, because the condition it was accepted
under is the thing they are removing.

The `mitigate` rows are the ones with an owner, and the ownership is enforced rather than requested:

```python
def check_mitigations_have_owners() -> str:
    """Every 'mitigate' row names the day that does it, so nothing is deferred to nowhere."""
    orphans = [
        what for verdict, what, why in model.DECISIONS if verdict == "mitigate" and "Day" not in why
    ]
    return f"mitigations with no day named: {orphans}" if orphans else ""
```

**Line by line:**

- The rule is one substring: the `why` column of a `mitigate` row must contain the word `Day`. It is
  crude, and being crude is the point — a check that tried to validate a plan would need judgement,
  and this one only asserts that a plan has an addressee.
- It returns **the list of orphaned rows**, not a boolean, so the failure message names the rows a
  person then has to make a decision about. A check that says `False` gives you nothing to do next.
- It scans `model.DECISIONS` directly rather than the generated file, so it is checking the
  declaration and not the paperwork. Part [7.2](7.2-the-document-that-writes-itself.md)'s
  `check_entry_points()` does the opposite, deliberately: one check guards the code, the other guards
  the document.
- Empty string means pass, which is the convention every check in `gate.py` uses. It reads oddly for
  one function and reads well when five of them are in a list.

## When it breaks

The realistic failure is not a wrong verdict. It is the `mitigate` row that stops naming anybody —
which happens, in practice, in exactly one way: somebody edits the document in a hurry, before a
deadline, and softens the reasons.

Do it on purpose:

```bash
uv run python -c "
import gate, model

model.DECISIONS = tuple(
    (v, w, 'we will sort it out later') if v == 'mitigate' else (v, w, y)
    for v, w, y in model.DECISIONS
)
print(gate.check_mitigations_have_owners())
"
```

**Line by line:**

- The comprehension rewrites every `mitigate` row's reason to a sentence that names nobody, and
  leaves the `accept` and `refuse` rows untouched — because those are not the rows that rot. An
  accepted risk with a bad reason is at least visibly a decision; a mitigation with a bad reason still
  reads like work in progress.
- *"We will sort it out later"* is not a straw man. It is what a mitigation reason becomes when the
  day it named slips and nobody wants to write down which day it is now.
- The check is called directly rather than through `gate.main()`, so the output is the one finding
  under test.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
mitigations with no day named: ['hostile text reaching the prompt', 'a steered agent reaching a dangerous tool', 'private data crossing a boundary', 'quota drain through a forced loop']
```

Four mitigations, no owners, caught by name. Those four are the whole defensive plan of Phase 10 —
guardrails, least privilege, data boundaries, the quota router — and the ablation shows what the
document degrades into when the reasons are softened: four sentences that sound like control and
schedule nothing.

There is a failure this check cannot catch, and a threat model should say so rather than let the green
tick imply otherwise. It asserts that a day is **named**. It cannot assert that the day happened, that
the mitigation was adequate, or that the named day still exists after a replan. That is why part
"In production" below moves the owner from a day to a ticket: a ticket has a state, and a state can be
queried.

## In production

**Accepted risks are the long-term danger, not the mitigated ones.** This is counter-intuitive and it
is the most useful thing in this part. A `mitigate` row is uncomfortable, so it gets revisited; it has
an owner, and the owner is asked about it. An `accept` row is comfortable — it has been decided, it
requires nothing, and it quietly stops being reasonable when the condition it depended on changes.
The two rows above are only right while the outward leg is cut, and the person who eventually adds an
outbound tool will not be reading this document. So accepted risks need a **review date and a trigger
condition**, and the trigger is more valuable than the date: *this acceptance is void if an outbound
capability is added to the answering path* is a sentence that can be attached to a code review, and a
date is a sentence that can be attached only to a calendar.

**Rows belong to tickets, not to days.** In this curriculum a mitigation names a day because the days
are the plan. In a real system, `Day 67` becomes a ticket reference, and the difference is that a
ticket has a state you can query: open, closed, closed-as-wont-fix. That turns the threat model from
a document into a report — *these four mitigations are open, this one was closed without a change,
here is why* — and it makes the check above stronger, because it can assert not just that an owner
exists but that the owner's ticket is not silently dead.

**What degrades at scale.** Verdicts drift towards `mitigate`. It is the comfortable column: it does
not require giving anything up, and it does not require defending an acceptance. After a few rounds a
threat model is thirty mitigations and nothing else, which reads as thorough and means that no
capability was ever traded away. The health check on a threat model is not how many rows it has — it
is how many rows are `refuse`, because those are the only ones that cost something, and a document
where nothing costs anything did not change any behaviour.

**Refusal is cheap now and expensive later.** Both `refuse` rows here are easy: nothing in Sutra needs
an outbound fetch tool, and nothing needs real customer data. That is the whole reason to make the
decision today. A capability is trivial to decline before anybody has built on it and nearly
impossible to withdraw afterwards, so the moment to spend a refusal is the moment it feels
unnecessary.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"The three-verdict structure is right and the accept
rows need one more sentence each. 'We accept that any archive row may be hostile' is only true while
nothing in the answering path can send data out — put that condition in the row, not in the design
doc, because in six months someone will propose a fetch tool and the only thing that will stop them is
this table saying so. Same for mislead: accepting it is fine, accepting it silently is not. Write
'accepted while a human reviews the draft' so that turning on auto-reply has to come back through
here."*

**The interview question:** *"You've done a threat model and found ten problems. What now?"* An honest
answer: *"Every one gets exactly one of three verdicts — accept, mitigate, refuse — and each verdict
owes something different. An acceptance owes the conditions it's reasonable under, because that's what
turns it into a trigger later: 'we accept hostile archive content given nothing in this path can send
data outward' fires when somebody proposes an outbound tool, and 'we accept hostile archive content'
doesn't. A mitigation owes an owner and a ticket, and I'd have a check that fails when a mitigation
names nobody, because that's the one that rots — I've seen the reason column degrade to 'we'll sort it
out later' and it still reads like a plan. A refusal owes what you're giving up, and it's the column
to watch: if a threat model has no refusals, nothing was traded and probably nothing changed. And I'd
be honest about the harm with no control — for us that was misleading answers — and write it as
accepted rather than dressing it up as mitigated."*

# 7.2 — The document that writes itself

## One-line answer

A threat model typed by hand describes the system **as it was on the day somebody typed it**, so this
one is generated from the same declarations `legs.py` and `channels.py` measure against — change the
wiring and the document changes with it, and `gate.py` goes red when the two stop agreeing.

## The story

There is a fire-exit plan screwed to the wall by the lifts, in a frame, behind glass. A red arrow
runs along the corridor, turns left past the kitchen, and ends at a door on the north side of the
building marked **EXIT 3**.

Exit 3 was bricked up two years ago, during the works that added the meeting rooms. The wall is
plastered and painted and there is a whiteboard on it now.

Everybody in the building walks past the plan several times a day and nobody has read it since the
week they joined. It is not that people are careless: the plan looks exactly the way a correct plan
looks. It is framed, it is official, it is accurate about the kitchen and the corridor and the lifts,
and it is wrong about the only thing anyone would ever need it for.

## The idea in plain language

A threat model is a document, and documents have a specific way of failing: they stop being true
without stopping being convincing.

The failure is not that somebody wrote it badly. The document above was correct when it was made. The
failure is that the **building changed and the drawing did not**, and nothing about the drawing knows
the difference — it has no way to be wrong out loud.

So this day's threat model is not typed. It is **generated**: one function reads the same declarations
the day's analysis scripts read, and prints a markdown document.

That gives it one property a hand-written document cannot have. When somebody adds a tool to
`legs.py`, or a channel to `channels.py`, the tables in the document change on the next run, because
they were never a separate copy of the facts — they *are* the facts, formatted. There is no version
of the system in which the wiring moved and the tables did not.

It also gives it one property people find uncomfortable, and part of this document's job is to be
straight about it: **a generated document cannot hold an argument.** It can hold the tables. Where the
reasoning goes is the subject of "In production" below, and this day's answer is the whole `parts/`
directory you are reading.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because the analysis in sections 2 to 6 is worth nothing next month if it is only in these documents.
Days 67 to 70 implement against this threat model — provenance and guardrails, least privilege, data
boundaries, the quota router — and each of them needs a stable statement of what it is implementing
against. That statement has to be a file, in the repo, that a person can open.

And because the specific way this day's analysis will go stale is known in advance. Sections 4 and 6
are both **counts over a declared wiring**: five stages, five channels, the trifecta closing at
`review`. Somebody will add a tool. When they do, those counts change, and the sentence that must not
be allowed to survive is *"we did a threat model, the trifecta is closed"*, written from a document
that was true in September.

## The mechanism

The generator is one function, and its shape is the argument:

```python
def render() -> str:
    """The threat model, as markdown."""
    out: list[str] = ["# Sutra threat model - injection", ""]
    out += ["Generated by `days/day-66-injection-threat-model/lab/model.py`. Do not hand-edit.", ""]

    out += ["## Assets", "", "| asset | what it is |", "| --- | --- |"]
    out += [f"| {name} | {what} |" for name, what in ASSETS]

    out += ["", "## Entry points", "", "| entry point | who can write it |", "| --- | --- |"]
    out += [f"| {name} | {who} |" for name, who in ENTRY_POINTS]

    out += ["", "## Where the three legs meet", "", "| stage | private | untrusted | outward |"]
    out += ["| --- | --- | --- | --- |"]
    carried: set[str] = set()
    trifecta_at = ""
    for stage in legs.GRAPH:
        carried = carried | legs.legs_of(stage, None)
        marks = ["yes" if leg in carried else "-" for leg in legs.LEGS]
        if len(carried) == 3 and not trifecta_at:
            trifecta_at = stage.name
        out.append(f"| {stage.name} | {marks[0]} | {marks[1]} | {marks[2]} |")
    out += ["", f"Accumulated along the path, the trifecta is complete at **{trifecta_at}**."]

    out += ["", "## Ways out", "", "| channel | needs | looks like a network call |"]
    out += ["| --- | --- | --- |"]
    out += [f"| {c.name} | {c.needs} | {'yes' if c.obvious else 'no'} |" for c in channels.CHANNELS]

    out += ["", "## Decisions", "", "| decision | about | because |", "| --- | --- | --- |"]
    out += [f"| {verdict} | {what} | {why} |" for verdict, what, why in DECISIONS]

    out += ["", "## The attacker", ""]
    out += [
        "Not a genius and not funded. Someone who can open a support ticket, has read one article",
        "about prompt injection, and is willing to wait a week. Every entry point above is",
        "reachable with a free account and a text box.",
        "",
    ]
    return "\n".join(out)
```

**Line by line:**

- The second line written into the document is *"Do not hand-edit"*. It is the most important line in
  the file: a generated document that somebody has edited by hand is worse than no document, because
  the next regeneration silently deletes their edit and nobody finds out which parts were theirs.
- The Assets and Entry points tables are comprehensions over the tuples part
  [7.1](7.1-what-is-worth-taking.md) taught. There is no second copy of those lists anywhere.
- The trifecta table is not a copy of section 4's finding — it **re-runs** it. `carried = carried |
  legs.legs_of(stage, None)` is the same accumulation part
  [4.3](../04-three-legs/4.3-the-line-that-passes-it-along.md) taught, executed at render time
  against whatever `legs.GRAPH` currently says. The document cannot claim a stage the code disagrees
  with.
- `trifecta_at` is captured on the **first** stage where the carried set reaches three, guarded by
  `and not trifecta_at` so a later stage does not overwrite it. Where it first closes is the useful
  fact; everything downstream of that point inherits it.
- The Ways out table iterates `channels.CHANNELS` and prints `obvious` as yes/no. Section 6's whole
  argument — four of five do not look like network calls — is a column in the document rather than a
  claim in it.
- `render()` returns a string and writes nothing. Printing and writing are separate decisions, which
  is what lets you read the document before committing to a file.

Generating it is one flag:

```bash
cd days/day-66-injection-threat-model/lab
uv run python model.py --write
```

**Line by line:**

- Without `--write` the document goes to stdout only. With it, `model.py` also writes
  `lab/threat-model.md` next to itself, using `Path(__file__).with_name(...)` so the file lands beside
  the generator rather than wherever you happened to be standing.
- The last line of the run reports the path and the line count, so a regeneration that produced
  nothing is visible rather than silent.

## When it breaks

The claim is *"change the wiring and the document changes with it."* A claim in a teaching document is
worth what its demonstration is worth, so here is the demonstration: take away the tool part
[4.4](../04-three-legs/4.4-closing-the-tap-nearest-the-outlet.md) refused, and re-render the section
that should move.

```bash
uv run python -c "
import channels, model

channels.CHANNELS = tuple(c for c in channels.CHANNELS if c.needs != 'fetch_url')
print(model.render().split('## Ways out')[1].split('## Decisions')[0].strip())
"
```

**Line by line:**

- The filter rebuilds `channels.CHANNELS` **in memory** without the `fetch_url` row. Nothing on disk
  is edited, which is deliberate: this has to demonstrate that the document follows the declaration,
  not that a file can be changed with an editor.
- `model.render()` is then called normally. It has no idea anything was patched — it reads
  `channels.CHANNELS` at render time, which is exactly the property under test.
- The two `split` calls cut out the one section that should have changed, so the output is the
  evidence and not a wall of unchanged tables.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
| channel | needs | looks like a network call |
| --- | --- | --- |
| markdown image | send_reply (rendered) | no |
| the reply itself | send_reply | no |
| memory write | save_memory | no |
| error message | any tool that echoes its input | no |
```

Four rows instead of five, and nobody edited a document. That is the property: the tables are a view
over the declarations, so the drawing on the wall changes when the wall does.

Now the failure this part exists for. A generated document only stays true **if it is regenerated**,
and the realistic mistake is not editing it — it is adding a declaration and never running the
generator again. `gate.py` is where that is caught:

```bash
uv run python -c "
import gate, model

model.ENTRY_POINTS = model.ENTRY_POINTS + (('ticket attachment', 'anyone who can attach a file'),)
print(gate.check_entry_points() or 'pass')
"
```

**Line by line:**

- A sixth entry point is added in memory — a ticket attachment, which is a perfectly reasonable door
  somebody would add — and the artefact on disk is left exactly as it was. That is the shape of the
  real mistake: the code moved, the file did not.
- `gate.check_entry_points()` reads `threat-model.md` from disk and looks for each declared entry
  point's name in the text. It returns an empty string on success, so `or 'pass'` makes a pass
  visible rather than printing a blank line.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
entry points absent from the artefact: ['ticket attachment']
```

Red, by name, on the row that is missing. That is a test that can go red (Principle 11) for the exact
reason threat models actually rot, and the two checks that do the work are worth reading:

- `check_artefact()` fails when `threat-model.md` does not exist at all, with the command that fixes
  it in the message. A missing document is the easy case; the message exists so that the person who
  hits it does not have to go looking.
- `check_entry_points()` is the drift check above. Note its shape — it compares **the declaration
  against the text of the document**, in that direction. It can tell you the document is behind the
  code. It cannot tell you the code is behind the building; that is part
  [7.1](7.1-what-is-worth-taking.md)'s limit, and no script has it.

## In production

**Generated documentation that nobody regenerates.** This is the standard fate, and it is worse than a
hand-written document in one specific way: the "Do not hand-edit" line makes people trust it more. The
only version of this that works is one where the generator runs somewhere nobody can skip — in CI, on
every change to the files it reads, failing the build when the committed artefact differs from a fresh
render. That is a two-line job and it is the difference between a control and a habit. Until it
exists, write down honestly that the document is current as of the last time somebody ran it.

**The reviewer's objection, answered.** *"A generated threat model can't hold the reasoning, and the
reasoning is the point."* That is correct, and it is not an argument against generating the tables. It
is an argument for keeping the two in different places: facts that can go stale are generated, and the
argument — why the outward leg is the one to cut, why direct injection is accepted, why `mislead` has
no owner — is written by a person and reviewed by people. On this day the argument is the `parts/`
directory, and the generated file links back to it. What must never happen is the reverse: a
hand-written narrative that restates the counts, because the narrative is where the stale number will
live.

**What degrades at scale.** One generator over one wiring is easy. Ten services with ten wirings
produce ten documents, and the interesting risks are the ones that cross them — precisely the risks no
single generator can see. The organisation-level version of this is a system inventory that each
service registers into, and the honest thing to say is that the aggregation problem is not solved by
generating harder; it is solved by somebody whose job is to read across.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"I like that it's generated, but nothing enforces
regeneration — the artefact in the repo could be six weeks behind the declarations and the only way to
find out is to run the gate by hand. Add a CI step that renders and diffs against the committed file.
And put a generated-on date in the header, because the first question anyone asks about a threat model
is how old it is, and 'do not hand-edit' doesn't answer it."*

**The interview question:** *"How do you keep a threat model from going stale?"* An honest answer:
*"Generate the parts that can be derived, and put the parts that can't next to it rather than inside
it. On the desk I built, the asset list, the entry points, the trifecta table and the channel list all
come out of the same declarations the analysis scripts use, so adding a tool changes the document. The
argument stays hand-written, because a generator can't tell you why we refused an outbound fetch tool.
Then the piece that actually matters: a check that fails when the committed document doesn't match a
fresh render, running in CI. Without that it's still a document somebody has to remember to update,
and the failure mode is the fire-exit plan on the wall — correct about everything except the thing you
need it for."*

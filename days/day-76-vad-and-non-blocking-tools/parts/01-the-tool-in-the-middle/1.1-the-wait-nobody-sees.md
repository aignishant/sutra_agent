# 1.1 — The wait nobody sees

## One-line answer

A tool call is **invisible in a request and unavoidable on a line**: when the caller sent one message
and is waiting for one answer, nobody can tell whether that wait was spent thinking or looking
something up — but when the line is open the caller is *present* for the wait, can hear the silence,
and is entitled to speak into it, and every design question on this day follows from that one change
in who is in the room.

## The story

Your friend keeps the keys to the community hall, and you want to know whether it is free on
Saturday.

So you text them. *Is the hall free Saturday?* Then you put your phone in your pocket and get on with
the shopping. Some time later the reply comes back: *yes, from six.* You have no idea what happened
in between. Maybe they knew straight away and were just slow to pick up the phone. Maybe they had to
go home, find the paper diary in the kitchen drawer, and turn to the right page. It makes no
difference to you whatsoever, because you were not there. The lookup, if there was one, happened
somewhere you could not see, and the only thing that reached you was the answer.

Now the same question on a different day. You run into them on the corner by the postbox, both of you
holding bags, and you ask out loud: *is the hall free Saturday?*

They say "hang on" and start scrolling their phone.

And now the wait is a completely different object. You are standing there. You can see them
scrolling. You watch the little spinner on their screen. The silence is not somewhere else any more —
it is between the two of you, on a street corner, and it is yours to fill. Do you say something? Do
you wait? After a while you start to wonder whether they actually heard the question, and you have to
decide whether repeating it would be helpful or annoying. When they eventually say "yes, from six",
the answer is exactly the same answer as the text message. Nothing about the lookup changed. What
changed is that you were **present for it**.

Notice what you gained and what you lost. On the corner you can say "actually, never mind, it was
just an idea" half-way through and save them the trouble — you could never do that with the text,
because there was no half-way through that you could reach. But you also now have a problem you did
not have before: somebody has to fill that silence, and if nobody does, it starts to feel like
something has gone wrong.

That is the whole of today. The lookup is the same lookup. The person waiting for it moved.

## The idea in plain language

Two words first, because the rest of the day leans on both.

A **tool call** is the moment an agent stops producing words and runs a piece of your code instead.
The model does not know your order numbers, so it emits a structured request — *call `check_order`
with `order_id="A17"`* — the runtime runs that function, hands the return value back to the model,
and the model carries on talking with the answer in hand. You have been writing tools since the early
days of this curriculum. What is new today is not how they are written; it is who is watching while
they run.

An **open connection** is a network link that stays up between messages, so that both ends may send
at any moment rather than taking strict turns. Day 75 opened one and named the shape: a request is
half duplex — you speak, then it speaks — while a live session is full duplex, with both directions
live at once. Everything below is a consequence of that connection still being open while your
function is off doing something.

Now put the two together. In a request/response agent, a tool call happens **inside** the wait the
caller already accepted. They sent one message; they are owed one answer; the gap in between is
undifferentiated. Whether it contained zero tool calls or five, whether one of them was retried
twice, whether one of them was slow enough to be embarrassing — none of that is observable from
outside, and none of it is the caller's problem. The wait is a single opaque block, and the answer is
the only thing that crosses the boundary.

On an open voice line, that block is not opaque. The microphone is live for the whole of it. The
caller can hear the silence, and — this is the part people underestimate — they are *entitled* to
speak into it. They have not handed over a turn and gone away. They are standing on the corner.

Line the three arrangements up:

| What the caller can observe | A request/response agent | A streamed text answer (Day 74) | An open voice line |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Can they tell a tool ran? | No. One wait, no detail | Only that the text stopped arriving for a bit | Yes — they are listening to the gap in real time |
| Can they say something during it? | No. There is no "during" they can reach | Not meaningfully — the channel is still one-way | Yes, at any moment, and they will |
| What does silence mean? | Nothing. It is not visible | The stream is between chunks | Ambiguous: thinking, broken, or not listening — and they cannot tell which |
| Who has to fill the wait? | Nobody | Nobody | Somebody must decide — part [2.3](../02-when-the-answer-comes-back/2.3-what-to-say-while-you-wait.md) |

Read the second row twice. Day 74 made the answer arrive in pieces, which changed *when the reader is
served*, and it is tempting to file voice under "streaming, but louder". It is not. Streaming
improved the wait for a caller who was still, structurally, absent. A live session puts them in the
room. The middle column is a better request; the right-hand column is a different situation.

And read the third row twice as well, because it is the one that turns into work. In every
arrangement you have built so far, silence was either invisible or self-explanatory. Here it is a
message with three possible meanings and no way for the caller to distinguish them, which is why the
rest of this day exists.

## Why Sutra needs it

Day 75 gave the desk an open line and answered the questions that come with merely holding one open:
who ends the call, what the one event that says *stop* actually says, and what a session costs when
it is priced by how long it is held. Every measurement on that day was taken with the desk doing
nothing but talking. The model spoke, the caller spoke, and no code of yours ran in between.

That is not a desk. A desk looks things up. The moment the desk can answer "where is my order", there
is a function in the middle of the conversation, and the conversation is on an open line while it
runs.

So today is Day 75's loop with a tool call dropped into it, and the hub's build brief is one module —
`sutra/livetools.py` — holding the tools the desk is allowed to call while somebody is listening. The
brief asks for things no request-shaped tool module has ever needed: every tool genuinely
non-blocking, a decision about when each result reaches the model, a decision about what the caller
hears while it runs, and a timeout per tool. Those are not four unrelated requirements. They are four
consequences of one fact, and this part is that fact.

There is a second reason and it outlives voice. Any system where the requester stays connected while
you work — a collaborative document, a device holding a session open, a support chat where the person
is watching the typing indicator — inherits the same reversal. Waiting becomes part of the product
rather than a gap in it. Once you have seen it in a voice agent, you will recognise it everywhere,
and you will start asking "how long is this at its worst" about code you would previously have called
finished.

## The mechanism

The day's lab file is `lab/_live.py`: Day 75's scripted connection with a tool added and a timestamp
on everything that passes through. Two pieces of it belong to this part. Both are quoted exactly as
they sit in the file; both already start at the left margin, so nothing has been dedented.

First, the number the whole day turns on:

```python
# How long the lookup takes. A real one waits on a warehouse system; this one waits on a clock, and
# the number is small enough that the day's runs finish and large enough that a caller would notice.
LOOKUP_SECONDS = 0.5
```

**Line by line:**

- `LOOKUP_SECONDS = 0.5` is a stand-in, and it is worth being explicit about what it stands in for.
  It is not "a pause to make the demo interesting". It is the place where a real tool would be
  waiting on something outside this process: a warehouse system, a database, another team's service
  behind two hops of network. You do not own that thing, you did not choose how fast it is, and on a
  bad afternoon it is slower than it was when you wrote the tool.
- The comment says *large enough that a caller would notice*, and that is the design decision in the
  constant. Half a second of dead air on a phone line is not nothing. Say a sentence out loud, stop
  dead in the middle of it for half a second, and then carry on — that is the experience this number
  is buying. A tool that took twice as long, or five times, is entirely ordinary in a real
  system, and nobody who wrote it ever had to think about it, because nobody was listening.
- The number is a constant rather than a literal inside the function for one practical reason: the
  rest of the day measures against it, and a measurement wants one place to change.

Second, the tool itself:

```python
async def check_order(order_id: str) -> dict:
    """Look up an order in the warehouse system.

    Args:
        order_id: the order reference the caller read out.

    Returns:
        The order's status. Slow, because looking things up in somebody else's system is slow.
    """
    if BLOCKING:
        # Does not yield. Nothing else in this process runs while it waits - including the task
        # reading the caller's microphone. A synchronous database driver, a blocking HTTP client
        # and a busy loop all behave exactly like this line.
        time.sleep(LOOKUP_SECONDS)
    else:
        # Yields. The event loop is free to run everything else while this tool waits.
        await asyncio.sleep(LOOKUP_SECONDS)
    return {"order": order_id, "status": "shipped"}
```

**Line by line:**

- `async def check_order(order_id: str) -> dict:` — an ordinary Python function with a type-annotated
  argument and a return type. There is no decorator, no registration call, no schema written by hand.
  ADK reads the signature. That is why the annotations are not decoration: `order_id: str` is what
  tells the model the argument is a string, and a missing annotation is a missing part of the
  contract.
- The **docstring is read by the model**, and this is the single most-missed fact about tools in any
  framework. *"Look up an order in the warehouse system"* is not a note for the next developer; it is
  the sentence the model uses to decide whether this function is the right one to call for what the
  caller just said. `Args:` and `Returns:` are read too. A tool with a vague docstring is not badly
  documented, it is badly *specified*, and the symptom is a model that calls it at the wrong moment
  or not at all.
- The `Returns:` line says *"Slow, because looking things up in somebody else's system is slow."*
  Read that as a statement of fact about your job rather than as a joke. The slowness is not a defect
  in this tool. It is the normal condition of any tool that leaves the process.
- `if BLOCKING:` — there is a switch here, and it decides how the tool waits. **Naming it is all this
  part does.** Which branch you are on turns out to decide whether the caller's microphone is still
  reaching the model at all, the two branches produce measurably different sessions, and the whole of
  part [1.3](1.3-the-line-that-goes-dead.md) is that measurement. Do not reason about it yet; you are
  not supposed to be able to predict the result from this page.
- `time.sleep(LOOKUP_SECONDS)` and `await asyncio.sleep(LOOKUP_SECONDS)` are the two branches, and
  they are the same wait, for the same duration, written two ways. The word in the second comment —
  **event loop** — is the name of the thing that gets to run while a tool is waiting. Part 1.3 is
  where that name earns its keep. Here, note only that one branch hands control back and the other
  does not, and that both live inside a function declared `async def`.
- `return {"order": order_id, "status": "shipped"}` — a plain dictionary. Nothing about the return
  value knows or cares that a person is listening. That is exactly the point of this part: **this
  function is fine.** It would pass review in any request-shaped service in the world. Everything
  difficult about today happens outside it.

What the runtime actually does with this function — the model asking for it, the runtime running it,
the result travelling back through the connection's `send_content`, and the answer coming out the
other side, with a real timestamp on each step — is part [1.2](1.2-the-sequence-measured.md), which
is the next thing to read. Part [1.3](1.3-the-line-that-goes-dead.md) then flips the switch and counts
four slices of the caller's audio against zero.

## When it breaks

The failure this part sets up is not a traceback. There is nothing to paste, and that is precisely
what makes it expensive.

**A tool that is perfectly acceptable in a request becomes a user-experience decision the moment
somebody is listening.** Consider three properties that are completely unremarkable in a
request-shaped tool layer:

| Property | In a request | On an open line |
| --- | --- | --- |
| It is slow at the tail | Latency graph has a long tail; nobody complains | Dead air, of unpredictable length, in the middle of a sentence |
| It retries on failure | Correct. Improves the success rate | Each retry is more silence the caller has to sit through |
| It is chatty — several calls to answer one thing | Fine; they are cheap and parallel | Several gaps, or one long one, with the caller in the room |

Every row on the right is the same tool. Nothing was rewritten. The tool did not get worse; the
audience arrived.

**And teams discover this by shipping.** The order it happens in is almost always the same, and it is
worth recognising because you will be in the middle of it one day. A company has a working tool layer
— it has been in production for a long time, it is well tested, it is somebody's pride. Then a voice
interface is added on top, because that is obviously the cheap way to do it: the tools already exist,
so this is a new front end, not a new system. The demo goes beautifully, because the demo asks the
one question whose lookup is fast and cached. Then it meets real callers, and the bug reports come
back in language that no engineer can act on. *It goes quiet.* *It feels broken.* *I thought it had
hung up on me.* *I said it again and then it answered twice.*

None of those map to a metric anyone has. The error rate is zero, because nothing errored. The
latency dashboard looks the same as it did last month, because it *is* the same as it was last month.
The tool layer is not at fault in any sense a test can express — it was simply never designed to be
waited on out loud, and nobody asked it to be, because until the microphone existed the question was
meaningless.

The honest version of this failure is a sentence you will have to say in a meeting: *the tools are
fine, and they are also the problem, and those are not contradictory.*

## In production

**The wait becomes content.** This is the shift, and it deserves to be stated as bluntly as possible.
In a request, silence costs nothing and communicates nothing — there is no channel on which it could
be heard. On a line, silence is delivered to the caller, continuously, and it means something to
them. So somebody has to decide what fills it. Nothing? A held tone? A sentence like "let me check
that for you"? All three are choices, all three ship, and the third one is more dangerous than it
looks, because a sentence is a claim and a claim can be false — the lookup may already have failed by
the time the words come out. Part
[2.3](../02-when-the-answer-comes-back/2.3-what-to-say-while-you-wait.md) is that problem in full.
What belongs here is only that the decision now exists and cannot be skipped: not deciding is
deciding on silence.

**What a professional checks before exposing an existing tool to a voice agent.** Three questions,
and they are asked of the tool, not of the agent:

| Question | Why it is now different | What "no answer" costs you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How long does it take at p99? | The average is irrelevant; the caller experiences the tail | You are shipping an unknown length of dead air |
| What does it do when it fails? | It has to fail into something sayable, fast | The line holds while a retry loop the caller cannot hear plays out |
| Can it be cancelled? | The caller may change their mind mid-lookup | You finish a lookup nobody wants and answer a question that was withdrawn |

*p99* is the ninety-ninth percentile: the value that all but one call in a hundred comes in under. It
is the standard way to talk about the slow tail, and it matters here because one call in a hundred is
a very large number of callers sitting in silence. The first question usually has an answer somebody
can find in a dashboard. The second usually has an answer nobody has written down. The third almost
never has an answer at all, because cancellation is the requirement that request-shaped systems get
away with ignoring — if the requester disappears, their answer is just discarded, and nobody notices
the wasted work. Part [4.2](../04-in-production/4.2-what-a-real-one-adds.md) is where cancellation
and per-tool timeouts get built.

**The tools become a smaller, curated set.** A request-shaped agent can carry a large tool list
cheaply, because a bad or slow choice costs a little latency inside an opaque wait. On a line, every
tool in the list is a potential silence, so the list stops being "everything the agent might need"
and becomes "everything we are willing to make somebody wait for out loud". That is a product
decision wearing a configuration file's clothes, and it is the reason the hub's build brief asks for
`TOOLS` as a single list in one place rather than tools assembled at each call site.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"The function is fine and I have no changes to it,
which is what worries me. This tool was written for the API and it is about to be reachable from a
phone line, and nothing in the diff shows me that anybody thought about the difference. Three things
before this goes out. One: I want the p99 for this lookup written in the docstring, from the actual
dashboard and dated, not an estimate — if we cannot get that number then we do not know what we are
shipping. Two: what happens when the warehouse is down? Right now I think we hold the line while
something retries, and the caller hears nothing and hangs up, and we log a success because the retry
eventually worked. Three: if the caller changes their mind while this is running, what stops it? I
already know the answer is 'nothing', and I am not blocking on it today, but I want a `TODO` with a
name on it rather than silence. And please stop calling this 'adding voice to the existing tools'.
The tools have a new audience and that is a change to the tools."*

**The interview question:** *"You have a working set of agent tools behind a REST API and the company
wants a voice interface. What do you look at first?"* An answer that shows the work: *"I would not
start with the voice stack, I would start with the tools, because the thing that changes is who is
present during a tool call. In a request nobody can tell whether the wait was spent thinking or
looking something up — it is one opaque gap and it is not the caller's problem. On an open line the
caller is in the room for it: the microphone is live, they can hear the silence, and they are allowed
to speak into it. So properties that were completely fine become user-experience decisions. Slow at
the tail is now dead air. A retry is now more dead air. Several small calls are now several gaps. So
the first pass is per tool: what is the p99 from the real dashboard, what does it do when the
dependency is down, and can it be cancelled if the caller changes their mind — and in my experience
the third one has no answer, because request systems get away with never implementing cancellation.
The second thing is that silence is now content, so somebody has to choose what fills it, and 'say we
are checking' is a claim that can turn out to be false if the lookup already failed. And I would push
back on the framing that this is a new front end over the same tools. The tools have a new audience,
which makes it a change to the tools. The failure mode I would warn the team about is that none of
this shows up in monitoring — error rate stays at zero, latency is unchanged, and the bug reports
come back as 'it feels broken', which is not something you can put on a dashboard."*

## Check yourself

```bash
cd days/day-76-vad-and-non-blocking-tools/lab

```

**Line by line:**

- `cd` into the lab first, because the day's scripts do `import _live` as a sibling module and will
  not find it from the repository root.
- `uv run` executes inside this project's pinned environment, so the `google-adk` being exercised is
  the version in `uv.lock`. Nothing here contacts a provider: the model at the far end is
  `lab/_live.py`, a scripted connection, so the run costs no quota.
- `freeze.py` is the day's central measurement and it belongs to part
  [1.3](1.3-the-line-that-goes-dead.md). **Run it now and read only the ordering** — which things
  happened before which — and deliberately ignore every number it prints. The question this part can
  answer is just: is there a stretch of the run where the tool is busy and the connection is still
  open? The numbers are 1.3's, and reading them here will spoil a result you should meet as a
  surprise.

Two exercises.

**One.** Open the last tool you wrote for a request-shaped agent — a real one, from real work, not an
example. Without changing a line, write down three sentences: what it does at its slowest, what it
does when the thing it depends on is unavailable, and what stops it if the caller walks away. If you
cannot answer any of the three from memory, that is the finding, and it is the same finding a team
gets on the day they add a microphone.

**Two.** Write out, in your own words, what the caller hears in each of the three columns of this
part's first table when a tool takes half a second. Then do it again for a tool that takes ten
seconds. One of the three columns changes character completely between the two, and naming which one
and why is the whole of this part.

**Out loud, without scrolling up:** *the lookup is identical in both cases, so what exactly does an
open line change about a tool call — and why does that change produce bug reports that no error rate
and no latency dashboard will ever show?*

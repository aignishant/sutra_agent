# 1.2 — The sequence, measured

## One-line answer

A tool call inside a live session is a **four-step round trip in which the runtime does the middle
two** — the model asks for the tool and does not finish its turn, the runtime runs it, the runtime
hands the result back through the connection's `send_content`, and only then does the model answer —
and knowing which two steps are the model's and which two are the runtime's is what makes every
measurement in the rest of this day readable.

## The story

The bakery on the corner has a counter at the front and a hatch at the back, and one person serving.
You ask for two of the seeded loaves. She does not go anywhere. She turns her head towards the hatch
and calls through it — *have we got seeded, two?* — and then she stands there, at the counter, in
front of you, saying nothing, because she has asked and she has not been answered yet.

A few seconds later a tray comes through the hatch with two seeded loaves on it, and a voice says
what it is. Only then does she turn back to you and say yes, two seeded, that's four pounds.

Count what happened. She asked. Someone else fetched. That someone handed the loaves through the
hatch and said what they were. She told you. Four steps — and two of them were not hers at all. You
never spoke to the person in the back. You do not know their name. You could not have asked them
anything, because the only person you have any way of talking to is the one at the counter, and she
was busy waiting.

Two details from that are the whole of this part.

The first is that she never left. Part [1.1](1.1-the-wait-nobody-sees.md) put you at a counter and
made you wait; this part is about who was doing what during the waiting. She stayed where she was.
Everything that came out of her mouth came out at the counter, and the fetching happened somewhere
you cannot see.

The second is the voice at the hatch. The loaves alone would not have been enough. If the tray had
appeared silently she would have had to guess what was on it, and if two trays were coming — seeded
and the tin loaf somebody else asked for — she would have had to guess which was which. The words
*that's your seeded* are not politeness. They are the label that makes a tray usable, and the
mechanism below has exactly the same label in it, spelled `name`.

## The idea in plain language

Three words have to be exact before the sequence makes sense, and all three appear in the code.

A **turn** is one side's go in a conversation. In a request/response agent a turn is obvious: you
send a message, the model produces an answer, the turn is over. On an open line the model has to say
when its turn ends, because nothing else can tell — which is why every response carries a
`turn_complete` flag, and why Day 75 part 3.2, *Who says the turn ended*, was about who sets it.

A **function call** is not the model running anything. It is the model producing a small structured
message that says *I want this tool run, with these arguments* — a name and a bag of arguments, and
nothing else. The model has no hands. It cannot open a database or reach a warehouse system. It can
only ask.

A **function response** is the answer travelling the other way: a small structured message carrying
the name of the tool that was run and whatever that tool returned. It is the tray coming through the
hatch, with the words attached.

Now the sequence, in the order it happens:

1. **the model asks** — it emits a `function_call` and marks the response `turn_complete=False`,
   meaning *I have not finished, I need something first*;
2. **the runtime runs the tool** — it looks the name up among the tools the agent was given, calls
   the function, and waits for it;
3. **the runtime sends the result back** — it wraps what the tool returned in a `function_response`
   and pushes it into the model's connection through `send_content`;
4. **the model answers** — now it has something to say, and this time it finishes its turn.

Steps 1 and 4 are the model's. Steps 2 and 3 are the runtime's. That split is the thing to keep,
because almost everything that goes wrong on this day goes wrong in step 2 or step 3, and neither of
them is the model's fault or the model's to fix.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because the rest of the day is a set of measurements taken at specific points in that sequence, and
without the sequence they are just numbers.

Part [1.3](1.3-the-line-that-goes-dead.md) measures what reaches the connection **during step 2** —
four slices of the caller's audio, or none, depending on one word inside the tool. That question only
exists once you know that step 2 is the runtime's, that it takes real waiting, and that the caller is
still on the line while it happens.

Section 2 is entirely about **step 3**, and specifically about *when* it should land — part
[2.1](../02-when-the-answer-comes-back/2.1-three-schedulings.md) reads `SILENT`, `WHEN_IDLE` and
`INTERRUPT` off the installed package. That is a question about the timing of one arrow in a diagram,
and you cannot ask it until the arrow has a number on it.

And it closes a loop from yesterday. Day 75 part 1.2, *Five methods and a context manager*,
established that a live model is five methods behind a context manager, and listed `send_content` as
*push one complete message — typed text, or a transcript the client already has*. That was true and
it was half the story. Today the same method carries something the caller never said and never
sees: the runtime's own message, on the caller's behalf. One method, two speakers.

## The mechanism

### The run

The measurement below is the default run of the day's central script, on 2026-09-06:

```bash
cd days/day-76-vad-and-non-blocking-tools/lab
uv run python freeze.py
```

**Line by line:**

- `cd .../lab` puts you in the day's lab directory, because `freeze.py` does `import _live` — a
  plain module import that resolves against the directory the script lives in. Run it from the repo
  root and you get `ModuleNotFoundError: No module named '_live'` before anything else happens.
- `uv run` executes it inside this project's environment, so the ADK doing the work is the version
  this repository pins rather than whatever Python is first on your `PATH` (Principle 7).
- No flag. `freeze.py --blocking` is the ablation, and it belongs to part
  [1.3](1.3-the-line-that-goes-dead.md); this part reads only the ordering of the default run, where
  the lookup awaits.
- No API key is needed and no provider is called. The model's side is scripted in `_live.py`; the
  runner, the queue, the tool call and the tool are the real ones.

### What the connection saw

This is the ordering half of that run's output, pasted exactly:

```text
  what the connection saw, in order:
      caller says something
      model asks for the tool
    | caller audio arrives
    | caller audio arrives
    | caller audio arrives
    | caller audio arrives
      tool result in: check_order
      model answers
      hang up
```

**Line by line:**

- Every line is something that passed through the one open connection, in the order it passed
  through, timestamped by the connection itself as it happened.
- `caller says something` is the question that starts the call — `where is order A17?`, pushed into
  the live request queue as text, arriving at the connection through `send_content`.
- `model asks for the tool` is **step 1**. The model has produced a `function_call` and has not
  finished its turn.
- `tool result in: check_order` is **step 3**, and note what it prints: the name of the tool that
  answered, read off the `function_response`. That is the voice at the hatch.
- `model answers` is **step 4**. Between it and `model asks for the tool` there is no other line from
  the model, which is the sequence's whole shape: the model said one thing, went quiet, and said the
  next thing only after something arrived for it.
- **Step 2 has no line of its own**, and that is not an omission. The tool runs *beside* the
  connection, not through it — it is an ordinary Python function the runtime calls, and the
  connection never sees it. What you can see of step 2 is its duration: everything between the second
  line and the seventh happened while the lookup was running.
- The four `| caller audio arrives` lines, and the marker on them, are **part 1.3's subject** — they
  are what the caller did during step 2, and the run prints counts underneath this block that this
  part deliberately does not quote. Read them here as evidence that the sequence has a *middle* with
  room in it, and move on.
- `hang up` is the connection closing, which happens because the turn completed and the script closed
  the queue — Day 75 part 1.3, *Who ends the call*, is the part about who is responsible for that
  line existing at all.

### The four steps, side by side

The last column is the point of the table. Three of the four steps are the same in a live session as
in an ordinary request/response agent, because a tool call is a tool call. Only one row differs, and
what differs about it is not the mechanism — it is that somebody is present for it.

| Step | Who does it | In the connection's log | In a request/response agent |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 — ask for the tool | the model | `model asks for the tool` | identical — the model returns a function call instead of text |
| 2 — run the tool | the runtime | nothing of its own; the caller's lines are what appear here | **the one that differs** — nothing can arrive during it, because the caller sent one message and is waiting for one answer |
| 3 — send the result back | the runtime | `tool result in: check_order` | identical in substance — the result is appended to the conversation and handed back to the model |
| 4 — answer | the model | `model answers` | identical — the model finally produces text |

### The method the result comes back through

This is `send_content` from `ScriptedConnection` in `lab/_live.py`. It sits inside a class, so the
common leading indentation has been removed to put it at the left margin; nothing else is changed.

```python
async def send_content(self, content: types.Content) -> None:
    for part in content.parts or []:
        if part.function_response:
            self._note(f"tool result in: {part.function_response.name}")
        elif part.text:
            self._note("caller says something")
    await self.inbox.put("go")
```

**Line by line:**

- The signature is the one the base class declares, unchanged: one `types.Content` in, nothing out.
  A real connection would serialise that object and write it to a socket.
- **The same method carries two completely different things**, and this is the sentence to take away
  from the whole part. A `function_response` arriving here was produced by the *runtime* in step 3.
  A piece of text arriving here was produced by the *caller*. Neither the method name nor the
  parameter type distinguishes them, because at this level they are both simply "a message for the
  model" — which is why the body's first job is to look at what it actually got.
- `for part in content.parts or []` walks the message's parts. A `Content` is a container: it holds a
  list of `Part` objects, and a part is where the actual thing lives. The `or []` guards the case
  where `parts` is `None` rather than an empty list, which is legal on the type and would otherwise
  raise `TypeError: 'NoneType' object is not iterable` on a message nobody expected.
- `if part.function_response:` is the test that tells the two speakers apart. It asks whether this
  part carries a tool result, and it does so by looking at the part rather than by trusting the call
  site. That is the honest way round: the connection is at the bottom of the stack and cannot know
  who called it.
- `part.function_response.name` is **how it knows which tool answered**. Today there is one tool and
  the field looks like decoration. It stops looking like decoration the moment two tools are in
  flight at once — the model asks for an order lookup and a delivery estimate, both results come back
  through this same method, and without `name` there is no way to say which reply belongs to which
  question. The voice at the hatch, again.
- `elif part.text:` is the other speaker: a whole finished message from the caller. `elif` rather
  than a second `if` because a part is one thing or the other, and running both branches on one part
  would double-count it in the log.
- `self._note(...)` appends `(seconds since the connection opened, what happened)` to the
  connection's event list. That list is the log printed above, and the reason this day can measure
  ordering at all: the timestamps are taken where the traffic actually is.
- `await self.inbox.put("go")` is what **wakes the scripted model**. `receive` is sitting on that
  queue waiting for something to arrive; putting an item in it lets `receive` continue and produce
  the next response. In a real connection this line is not a queue at all — it is the message going
  out over a socket to a server somewhere, and the model waking up is something that happens on
  another machine. The queue is a local stand-in for a network hop, and it is worth knowing which of
  the two you are reading.

### The model asking, and not finishing

This is the first-turn branch of `ScriptedConnection.receive` in the same file, dedented the same
way. The fragment starts one line above the branch, at the turn counter, because the counter is what
decides which branch runs:

```python
self.turn += 1
if self.turn == 1:
    self._note("model asks for the tool")
    call = types.FunctionCall(name=TOOL, args={"order_id": "A17"})
    yield LlmResponse(
        content=types.Content(role="model", parts=[types.Part(function_call=call)]),
        turn_complete=False,
    )
    continue
```

**Line by line:**

- `self.turn += 1` counts the model's goes. The scripted model asks for the tool on its first go and
  answers on its second, and the counter is the entire script.
- `types.FunctionCall(name=TOOL, args={"order_id": "A17"})` is the request for a tool, and it is
  worth seeing how little there is in it: a name and a dictionary of arguments. `TOOL` is the module
  constant `"check_order"`, and it has to match the name of the Python function the agent was given
  — that is the failure the next section is about.
- **The args are scripted**, and deliberately so. `order_id="A17"` was never extracted from anything
  the caller said; it is typed into the file. This day is about the plumbing — whether the result
  gets back, when it gets back, and what the caller can do meanwhile — and not about whether a model
  picks the right arguments out of a sentence. Making the arguments constant removes a variable that
  would otherwise be free to wobble in the middle of a measurement.
- `types.Part(function_call=call)` wraps the call as a part, and `types.Content(role="model",
  parts=[...])` wraps that as a message from the model. The same two-layer shape as `send_content`
  above, in the other direction.
- `turn_complete=False` is the load-bearing keyword in this fragment. It is the model saying *I have
  not finished, I need something first*. A model that set `turn_complete=True` here would be
  declaring its turn over while holding no answer — the runtime would treat the exchange as finished,
  the consumer's loop would break out on the completed turn, and the caller would be left with a
  hung-up line and a question nobody answered. The tool result would come back to a conversation that
  had already ended.
- `continue` returns to the top of `receive`'s loop, which blocks on the inbox again. That is the
  model going quiet — it has asked, and it now waits, and the only thing that will wake it is
  something arriving at `send_content`. Steps 2 and 3 happen entirely inside that wait.
- Nothing in this branch runs the tool, mentions the tool's code, or knows what a warehouse is. The
  model's contribution to step 1 is a name and two words of JSON, and everything else on this page is
  somebody else's job.

## When it breaks

**The name has to match, and getting it wrong is not subtle.** The `name` in the `FunctionCall` is
looked up against the tools the agent was actually given. If they disagree — a typo, a renamed
function, a tool dropped from the list during a refactor — the runtime raises a `ValueError` whose
first line reads `ValueError: Tool 'check_order' not found.`, followed by the list of tools it does
have and a set of suggested causes. That is the shape of the error rather than a transcript of it,
because this page has not run it, and pasting an invented traceback would be exactly the failure
Principle 8 exists to prevent. The reader can have the real one in one edit: rename the tool in
`_live.py` and run `freeze.py` again. That is the first exercise below, and it is worth doing, partly
because the error text is genuinely helpful and partly because the class of bug — *the model asked
for something that does not exist* — is one you will meet again wherever a name crosses a boundary.

**The scripted model always asks on its first turn and never on its second.** That is a fixture, not
a fact about models. A real model decides, on each turn, whether to call anything at all, and the
honest list of what it may do is longer than what `_live.py` does: call one tool, call several in the
same turn, call none and simply answer, or call the same tool twice with different arguments because
the caller mentioned two orders. Every one of those is normal. What that means for reading this day's
measurements is that the numbers describe the *plumbing* under one fixed script, and the plumbing is
what the day is claiming to have measured — not the model's judgement, which is not being exercised
here at all.

**Nothing on this page shows what happens when the tool raises.** `check_order` returns a dictionary
every time; it has no failure path. That is out of scope for this part on purpose, because the
interesting question about a failing tool on an open line is not *what does the sequence do* but
*what does the caller get told*, and that has been answered twice already. Day 67, guardrail
callbacks, is where a tool's inputs and outputs get inspected and blocked before they reach the model
at all. Day 72, backoff with honesty, is the rule that a failure is retried under a policy and then
**escalated** rather than smoothed over — never a fabricated result standing in for one that did not
arrive (Principle 10). Both of those apply unchanged to a live session; what a live session adds is
that somebody is listening to the silence while you decide, and part
[4.1](../04-in-production/4.1-the-silence-you-cannot-see.md) is that problem.

## In production

**What a professional logs for a tool call in a live session.** Four things, and the last one is the
one nobody thinks of until they have been burned:

| What to record | Why |
| --- | --- |
| which tool was asked for | the name that failed to resolve is the name in the log, and it is the first thing you look at |
| what arguments it got | the same tool with a different order reference is a different call; redact anything personal at the boundary, per Day 69 |
| how long it took | a tool the caller experienced as a pause needs a number on that pause, and the number has to be per tool rather than per session |
| whether the caller said anything while it ran | the only signal that distinguishes *the desk was busy* from *the line was dead* |

The fourth row is not obvious in advance. In a request/response agent it is not even meaningful —
nobody can say anything during a request. It becomes obvious about one line into part
[1.3](1.3-the-line-that-goes-dead.md), which is exactly the wrong time to discover you have not been
recording it. The general shape of the lesson: on a live channel, log what the *other* side did
during your work, not only what your work did.

**What changes at scale is that `name` becomes load-bearing.** With one tool and one call in flight,
matching a result to a request is trivial and every implementation gets it right by accident. With
several — a model that asks for the order status and the delivery estimate in the same turn — two
`function_response` messages come back through the same `send_content` and the only thing that says
which is which is the name field this part pointed at twice. Two consequences follow. First, tool
names have to be genuinely distinct and stable; renaming a tool is a wire-format change, not a
refactor. Second, if your system can have two calls to the *same* tool in flight, the name alone is
no longer enough to disambiguate them, and you need whatever call identifier the protocol gives you
rather than inventing a convention on top of the name. That is the point at which people discover
they have been treating a label as a key.

**And the timing of step 3 stops being free.** Once results can arrive while the model is mid-answer,
*when* the runtime delivers one is a decision with a caller on the other end of it. That is section
2, and part [2.1](../02-when-the-answer-comes-back/2.1-three-schedulings.md) reads the three
supported answers off the package rather than guessing at them.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Two things about the tool path. First, the log line
prints the tool name and nothing else — I want the duration and a request id on it, and I want the
duration measured around the runtime's call to the tool rather than inferred from the gap between two
connection events, because those two numbers will diverge the first time something queues. Second,
and this is the one that will bite us: we match results to requests by name. That works today because
we have one tool. The day product asks for a status lookup and an ETA in the same turn we will have
two responses coming back through the same method and a name field that no longer identifies
anything uniquely. Use the call id the protocol already gives us now, while it is a two-line change,
not after a caller has been read somebody else's order status. And put `turn_complete=False` in a
comment where we emit function calls — the next person to touch that branch will 'tidy' it to True
and the failure will look like a hang-up, not like a bug."*

**The interview question:** *"Walk me through what happens when a voice agent needs to look something
up mid-conversation. Who does what?"* An answer that shows the work: *"It's four steps and the
runtime owns the middle two. The model emits a function call — a name and some arguments, nothing
else, because the model can't run anything — and crucially it marks its turn as not complete, so it's
saying 'I've asked, I'm not done'. The runtime resolves that name against the tools the agent was
given and calls the function. Then the runtime wraps whatever came back in a function response and
pushes it into the model's connection, through the same method that carries the caller's own
messages. Only then does the model answer and finish its turn. I measured that ordering against a
scripted model with the real runner, and the connection log shows exactly those four lines with the
tool running in the gap. The two things I'd flag: the function response carries the tool's name, and
that's what matches an answer to a question — the moment you've got two tools in flight that field
is load-bearing and you probably want a proper call id rather than the name. And the middle two steps
are where the caller is still on the line, still speaking, which is a situation a request/response
agent never has to think about, because nobody can say anything during a request."*

# 2.2 — Choosing one

## One-line answer

Every one of the three schedulings is **wrong for something**, which makes this a per-tool decision
rather than a per-system one — and leaving `response_scheduling` unset is not abstaining from the
decision, it is taking whatever default the model happens to apply to a tool nobody thought about.

**Nothing in this part has been measured.** Part [2.1](2.1-three-schedulings.md) read the installed
package's contract for `response_scheduling`, and that is all anybody on this day has done: the field
is documented as Live API only, Day 74's free-quota check parked the paid model, and so not one of
these three values has been sent to a model on this machine. What follows is reasoning about a
documented contract, argued out loud so you can disagree with it. There is no output to paste here,
and I am not going to invent any.

## The story

You are at home, in the middle of a conversation with somebody about something that matters, and
three things arrive.

The first is your phone buzzing on the table. You glance at it: rain tomorrow. You do not say
anything. You are not hiding it — you have simply filed it, and later, when somebody asks whether to
put the washing out, you will already know the answer. The conversation does not pause.

The second is someone calling through from the next room that the school bus is running late. You
hear it, you keep listening to the person in front of you, and when they finish their sentence you
say *"the bus is late, so I'll go and get her."* You waited for the gap. There was going to be a gap.

The third is a smell of burning from the kitchen. You stop mid-word. You do not wait for a gap, you
do not apologise first, and the person you were talking to does not think you were rude — they follow
you in.

Nobody taught you which of those three was which. And notice what makes them different: it is not how
important the information is in the abstract, it is **what happens if the person keeps talking**. If
they keep talking, the rain forecast is fine and the bus is fine and the kitchen is not. That is the
whole question, and it is the question you are about to answer once per tool.

## The idea in plain language

A **tool** is a function the model is allowed to call — a lookup, a booking, a write. When it
finishes, its answer has to travel back to the model. On a request/response agent that is the end of
the story, because nothing else was happening: the caller sent one message and is sitting there
waiting for one reply.

On an open voice line, something else *is* happening. The caller may be mid-sentence. The model may
be mid-sentence. So there is a second question that a request never had to ask: when the answer
arrives, **what should the model do about it right now?**

That question is what `response_scheduling` answers, and the package offers four possible states for
it. Part [2.1](2.1-three-schedulings.md) printed them off the installed `google-adk==2.7.1` and
quoted the package's own sentences; this part takes those sentences and argues about which tool each
one belongs on.

One term first, because the package's own caveat depends on it. **Asynchronous function calling**
means the model can carry on with the conversation while a tool call is still outstanding, and deal
with the answer whenever it turns up, instead of stopping dead until the tool returns. A model that
cannot do that has nothing to schedule — there is only one moment the answer can arrive, so a setting
naming three of them means nothing. The package says as much: the field is ignored by models that do
not support asynchronous function calling, and `None` preserves the default behaviour.

## Why Sutra needs it

The hub's build brief asks for `sutra/livetools.py`, and one of its `TODO(me)` lines is to declare,
on at least one tool, how that tool's result should come back. That line exists because the tools a
voice desk calls are not interchangeable. Looking up which plan a caller is on and telling a caller
their booking just fell through are not the same kind of news, and the difference is not in the code
that fetches them — it is in what the caller should experience when the answer lands.

If Sutra ships a list of live tools with nothing declared, the list still works. That is exactly the
problem: it works in a way nobody chose, and the way it works can differ from tool to tool and from
model to model. Day 73 part 5.2 makes the same point about ambient jobs — a gate can check that
somebody wrote the function, not that they thought about it — and this is the voice-shaped version of
that gap.

## The mechanism

Here is the whole decision, one row per value. The middle column is the package's contract as part
2.1 read it; the last two columns are this part's argument, and you should push back on them.

| Value | What the package says it does | Right for | Wrong for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `SILENT` | feeds the response back without triggering a model turn | a preference lookup, the caller's account tier — things the model should *know* | anything the caller is waiting to hear, because nothing will prompt the model to say it |
| `WHEN_IDLE` | defers the reaction until the model is idle | almost everything: the caller finishes their sentence, then hears the answer | news that invalidates what the model is saying **while** it is saying it |
| `INTERRUPT` | reacts immediately | a result that changes what should be said *now* — the booking just went, the payment failed | anything routine, because interrupting a person is a strong act |
| `SCHEDULING_UNSPECIFIED` / `None` | `None` preserves the default behaviour | nothing you have thought about | every tool, because it is a decision made by not deciding |

A **model turn** is the model taking the floor and producing something — speaking. **Idle** is the
state where it is not doing that and the caller is not either: the gap. Those two words are what the
first three rows turn on.

Two of those rows are interesting enough to argue properly.

**`SILENT` is a filing cabinet, not a mute button.** The rain forecast in the story is `SILENT`. The
information genuinely arrived, it genuinely changed what you will say later, and it produced no
sentence at all at the moment it landed. That is the right shape for a lookup whose job is to make
the *next* answer better rather than to be an answer itself: which plan the caller is on, whether
they have opted out of marketing, what name they prefer to be called. Feeding that back without
triggering a model turn is precisely what you want, because a voice assistant that announces *"I have
retrieved your account tier"* is announcing its own plumbing.

Now the trap, and it is a bad one. Suppose the caller asked *"what plan am I on?"* — and the plan
lookup is the tool marked `SILENT`. The tool runs. The answer comes back. The model now knows the
answer, and nothing has asked it to speak. The caller hears silence, from a system that is not stuck
and not broken and has the answer sitting inside it. Nothing raised. Nothing logged. `SILENT` is
right for a result the model should *have* and wrong for a result the caller is *waiting for*, and
the same tool can be both depending on why it was called — which is uncomfortable, and worth saying
out loud rather than smoothing over.

**`INTERRUPT` is the one that costs something.** The burning smell in the story earns the
interruption. An order status does not. If your live tools interrupt the caller to tell them their
parcel is in transit, you have built something people find unpleasant to talk to, and you will not
find that in a log, you will find it in the fact that callers stop finishing their sentences.

The test that works is the story's test: **what happens if the caller keeps talking?** If the answer
is *nothing bad*, you want `WHEN_IDLE`. If the answer is *they carry on planning around something
that is no longer true*, you want `INTERRUPT`. A seat that has just been taken by somebody else, a
payment that has just been declined, a delivery slot that has just gone — in each of those the caller
is, at this exact moment, building the rest of their sentence on a fact that stopped being a fact.
Letting them finish is not politeness, it is letting them waste the words.

And there is a second argument, which is the one that generalises past this framework and past this
day. Day 75's paper on the organisation of turn-taking in conversation makes the case that
interruption is not a malfunction of conversation but a normal, licensed part of it — between parties
who hold equal rights to the floor. Then it observes what we actually deploy: a voice assistant is
given the obligations of a party and none of the rights. It must always yield. It never
self-selects. That asymmetry is deliberate, and it is most of what makes an assistant tolerable.

`INTERRUPT` is the single setting that hands a fragment of that right back to the machine. It is a
courtesy decision at least as much as a technical one, and it should be spent the way you would spend
a favour: rarely, on something that genuinely warrants it, by someone who has thought about what they
are asking for. A codebase where half the tools are `INTERRUPT` has not tuned a parameter, it has
quietly decided that the machine outranks the caller.

Which leaves the fourth row, and it is the honest one. Unset is not neutral. It is the default for a
tool whose behaviour nobody considered, applied by a model whose choice of default you did not read,
and it can change when you change models. The useful question was never *"which of these is best"* —
there is no best, that is the entire content of the table. It is *"is this result worth interrupting
a person for?"*, and an order status and a fire alarm do not have the same answer.

Now here is what the day's eval can and cannot see. From `lab/gate.py`, dedented from inside `main()`
and otherwise exactly as on disk:

```python
declared = [
    t
    for t in entries
    if getattr(t, "is_long_running", False) or getattr(t, "response_scheduling", None)
]
```

**Line by line:**

- `declared = [` — a list of the tools that said something about how their result comes back. The
  gate's check is `bool(declared)`: at least one, not all, because the build brief asks you to make
  the decision once and mean it before it asks you to make it everywhere.
- `for t in entries` — `entries` is `TOOLS` from `sutra/livetools.py` when it is a list, and an empty
  list when the module does not import. An empty list makes this check fail rather than silently
  pass, which is Principle 11: a check that cannot run counts as a failure.
- `getattr(t, "is_long_running", False)` — the first of the two ways a tool can say how its answer
  behaves. The default is `False`, so the third argument matches the package's own default and this
  reads as *"is this tool declared long-running?"*
- `getattr(t, "response_scheduling", None)` — the important one. The third argument is `None`, and
  `None` is exactly what an undeclared field holds. So a tool that set the field to any of `SILENT`,
  `WHEN_IDLE` or `INTERRUPT` is truthy here and a tool that left it alone is not. **That distinction
  — set to something, versus not set — is the whole subject of this part**, expressed in a
  single default argument.
- `or` — either field satisfies the check, because both are ways of answering the same question. A
  long-running tool is saying *the answer comes back later, separately*; a scheduling is saying *the
  answer comes back at this moment relative to the conversation*. A tool that declares neither has
  answered neither.

And the limit, which matters more than the check: this cannot tell whether the choice was **right**.
A tool marked `INTERRUPT` to look up an order status passes it. A tool marked `SILENT` for the exact
question the caller asked passes it. The gate can see that somebody made a decision; it cannot see
that they made a good one. Day 73 part 5.2 names that limit for gates in general, and it is worth
re-reading the next time a green gate feels like evidence.

## When it breaks

**The setting is right and inert, and nothing tells you.** The package says the field is ignored by
models that do not support asynchronous function calling. So you can reason your way to the correct
scheduling, write it down, review it, merge it — and have it do nothing at all, because the model on
the other end never had that capability. There is no warning for this. It is not an error. It is not
a log line. Your `INTERRUPT` simply never interrupts, and the first person to notice is a caller who
finds out too late that the seat had gone.

**The reasoning in this part is from a docstring, not from a run.** I want to be exact about the
status of everything above: part 2.1 read `google-adk==2.7.1` and quoted its sentences, and every
claim here about `SILENT`, `WHEN_IDLE` and `INTERRUPT` is downstream of those sentences plus an
argument about conversation. Nobody on this day has watched a model do any of it. Before you rely on
this — before a caller relies on it — verify the behaviour against the model you are actually going
to use, and be prepared for the answer to be *"the field was ignored."*

**The failure of choosing badly is not an exception.** This is the part that catches people. Every
other failure on this day has a shape you can catch: part 1.3's frozen line has a count of audio
slices that goes to zero, and the freeze measurement encodes it in an exit code. Choosing the wrong
scheduling raises nothing, logs nothing and exits zero. What it produces is a caller who finds the
assistant rude, or a caller who asked a question and never heard the answer. Both of those are
reports from a human being, arriving days later, phrased as *"it's a bit weird to talk to."* You
cannot grep for that. You can only decide it correctly in advance, which is why this part is an
argument and not a check.

## In production

What a professional actually does is small and it takes one line. Next to each tool, the scheduling
and **one sentence saying why** — not what the value means, which the package already says, but what
the caller experiences:

- the account-tier lookup, `SILENT`: *the model should know this; the caller never asked for it.*
- the order status, `WHEN_IDLE`: *they asked, they should hear it, and they can finish their
  sentence first.*
- the seat-availability re-check, `INTERRUPT`: *if this comes back empty, everything the caller is
  currently saying is about a seat that no longer exists.*

Three tools, three lines, and every future reader of that file can now disagree with you on the
record — which is the point. A value with no sentence beside it is indistinguishable from a value
somebody pasted.

Two things make this worth more care than its size suggests.

**It is a per-tool decision, made once, inherited for years.** The scheduling on a tool is written on
the day the tool is written, by whoever is closest to it, usually in the same commit as its
implementation. Nobody revisits it. It is not in a config file anyone reviews quarterly, it does not
appear on a dashboard, and there is no alert that fires when it is wrong. The person who suffers from
it is a caller in some other year, and they will not be able to describe the problem in terms of this
field. That asymmetry — a cheap decision now, an undiagnosable annoyance later — is exactly the
profile of the things worth writing a sentence about.

**And the count of `INTERRUPT` is a number worth knowing about your own system.** Not because there
is a correct value, but because it is the one setting that takes the floor from a person. If you
cannot say roughly how many of your tools have it, you cannot say how often your system is willing to
cut somebody off. Part [4.2](../04-in-production/4.2-what-a-real-one-adds.md) makes the neighbouring
argument about per-tool timeouts: the framework will not do it for you, and *per tool* is the
granularity that matters in both cases.

*The review comment a senior engineer leaves:* "Every tool in this list has `response_scheduling`
unset, and I'd like it set explicitly on all of them, including the ones where the answer is
`WHEN_IDLE` — an explicit `WHEN_IDLE` says somebody thought about it, and an absent one says nothing
at all, and I cannot tell those apart in six months. Second: put the reason in a comment, one
sentence, about the caller and not about the enum. Third, and this is the one I'd block on — the
availability re-check is `INTERRUPT` and the order-status lookup is also `INTERRUPT`. Those cannot
both be right. Interrupting somebody is the strongest thing this system can do to a caller, and if we
spend it on a parcel being in transit then nobody will believe us when the booking actually falls
through. Last: we have not run any of this against a model, so put that in the docstring. I would
rather read 'this is from the package contract, unverified against our model' than find out later
that we all assumed somebody had checked."

*The interview question:* "How do you decide when a tool's result should reach the model?" An honest
answer: *"There are three settings and a fourth state, and none of them is the best one — each is
wrong for something, so it's a decision per tool rather than per system. Silent feeds the result back
without triggering the model to speak, which is right for context the model should have and the
caller never asked for, and badly wrong when the caller asked for exactly that thing, because nothing
will prompt it to say the answer. When-idle is what most tools want: the caller finishes their
sentence and then hears it. Interrupt is for a result that changes what should be said right now — a
seat that's just gone, a payment that's just failed — and it's wrong for anything routine, because
interrupting a person is a strong act and doing it for an order status is how a voice agent becomes
unpleasant to use. And leaving it unset isn't neutral: it's a decision made by not deciding, resolved
by whatever the model defaults to. I'll be straight about where that reasoning comes from — I read
the framework's contract for the field, including the line saying it's ignored by models that don't
support asynchronous function calling, and I reasoned from that plus how turn-taking works between
people. I haven't measured it against a live model, so on a real system that's the first thing I'd
verify rather than assume."*

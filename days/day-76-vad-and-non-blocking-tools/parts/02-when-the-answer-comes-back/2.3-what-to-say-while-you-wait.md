# 2.3 — What to say while you wait

## One-line answer

On an open line the silence during a tool call has to be filled with **something**, and whatever
fills it is content — so *"I'm just checking that for you"* is a claim about what is happening, and a
desk that says it after the lookup has already failed, or while no lookup is running at all, has
produced a plausible sentence that is not true.

## The story

The train stops between stations. Not at a platform — in a cutting, with a hedge on one side and
nothing on the other. The engine noise drops away and the carriage goes quiet.

Nobody says anything. People look up from their phones, then back down. Somebody near the door stands
and sits again. The quiet is the worst part of it, because a train that has stopped and a train that
has broken down look exactly the same from inside, and the longer nothing is said, the more the
carriage assumes the second one.

Then the tannoy crackles. *"We're currently being held at a signal, waiting for a platform to become
available at the next station. We do apologise for the delay."*

The carriage relaxes. It is not good news, but it is a reason, and a reason is something you can sit
with.

Later the guard comes through the train checking tickets, and somebody asks how long the platform
will be. The guard says, honestly, that there is no platform problem — there is a fault somewhere
further up the line and the control room has not told them what it is yet. Nobody on the train knows
why the train has stopped. Nobody on the train knew when the announcement was made either.

The person on the microphone was not lying. They had a carriage full of people going quiet on them
and a set of sentences to choose from, and every sentence they had was a specific one. *Waiting for a
platform* is the ordinary reason a train stops outside a station, it is true most of the time, and it
was the closest thing to hand. It was not a lie anybody chose. It was a sentence that had to be said.

## The idea in plain language

**Before anything else: this part has no measurement, and it is not going to pretend it has one.**
The lab has no speaker, no voice and no filler in it. Part
[1.3](../01-the-tool-in-the-middle/1.3-the-line-that-goes-dead.md) measured something real and
narrow — that the line stays open while a tool runs, or does not, depending on one word inside the
tool. What to *put* into that opening is a design question, and this day does not run it. So there is
no command output below and no transcript, invented or otherwise. Where you would reasonably want
evidence, you will find the exact thing to go and try, marked `TODO(me)`.

One word first, because everything below leans on it.

**Filler** is whatever the system emits during a wait purely so that the wait is not silent. It is
not the answer, it is not part of the answer, and it exists only because the answer is late. *"One
moment."* *"Let me check."* *"Bear with me."* On a text interface it is a spinner, and a spinner
cannot be wrong. On a voice line it is words, and words can be.

That is the whole of this part, and it is one sentence: **filler is content.** It goes out of the
same mouth as the answer, the caller cannot tell it apart from the answer, and it is therefore
subject to exactly the same rule as the answer — Principle 10, *fail honestly*: never fabricate a
result to cover an error, and never assert something no measurement produced. The rule does not stop
applying because the sentence was only meant to be polite.

Part [2.2](2.2-choosing-one.md) settled *when* the tool's result should reach the model, and found
that not choosing is itself a choice. This part is the gap that setting opens up: whichever
scheduling you picked, there is a stretch of open line between the desk asking for the lookup and the
lookup coming back, and the caller is present for all of it.

There are exactly three honest things to put in that stretch, and one of them is usually not
available to you.

### 1 — Say nothing

Correct, free, and unpleasant past a certain length.

Nothing said is nothing claimed. Silence cannot be false, it costs no tokens, it needs no review, and
it can never contradict a result that has not arrived. As a default it is the only one of the three
you can ship without thinking, which is a real argument in its favour.

The problem is that on a voice call silence is ambiguous. Day 75's parts established this from the
other end: a line where nothing is being said and a line that has dropped sound identical, because in
both cases the caller's ear receives nothing. Part
[1.3](../01-the-tool-in-the-middle/1.3-the-line-that-goes-dead.md) says the same thing in the lab's
own words — a caller talking into a frozen session is "indistinguishable from the system having hung
up".

So silence is honest and uninformative at the same time, and the longer it runs the more the caller
fills it in themselves. That is the carriage going quiet before the tannoy. Nobody had said anything
false yet, and the carriage was already assuming the worst.

### 2 — Say something that is true regardless

A **neutral acknowledgement**: a sentence that tells the caller the line is alive and asserts nothing
that could later turn out false.

*"Bear with me a moment."* *"Still with you."* *"One moment, please."*

Read those against the story. None of them says why. None names a system, a step or a reason. What
they claim is that somebody is still on the line and has not forgotten the caller, and that claim is
true whatever the lookup does — it is true if the lookup succeeds, it is true if it times out, it is
true if the warehouse system returned a 500 before the sentence finished. The claim is about the
desk, and the desk is the one thing the desk can actually observe.

Compare with *"I'm just checking that for you."* That sentence is one small step more specific and it
crosses the line, because it asserts a fact about the lookup: that one is running and will produce
something. If the tool has already failed, or was never called, the sentence is false. It is the
tannoy's *waiting for a platform* — plausible, ordinary, usually true, and not known.

This is the option most voice systems should ship, and the thing to notice is that it is a **writing**
decision rather than an engineering one. Nothing in any framework stops you from saying the true
version. Somebody has to sit down and write a sentence that is comforting and empty at the same time,
which is harder than it sounds and is done once.

### 3 — Say something specific and true

The best answer, and the one you usually cannot have.

*"The warehouse system is taking a while — I'm still on it."* *"I've got two of the three, waiting on
the last one."* These are specific, they are useful, and they are honest — **if** they are derived
from something the tool actually reported.

That is the catch, and it is worth being blunt about where the cost lands. Most tools are a single
call that either returns or does not. There is nothing to report during it, because there is no
"during" — from the caller's side and from the code's side alike, the tool is one `await` with no
inside. To say something specific and true, the tool has to become something that emits progress: a
long-running call that hands back a reference first and finishes later, a stream of intermediate
states, a partial result. The hub's build brief points at exactly this surface when it asks you to
declare `is_long_running` or `response_scheduling` on at least one tool.

**So option three is a change to the tool, not to the voice layer.** No amount of prompt engineering
on the speaking side produces it, because the speaking side has nothing to speak from. If someone
asks for informative filler and is not willing to change the tools, they are asking for option two
with a bigger vocabulary, which is option two's failure mode rather than option three.

In the story, option three is the guard walking through the carriage with an actual reason from
control. Getting that sentence onto the tannoy is not a matter of the announcer choosing better
words. It is a matter of the control room telling them.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because the hub's build brief has this as a `TODO(me)` — *decide what the caller hears while a tool
runs, and write down why* — and because `sutra/livetools.py` is going to hold a lookup against
somebody else's system, which is the exact tool that is slow and the exact tool that fails.

There is a Sutra-shaped trap in it too. The desk already speaks fluently. Handing a fluent model an
open stretch of line and no instruction is not choosing silence; it is choosing whatever the model
produces to fill a gap, which will be grammatical, warm and about the lookup. The desk that says
*"just pulling up your order now"* while `check_order` is raising a connection error is not
malfunctioning. It is doing what an unconstrained speaker does with a pause.

And the desk is the same desk from Day 72. If it invents a vendor status after four failed retries it
is a fabrication, and everyone agrees. If it says *"I'm checking with the vendor now"* while nothing
is checking, most reviewers will not even flag it, because it sounds like manners rather than like an
answer. It is the same fabrication with better manners on it.

## The mechanism

There is no run in this part, so what stands in for one is the number the whole design hangs on.
Here it is from `lab/_live.py`, quoted exactly as it is on disk — it already sits at column 0 in the
file, so nothing has been dedented:

```python
# How long the lookup takes. A real one waits on a warehouse system; this one waits on a clock, and
# the number is small enough that the day's runs finish and large enough that a caller would notice.
LOOKUP_SECONDS = 0.5
```

**Line by line:**

- `LOOKUP_SECONDS` is a module-level constant rather than an argument, because the whole lab is built
  around varying it and watching what changes. Section 1 varied *how* the tool waits; this part is
  about *how long*.
- `0.5` — half a second. Read the comment's second clause carefully: *large enough that a caller
  would notice*. Half a second of dead air in a typed chat is invisible. Half a second of dead air in
  a spoken conversation is a pause somebody hears, and half a second is at the very bottom of the
  range a real lookup lives in.
- The comment names what the number stands for — *a real one waits on a warehouse system* — and that
  is the point. The lab waits on a clock so the day costs no quota, but the design input is the real
  system's latency, not this constant.
- **This is the number nobody measures before building the voice layer.** All three options above are
  decided by it. If the wait is short, option one is right and anything else is noise the caller has
  to sit through. If it is long, option one is unshippable. If it is long *and variable*, option two
  is not enough on its own and you are into option three, which means changing the tool. Teams
  routinely write the filler first, from taste, and then discover the wait — which is the wrong order,
  because the wait is the input and the filler is the output.
- The constant is also the only honest place to start an argument about this. "How long does
  `check_order` actually take, at the median and at the bad end?" is a question with an answer, and
  nobody can design filler without it.

Now the failure this part exists for.

🎬 **The scene.** The desk is live. A caller reads out an order reference, the model asks for
`check_order`, and the runtime starts the lookup. The voice layer, correctly, does not want to leave
the line silent — part 1.3 has already convinced everybody that a quiet line is a frightening line —
so it emits a sentence the moment the tool call goes out: *"I'm just checking that for you now."*
Meanwhile the warehouse system refuses the connection, and the tool raises.

😬 **The naive fix.** A reviewer reads the transcript and says the filler is too thin. It sounds
robotic; it is the same seven words every time. Make it more informative — let the model generate it
from what it knows, mention the order number, mention the warehouse system, make it sound like
somebody actually doing something.

💥 **Why it fails.** Every increment of specificity is an increment of claim, and the claim is being
made *before* the result exists. *"Just checking"* is false when nothing is checking. *"Pulling up
order A17 in the warehouse system now"* is false in three separate ways at once when the connection
was refused. The more informative the filler, the bigger the lie in the case that matters, and the
case that matters is precisely the case where the tool fails — which is the case nobody writes the
filler for, because they are writing it while imagining the tool working.

💡 **The insight.** Filler is generated before the result is known, so the only sentences safe to put
in it are the sentences that are true regardless of the result. That is not a style rule; it is the
same rule Day 72 part
[3.1](../../../day-72-backoff-with-honesty/parts/03-after-the-last-retry/3.1-escalate-never-invent.md)
arrived at, moved one step earlier in time. That part banned returning *'no incidents reported'* after
a retry loop had failed to reach the provider — a plausible answer produced when nothing was
measured. This is the same shape, produced while nobody has reached anything **yet**. There the
fabrication filled a gap where a result should have been; here it fills a gap where a result is not
due for another half-second. The gap is different. The rule is the same: a sentence that asserts
something nothing observed is a fabrication, whatever tone it was said in.

Set the four candidates against each other. The last column is the one that decides the design:

| What the caller hears | What it claims | If the tool has already failed | Can it be said before the result is known? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| nothing at all | nothing | nothing false is said, but an open line and a dropped one sound the same | yes |
| *"Bear with me a moment."* | somebody is still on the line | still true; it never mentioned the lookup | yes |
| *"I'm just checking that for you."* | a lookup is running and will return | false — nothing is checking | yes, and that is the problem |
| *"The warehouse system is slow; still waiting."* | a fact about a named system's state | false and specific, which is worse than false and vague | **only if the tool reports progress** |

Rows two and three are one word apart in tone and on opposite sides of Principle 10. Row four is the
sentence everybody wants, and its cell in the last column is a work item on the tool rather than on
the voice layer.

### The second failure, which is quieter

There is a subtler version of this, and it deserves its own paragraph because it survives getting the
sentence right.

Ask where the filler goes *after* it has been said. Day 75 part
[2.3](../../../day-75-the-bidi-voice-loop/parts/02-talking-over/2.3-what-the-client-does.md)
established that on a live loop, what was **produced**, what was **heard** and what is **stored** are
three different facts that a text interface collapses into one. Filler makes that split matter in a
new way. If the filler is written into the conversation's history as an assistant turn, then the next
turn is prompted with a record in which the assistant has already claimed to be checking something.
A model reading that record has every reason to build on it — to refer back to the check, to treat it
as an established step, to answer as though a lookup happened. The sentence was emitted as a piece of
social lubrication and stored as a statement of fact, and a later turn cannot tell the difference. If
the tool then failed, the desk is now reasoning from a history that says it was checking, which is
the fabrication laundered into context and much harder to spot than the original.

So filler carries two decisions, not one: what it says, and whether it enters the transcript. The
second has no default worth inheriting, exactly as Day 75 part 2.3 found for an abandoned answer.

## When it breaks

**Filler that outlives the reason for it.** The wait ends early — the tool returns in a fraction of
the expected time — and the filler is already on its way to the speaker. The caller hears *"one
moment"* and then, immediately, the answer, which is harmless but silly. Worse is the reverse: the
tool takes far longer than the filler covers, and the line goes quiet again after the reassurance,
which is a worse experience than never having spoken, because now the caller has been told somebody
is there and then abandoned. Filler needs a length, and the length comes from the wait's
distribution, not from its median.

**Filler that collides with the caller.** The caller is entitled to speak during the wait — that is
the whole of part 1.3 — and filler is the desk speaking during the same wait. Two speakers, one line.
Whether the filler gets cut off, whether the caller's voice interrupts it, and whether either of them
is treated as the end of a turn are all decided by settings that section 3 is about, starting at
[3.1](../03-deciding-when-they-stopped/3.1-the-knobs.md). Filler is not a free action on an open
line; it is a turn, and it competes.

**Filler that says the same thing every time.** Three lookups in one call produce *"bear with me"*
three times, and by the third the caller has learnt that the sentence carries no information. This is
a real cost of option two and it is the honest price of it: a sentence that is true regardless is
also a sentence that never tells you anything. The fix is not to make it more specific — that is the
naive fix above, and it fails — the fix is to make the wait shorter or the tool talkative.

**And everything in this part is reasoning, not a run.** No output block appears above because none
was produced. Nothing in this lab speaks, nothing in this lab emits filler, and no measurement here
establishes how long a caller tolerates silence or which sentence they prefer. Those are real
questions with real answers, obtained by putting a system in front of people and listening. The
`TODO(me)` in *Check yourself* is the smallest step towards the one number this part actually needs.

## In production

**A professional writes the filler as fixed, reviewed content and does not generate it.** The set of
sentences the desk may say during a wait is a short list in the codebase, written once, read by
somebody who is allowed to say no, and unit-tested against one question: *is this still true if the
tool fails?* Generating filler puts the one category of utterance that must never make a claim into
the hands of the one component whose job is making claims. It is also cheaper: a fixed string costs
nothing, needs no round trip, and cannot arrive late — and a filler that arrives late has failed at
the only thing it was for.

**A professional decides, explicitly, whether filler enters the transcript**, writes the decision in
the docstring next to the reason, and tests it. The default — whatever your framework happens to do
with an emitted turn — is not a decision. See the second failure above for what it costs when it goes
the wrong way silently.

**And a professional ties the filler to a measured wait rather than to a guess.** Before writing a
single sentence: instrument the tool, get the median and the bad end of its latency, and only then
decide whether you need filler at all. Most teams do this in the opposite order and end up with
filler covering a wait that does not exist, or a silence covering one that does.

**Filler is one of the few places in a voice product where the writing matters more than the
engineering.** The code is a string constant and an `if`. The hard part is a sentence that is warm,
short, natural to hear twice in a row, and asserts nothing — and no amount of framework knowledge
produces it. This is worth saying out loud because engineers tend to route around it: faced with a
writing problem, the instinct is to make it a generation problem, and generation is exactly what
turns a safe sentence into a claim. The correct move is to write four sentences by hand and have
somebody read them aloud.

**What changes at ten thousand calls.** A fixed filler string is said tens of thousands of times a
day, which makes it one of the most-heard sentences your product owns — worth more review than most
of the answers. It also becomes measurable in a way one call never is: the rate at which callers
speak *over* the filler tells you it is too long or too frequent, and the rate at which they hang up
during a wait tells you what the silence is actually costing. Both of those are metrics on the voice
layer, and neither exists until somebody decides filler is a feature rather than a courtesy. Part
[4.2](../04-in-production/4.2-what-a-real-one-adds.md) covers the rest of what a real one carries
around a slow tool, starting with the timeout that decides how long the wait is allowed to be at all.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"The filler is generated, and it's emitted at the
same moment we dispatch the tool call — so it goes out before we know anything, including whether the
lookup is going to fail. Right now it says 'I'm just checking that for you', which is a claim about a
lookup that at that instant may already have raised. Make it a fixed string, and hold it to one test:
is it still true if the tool throws? 'Bear with me' passes, 'just checking' doesn't. If product wants
the informative version, that's a change to the tool — it has to report progress — not a change to
the prompt, and we should say that plainly rather than trying to write our way to it. Second thing:
tell me whether this goes into the session history. If it does, the next turn is prompted with us
having said we checked something, and the model will build on that. Pick a branch, put it in the
docstring, write the test. And somebody should get me the p50 and p99 on `check_order`, because we're
designing a wait we've never measured."*

**The interview question:** *"Your voice agent has to look something up mid-call. What does the
caller hear while it does?"* An honest answer: *"There are three options and only three. Say nothing,
which is free and can never be wrong, but on a voice line silence is ambiguous — a quiet line and a
dropped line sound the same, so past a certain length it stops being acceptable. Say something that's
true regardless of the outcome: 'bear with me', which claims only that somebody's still on the line,
and that stays true whether the lookup succeeds or blows up. Or say something specific and true,
which is the one everyone wants and the one you usually can't have, because it requires the tool to
actually report progress — most tools are a single call with no inside, so that's a change to the
tool, not to the voice layer. The failure I'd flag in a review is the middle ground: filler generated
before the result arrives and phrased as though it knows something. 'I'm just checking that for you'
is content, and if the lookup has already failed it's a false claim — same rule as never inventing an
answer after your retries are exhausted, just moved earlier in time. The instinct to make filler more
informative makes it worse, because a more specific claim is a bigger lie in the failure case. So:
fixed strings, reviewed, tested against 'is this still true if the tool throws'. And I'd want a
decision on whether the filler goes into the conversation history, because if it does, the next turn
is prompted with the model having claimed to check something it may never have checked."*

# 4.1 — The silence you cannot see

## One-line answer

A frozen line raises nothing, ends cleanly and gives the caller the right answer — its only trace is
a log **four lines shorter than it should have been** — so the signal cannot be an error rate or a
failure count; it has to be the **gap between the things that were supposed to be arriving**.

## The story

There is a class you go to on a Tuesday, and there is a woman who always sits at the end of the
second row. She has been there every week since the first one. You do not know her surname. You have
talked about the weather and about how bad the car park is.

One Tuesday she is not there. You think nothing of it, because people miss a week. The next Tuesday
she is not there either, and you think nothing of that, because by now you are not certain she was
there the week before. Somewhere around the fifth Tuesday she quietly stops being somebody who is
missing and becomes somebody who used to come.

Nothing ever happened. There was no evening on which anybody said anything: no message, no apology,
no argument, nothing to repeat to the person next to you. If she had turned up and been upset, the
whole room would have known at once and been talking about it for a month. What actually happened
produced nothing at all.

And the only place it was ever visible was a pattern nobody was writing down — who was in the room,
week after week. Somebody keeping that list would have seen it on the second Tuesday. Nobody was
keeping it, because a list of people who turned up looks like the most pointless piece of paper in
the building, right up until the interesting fact is who stopped.

## The idea in plain language

Part [1.3](../01-the-tool-in-the-middle/1.3-the-line-that-goes-dead.md) ran the same voice session
twice, changing one word inside the tool. The version that used `await` delivered **4 slices** of the
caller's audio to the model's connection while the lookup was running. The version that used
`time.sleep` delivered **0**.

Now look at what the broken run gave you to work with. It ended cleanly. It raised nothing. The tool
returned the correct order status and the model told the caller their order had shipped. The only
difference visible anywhere was that the connection's log had **five lines instead of nine** — the
four audio arrivals were not late, not reordered, not marked. They were simply not there.

That is the whole problem in one sentence: **the failure removed evidence rather than producing
any.** Every monitoring habit most of us have is built on the opposite assumption — that a bad thing
generates a record, and the job is to count records.

Two words for the rest of this part, both defined here because the whole argument is made of them.

An **inbound event** is anything that arrives at the session from the caller's side: a slice of
microphone audio, a text part, an activity marker saying speech started or ended. In the lab these
are the `caller audio arrives` lines in the connection's log.

A **gap metric** is a number measured on the *space between* those arrivals rather than on the
arrivals themselves — how long since the last one, and how long the longest silence was. Counting
inbound events tells you a session was busy. Measuring the gaps tells you when it stopped being.

With those two words, here is every signal you might reach for, and whether it moves when the line
freezes:

| Candidate signal | Does it move? | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| error rate | no | nothing failed; there is no error to have a rate of |
| exception count | no | nothing was raised, so nothing was caught, so nothing was counted |
| session success rate | no | the session completed and the caller got a correct answer |
| tool call duration | yes — and also for healthy tools | a slow lookup and a frozen line look identical here |
| largest gap between inbound events | **yes, and only for this** | the hole *is* the failure, measured directly |

The first three do not move **at all**. Not slightly, not with a delay — they are structurally
incapable of moving, because each of them counts a kind of record that this failure never creates.

The fourth is the tempting one, and it is worth being precise about why it is not enough. Tool
duration does rise when a tool blocks the loop, but it rises exactly as much when a tool is slow and
perfectly well behaved. A warehouse lookup that takes a while and yields properly and a warehouse
lookup that takes a while and holds the thread produce the same number. Alerting on it means alerting
on slowness, and slowness is not the thing that hurt the caller.

Only the last row separates them, because it is measured on the caller's stream rather than on the
tool's.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because the build brief in the hub's §4 hands you `sutra/livetools.py` and asks for tools that are
`async def` **and actually await**, and part 1.3 was honest that the gate can only lint for the easy
case. A blocking database driver inside an async tool passes every check in the repository. So the
question this part has to answer is what catches it *after* it ships, and the answer cannot be
anything the session emits when it goes wrong, because it does not emit anything.

But there is a larger reason, and it is the real point of this part.

**This is the third time this curriculum has met the same shape**, in three different phases, with
three different mechanisms:

| Where | What fails | What the system emits about it | What you have to measure instead |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Day 72 part 5.2 | a provider degrades and retries absorb it | nothing — every call still succeeds | escalations and wasted requests |
| Day 73 part 3.3 | a nightly job stops being triggered | nothing — yesterday's files, unchanged | the age of the newest artefact |
| Day 76 part 4.1 | a tool blocks and the line stops listening | nothing — a shorter log and a clean exit | the gap between inbound events |

Read the third column. It is the same column three times, and it is empty three times.

Say the shape out loud, because recognising it is worth more than any of the three metrics
individually: **in ambient and live systems, the failures that matter are absences, and an absence
has to be measured on purpose, because it never arrives.** A system reports what it did. No system
reports what it stopped doing, because the code that would have reported it is inside the thing that
stopped.

Day 72 found this at the level of one call, Day 73 at the level of one process, and today at the
level of one open connection. Once you can see the shape, the design move is automatic and always the
same: find the thing that should be arriving steadily, and put a number on the space between arrivals
before you ship.

## The mechanism

The lab already contains the detector, and it is worth looking at precisely because it is so small.
This is from `freeze.py`, quoted exactly as it is on disk except that the common leading indentation
has been removed so it starts at column 0:

```python
during = [
    (t, what)
    for t, what in log
    if what == "caller audio arrives"
    and asked is not None
    and came_back is not None
    and asked <= t <= came_back
]
```

**Line by line:**

- This is the entire detector. Six lines. Everything the day claims about a frozen line comes out of
  this list being empty, and there is no library, no agent and no model involved in deciding it.
- `for t, what in log` walks the connection's own record, which is a list of `(seconds since the
  connection opened, what happened)` pairs. The timestamp is what turns "during the tool call" from
  an impression into a comparison.
- `if what == "caller audio arrives"` narrows the stream to inbound events only. Everything else in
  the log — the model asking for the tool, the result going back, the hang-up — is the window, not
  the thing being measured.
- `asked is not None and came_back is not None` looks defensive and is load-bearing. `asked` and
  `came_back` are found with `next(..., None)` over the same log, so a run that fell over before the
  model asked for a tool leaves them as `None`. Without these two clauses that run would report *no
  audio arrived during the tool call* — a perfect description of a freeze, produced by a session that
  never called a tool at all. **A missing window is not an empty window**, and collapsing the two
  turns a broken measurement into a confident wrong answer, which is the one outcome Principle 10
  refuses.
- `asked <= t <= came_back` is inclusive at both ends, and that is a judgement rather than an obvious
  truth. A slice that arrives at the same instant the tool result comes back counts as having arrived
  during the call. Choose the other convention and a run that delivered exactly one boundary slice
  reports zero. Boundary rules like this decide small counts, and small counts are what alerts fire
  on, so the rule deserves to be a decision somebody made rather than whichever comparison got typed.
- **And here is the part that transfers.** In the lab you are handed `asked` and `came_back`, because
  the connection recorded them. In production nobody hands you the window: there is no line in the
  log saying *the freeze starts here*. The production equivalent of this comprehension is therefore
  not this comprehension — it is a **timeout on the space between inbound events**: remember when the
  last one arrived, and treat "nothing has arrived since" as the event. Same idea, opposite
  implementation. The lab knows the window and counts arrivals inside it; production knows the
  arrivals and infers the window from the hole.

The two runs behind the figures above are part 1.3's, and it owns both transcripts:

```bash
cd days/day-76-vad-and-non-blocking-tools/lab
uv run python freeze.py; echo "exit: $?"
uv run python freeze.py --blocking; echo "exit: $?"
```

**Line by line:**

- The first exits `0` and the second exits `1`, and the exit code is the only difference either run
  reports to anything outside itself. Read them in part 1.3 rather than here; this part is about what
  you would have had if `freeze.py` had not been watching on purpose.
- Notice what the exit code *is*: a person deliberately deciding that an empty list means failure.
  Nothing in the runtime made that call. A voice server has no equivalent line unless somebody writes
  one.

What the lab does not yet do is measure the gap, which is the version you would actually deploy:

  exercise: it has **no consecutive pairs to measure**, because no inbound events arrived at all. A
  gap metric computed only from arrivals is silent in exactly the case it exists for, so the measure
  has to be *time since the last arrival, evaluated continuously*, not *the largest difference in a
  list you have finished collecting*.
- Both arms in one command, because a single number from the healthy arm proves nothing. The claim is
  a comparison, and part 1.3's ablation switch is what makes it one.

## When it breaks

**The threshold is a guess, and it is the same guess as section 3's.** A gap metric is not a signal
until you say how large a gap counts as bad, and there is no correct answer available in advance. Set
it tight and a caller who stops talking to listen to the answer — which is the normal, healthy shape
of a conversation — trips it. Set it loose and a short freeze happens entirely inside the threshold
and is never seen. That is the identical trade part
[3.2](../03-deciding-when-they-stopped/3.2-sensitivity-is-a-trade.md) makes about end-of-speech
sensitivity: cutting people off and never letting them finish are one dial from two ends. Being
honest that your monitoring has a sensitivity dial of its own, tuned by the same guess-and-observe
process, is better than presenting a threshold as though it were derived.

**This day only ever measured one direction.** Everything above is about audio going *in*. A blocked
loop stops the outbound half too — the model's own words stop being delivered to the caller — and
nothing in this lab measured that, because there is no speaker in it. It is the same cause and the
same cure, and it is a second stream with its own gaps, which means a real endpoint has two gap
metrics and not one. Part 1.3 named this limit and this part inherits it rather than quietly
inheriting the impression that inbound is the whole story.

**A gap in inbound audio has innocent explanations, and most of them are the common case.** The
caller is listening. The caller pressed mute. The caller is in a lift and the network dropped
packets. Every one of those produces the same hole as a blocked event loop, so the metric is a
**symptom, not a diagnosis** — it tells you the line went quiet and says nothing about why. Which is
precisely why the next section is about correlating it with what the process was doing, rather than
about alerting on it alone.

## In production

**What a professional actually instruments**, and it is three things rather than one:

| What to record | Per what | What it answers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| count of inbound events | session | was this line ever being listened to |
| largest gap between inbound events | session | did it stop, and for how long |
| duration of each tool call, with its name | tool call | which lookup was in flight while it stopped |

The first two are the gap metric and its denominator. Recording only the largest gap gives you a
frightening number for a session with two inbound events in it, which is a quiet caller and not a
frozen one; the count is what stops you paging somebody about a polite silence.

The third is recorded **separately** and then **correlated**, and that separation is the design. On
its own, tool duration cannot distinguish a slow tool from a blocking one, and a gap cannot
distinguish a freeze from a mute. Put side by side, they can: a hole in the inbound stream with a
tool call sitting inside it is the failure this day is about, and a hole with nothing running in it
is a caller who stopped talking. Neither number is the alert. The overlap is.

**All three of those are cheap, and almost nobody has them until after the first incident.** They are
counters on a stream the server is already reading, and none of them needs a new dependency or a
sampling decision. The reason they are usually missing is not cost. It is that a dashboard gets built
out of the things a system already emits, and this system emits nothing about a hole — so the metric
has to be added by somebody who thought about absences before one happened. Every organisation that
has these adds them the week after the incident that made them obvious, which means the only real
question is whether you get to skip that week.

**What changes at scale.** One session's largest gap is a diagnostic; ten thousand sessions' gaps are
a distribution, and the useful alert is on the shape of the tail rather than on any one session,
because individual callers go quiet constantly. And since part 1.3 established that a blocked loop
freezes *every* caller on that worker, the tell at scale is specific and recognisable: several
sessions on one worker holing at the same instant, while the identical sessions on every other worker
carry on. That correlation is invisible in a per-session view and obvious the moment gaps are grouped
by worker.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"There's nothing in here that would tell us this
had happened. The session ends cleanly, the tool returns, the caller gets a right answer, and our
error rate never moves — so please add two counters before this goes out: inbound events per session,
and the largest gap between them. Emit the tool call's own duration next to them with the tool name
on it, because a gap on its own could just be someone listening, and a gap with a lookup inside it is
the bug. Don't alert on either one alone. And pick the gap threshold deliberately and write down why
you picked it — it's the same trade as the end-of-speech setting and it'll be wrong in one direction
or the other, so I'd rather it was wrong on purpose."*

**The interview question:** *"Your voice agent freezes mid-call. What fires?"* An honest answer:
*"Nothing, and that's the interesting part. I measured it: with the tool awaiting properly, four
slices of the caller's audio reached the model's connection during the lookup; with the identical
tool using a blocking sleep, none did. Both sessions ended cleanly, neither raised anything, and the
caller got the correct answer either way. The only trace was that the connection's log was four lines
shorter than it should have been. So error rate, exception count and session success rate are all
structurally incapable of moving — they count records, and this failure deletes records instead of
making them. Tool duration does move, but it moves the same amount for a tool that's just slow, so it
prompts an investigation rather than answering anything. What actually works is a gap metric: the
time between inbound events, with the tool call's duration recorded alongside it so a hole with a
lookup in it is distinguishable from a caller who's simply listening. And I'd say the wider thing
first if I had one sentence — this was the third time on this project that the failure worth catching
produced no output at all. A working retry policy hides a degrading provider; a scheduled job that
stops being triggered leaves yesterday's files; a blocked event loop leaves a hole in a stream. In
ambient and live systems the failures that matter are absences, and an absence has to be measured on
purpose, because it never arrives on its own. Recognising that shape is worth more than any one of
those three metrics."*

# 3.2 — Sensitivity is a trade

## One-line answer

End-of-speech sensitivity and silence duration are **one dial with a bad outcome at each end** — turn
it one way and the system decides you have finished while you are drawing breath, turn it the other
and it sits there in silence after you actually have — and no value avoids both, so every setting is
a choice about which of the two complaints you would rather receive.

## The story

There is a security light on the side of a house, and a small dial on the back of it.

The people who moved in turned the dial up, because the point of the light is to come on when
somebody is there. And it does. It also comes on for the neighbour's cat, for the branch of the
buddleia when the wind gets up, and for a moth at three in the morning, so the bedroom curtain glows
on and off all night and the neighbour opposite asks, politely, whether something is wrong with it.

So they turned the dial down. Peace. Then one wet evening somebody comes home with both arms full of
shopping bags, stands on the step in the dark, and the light does not come on. They shuffle sideways.
They wave an elbow. Nothing. They find the keyhole by feel, and the next morning somebody says the
light is broken.

The light is not broken, and it never was. There is one dial, there is a nuisance at each end of it,
and every position on it is somebody's opinion about which nuisance is easier to live with. And here
is the part that matters more than the dial: the right answer is different for the light over the
bins by the road and the light over the back steps, because different things walk past them.

## The idea in plain language

**First, plainly: this part measures nothing.** Part [3.1](3.1-the-knobs.md) did the honest half of
this subject. It read the voice-activity fields off the installed package and pasted what the package
said, and that output belongs to 3.1 — it is not repeated here. Nothing on this day has applied any
of those fields to a model or watched a caller's speech being cut into turns, because the live model
is parked (Day 74 part 4.2 is where that decision was made and why). So there are no defaults in this
document, no ranges, no recommended values and no benchmark. What follows is reasoning about a trade
that falls out of **what the fields are named and what those names denote**, plus ordinary knowledge
of how people talk. Where a sentence sounds like documented behaviour, it is not: it is an argument,
and the moment you have real callers you should trust them over it.

Here is what 3.1 established, and all this part assumes. **Voice activity detection** — VAD — is the
job of deciding, from the sound arriving on the line, when somebody started speaking and when they
stopped. The second half of that job has a name of its own: **endpointing**, finding the end of an
utterance. Four of the fields on `AutomaticActivityDetection` are about how that decision is made:

| Field | What its name denotes |
| --- | --- |
| `start_of_speech_sensitivity` | how readily the sound arriving is taken to be somebody starting to speak |
| `end_of_speech_sensitivity` | how readily a quiet stretch is taken to be somebody having finished |
| `prefix_padding_ms` | an amount of audio, in milliseconds, kept from before the detected start |
| `silence_duration_ms` | an amount of silence, in milliseconds, before that silence counts as the end |

The two sensitivities are enumerations, and 3.1 printed their members: each offers `HIGH`, `LOW` and
an unspecified value. The two `_ms` fields are amounts of time in milliseconds. That is the whole of
what is asserted here, and it is all read off the package rather than deduced.

Now the reasoning. A pause in speech and the end of speech produce **the same thing on the wire**:
sound, then no sound. There is nothing in the silence that says which one it is, because the
difference is not in the audio at all — it is in what the person meant to do next. So any setting has
to guess, and a guess has two ways to be wrong.

### 1. Declaring the end of speech too readily

The caller says *"the reference is J, K, four…"* and stops, because they are moving their thumb down
the card to find the next character. That silence is not the end of anything. It is the middle of a
sentence with a person thinking in it.

Turn the dial towards *declare the end quickly* — a high readiness to call it finished, a short
required silence — and the system takes the floor there. It answers half a reference number. The
caller, who had not finished, now talks over the answer, and whatever they say next arrives on top of
speech the system is already producing.

This is precisely the thing Day 75's paper on the organisation of turn-taking in conversation is
about. Its central observation is that people do not take turns by waiting for silence; they take
turns at points where the current turn *could* be complete — and a place where a turn could end is
not a place where it did. A pause at one of those points is an invitation, not a full stop, and
reading it as a full stop is the same error a person makes when they cut in and then have to say
sorry. The paper is on the curriculum because this failure is old, well described, and not a bug in
anybody's audio code.

The caller's word for this is **"it cuts me off"**, and they are not describing a threshold.

### 2. Declaring the end of speech too reluctantly

Turn the dial the other way — a low readiness to call it finished, a long required silence — and the
caller stops talking and nothing happens. They wait. They say *"hello?"* Or, far more often, they
**repeat themselves**, usually louder and usually in different words.

That repetition is the expensive part, and it is worth being precise about why. The system does not
now have one utterance to answer. It has two, spoken over a gap, and depending on where the endpoint
finally lands it may hear them as one long confused turn — *"account four two nine hello can you hear
me account four two nine"* — which is a worse input than either half. A wait does not merely delay
the answer; it changes what was said.

There is a temptation to file this as the safe end of the dial, and it is not quite. **A caller
judges this failure more harshly than the damage deserves.** Being interrupted is annoying, but it is
legible: something is there, it is listening, it jumped in. Silence is illegible, and people fill
illegible silence with the worst available explanation. The interruption gets described as rude. The
wait gets described as *broken* — and "it's broken" is a verdict on the whole product, delivered
after one call.

### The two failures, side by side

| | Cut off too early | Left waiting too long |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What the caller does | pauses mid-sentence — to think, to breathe, to read the next digits off a card | finishes their sentence and stops |
| What the system concludes | they have finished; take the floor | they have not finished; keep listening |
| What the caller experiences | being talked over, then having to start again | dead air, then repeating themselves louder |
| Which setting moves it | `end_of_speech_sensitivity` towards `LOW`; a longer `silence_duration_ms` | `end_of_speech_sensitivity` towards `HIGH`; a shorter `silence_duration_ms` |

Read the last row as one row rather than two cells. **Every move that fixes the left column makes the
right column more likely, and the reverse.** That is what makes this a dial and not a bug list.

### 3. `prefix_padding_ms` — the start of a word is quiet

The other end of the utterance has a smaller version of the same problem, and it has its own field.

Speech does not begin loudly. A word starting with *f*, *s*, *th* or *h* opens with a stretch of
breathy noise that carries very little energy, and anything deciding "speech has started" from
loudness alone will decide it a fraction late — after that opening has already gone past. The whole
paper at the end of this day is about exactly this problem, and it is worth reading afterwards rather
than now: [the endpoint-detection paper](../../papers/01-endpoint-detection.md) is the argument for
why a system that guesses the edges of a word from energy alone clips the quiet parts off both ends.

`prefix_padding_ms` is the blunt, practical answer: keep some audio from *before* the moment the
start was detected, so that the quiet opening is still there when the utterance is assembled. Its
name is an amount of milliseconds of padding on the front, and that is all this document claims about
it.

And it is a trade too, in the same shape as the others. Too little padding and the first sound of the
first word is missing, which is how *"Smith"* becomes *"mith"* and a spelled-out reference loses its
first character. Too much and you have deliberately included audio from before anybody was speaking:
the room, the road outside, the television, the end of whatever the caller was saying to somebody
else in the room before they turned back to the phone. Padding does not know the difference between a
quiet *s* and a quiet everything else.

### 4. `silence_duration_ms` — the dial in its plainest form

`end_of_speech_sensitivity` is the dial with a label on it — `HIGH`, `LOW`, unspecified. But
`silence_duration_ms` is the dial with a number on it, and it is the easiest one to reason about
because it states the rule in words anyone can check: *silence counts as the end of speech once it
has lasted this long.*

Everything above is contained in that one sentence. Make the duration short and you have promised to
treat a thinking pause as an ending. Make it long and you have promised to make every caller wait
after every sentence, including the ones who finished crisply and are now looking at their phone
wondering if the call dropped. There is no duration that is only one of those, because the same
silence is produced by both situations.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because the desk is going to be answering to somebody, and this is the first setting in this
repository where **the correct value is not a fact about the code**.

Everything Sutra has configured so far had a defensible right answer available from engineering: a
retry policy follows from the provider's quota, a timeout follows from what the call actually costs,
a model string follows from the free roster. This one does not. Two competent people looking at the
same session, the same package and the same measurements can reasonably choose opposite ends of this
dial, because they are picturing different callers.

And the hub says so out loud. Trap 8 is *"tuning sensitivity in one direction"* — arriving with one
complaint in hand ("it cuts me off"), turning the dial until that complaint stops, and shipping
without ever having asked what the move cost at the other end. It is the single most common way this
setting goes wrong, and it goes wrong slowly: the first complaint disappears immediately and the
second one takes a while to arrive, by which time nobody connects the two.

## The mechanism

There is no new mechanism in this part. The fields are 3.1's; the mechanism here is *where they are
attached*, because that is what decides how finely you are allowed to choose.

From `lab/vadconfig.py`, the two lines that print the `RunConfig` fields — dedented to column 0 from
inside `main()`, and otherwise exactly as on disk:

```python
for name in ("realtime_input_config", "explicit_vad_signal"):
    field = RunConfig.model_fields[name]
    print(f"    {name:<24} {field.annotation}")
```

**Line by line:**

- `RunConfig` is the point of the snippet. These settings arrive attached to a **run** — one session,
  one connection, one caller — and not to the agent definition. That is not a detail: it is the only
  reason per-flow values are possible at all. If voice activity detection were a property of the
  agent, every caller would get one dial position, and the argument in the next section would be
  academic.
- `RunConfig.model_fields[name]` is a plain dictionary lookup on the model's declared fields, and on
  a version of the package that renamed or removed either field it raises `KeyError`. That is the
  correct behaviour here rather than a rough edge. This script's entire job is to tell you the truth
  about what is installed, and a script whose job is truth must not have a fallback that lets it
  print a comforting line about a field that is not there — Principle 10 in three lines of code.
- `field.annotation` prints the declared type rather than a value, which is why 3.1's output is a
  census and not a configuration. Nothing here sets anything.
- Both annotations, as 3.1's output shows, are `Optional` — the fields may be `None`. **A run that
  sets neither still has behaviour.** Not setting the dial is not the same as there being no dial; it
  is accepting whatever position the far end ships with, chosen by people who had never heard of your
  callers. Part 2.2 made this argument about `response_scheduling` and it is the same argument here.

**Which brings the story back.** The dial is on the run, so you may have a different one for the
light over the bins and the light over the back steps — and you should, because the thing that walks
past them is different:

- A flow that **takes a reference code, a postcode, a card number or an address** must sit at the
  patient end. Those are read out with pauses in the middle of them, every time, by everybody: people
  group digits, they check the card, they say *"sorry, that's a five"*. A system that takes the floor
  after four characters cannot complete this task at all, no matter how quick it feels.
- A flow that **answers a short question in a noisy place** — a caller in a corridor, a shop, a car —
  can afford to cut in occasionally. The utterances are short, they are cheap to repeat, and here
  slowness is the expensive failure, because a hesitant assistant in a hurry reads as a broken one.

So **the setting belongs to the flow, not to the product**. A single global value across an assistant
that does both of those things is not a decision that came out well or badly; it is a compromise
before anyone has even started, and it will be wrong at both ends at once — slightly too eager for
the address, slightly too slow for the quick question, and defensible for neither.

## When it breaks

**Everything above is an argument, and arguments are not measurements.** This document has not
observed a single endpoint decision. The fields are real and the names are read off the package, but
what any particular value actually *does* is the behaviour of the model on the other end of the
connection, not of the framework you set it in — and that behaviour can change under you when the
provider changes a model. Treat this part as a way to know what you are looking at when you finally
tune against real callers, and treat the callers as the evidence.

**Tuning on yourself produces a setting that is wrong for everybody else.** This is the failure worth
naming loudest, because it feels like diligence. You put the headset on, you speak into a quiet room,
you say the sentence you already know you are going to say, and you turn the dial until it feels
right. Every one of those is unrepresentative: you do not hesitate, because you are not thinking
about the answer; you have no background noise; your microphone is good and close; and you already
know the reference number by heart. Real callers hesitate mid-address, hold the phone at an angle,
sit in a kitchen with a radio on, and read the number off a letter they are holding in the other
hand. A dial tuned in a quiet room by somebody who knows the script is tuned for the one caller who
will never phone.

**The same symptom has a completely different cause, and it is the first place to look.** *"It cuts
me off"* is exactly what part [1.3](../01-the-tool-in-the-middle/1.3-the-line-that-goes-dead.md)
produces — a blocking tool freezes the event loop, the caller's audio is not delivered at all while
the lookup runs, and from the caller's side that is indistinguishable from having been declared
finished. The measurement in 1.3 is the discriminator: four slices delivered during the tool call
against zero. **Check that before you touch anything in this part**, because if the cause is a
blocking tool, then turning the sensitivity dial cannot fix it — and worse, if you turn the dial
until the complaint quietens, you have now mistuned voice detection *and* still have the frozen line
underneath, and the second complaint will arrive from the other end of the dial.

**One thing this part deliberately does not do is give you a starting value.** Not out of caution —
because inventing one would be the exact failure this curriculum warns about. A number here would be
copied into somebody's configuration and repeated in a review as though it had come from somewhere.
Read it off the provider's documentation on the day you set it, or start from the default and move,
and write down which you did.

## In production

**Pick per flow, not per product.** The dial lives on the run, so a serious system sets it where the
conversation is: patient values on the flow that collects an address, quicker values on the flow that
answers a one-line question. This is a small amount of work and it is the whole of the benefit
available here.

**Log which value was in force for every session.** This is the part teams skip and the part that
decides whether the setting is ever improvable. A complaint arrives as *"it kept interrupting her"*,
attached to a session id and nothing else. If the session's record does not say what the sensitivity
and the silence duration were on that call, the complaint is unusable: you cannot tell whether the
settings were wrong, whether that caller got a different flow, whether a deploy changed it, or
whether it was part 1.3's blocking tool all along. One line in the session record turns an anecdote
into evidence.

**Change one thing at a time, and note the date.** Sensitivity and silence duration point at the same
outcome. Move both and the next batch of complaints tells you nothing about either.

**This is one of the few settings where the right value is discovered from complaints rather than
from tests**, and it is worth saying plainly because it cuts against everything else in this
repository. You can write a test that a tool is a coroutine function. You cannot write a test that
says a caller felt rushed. Recorded sessions and transcripts help — a transcript full of half-spoken
reference numbers, or full of the same sentence said twice, is a reading of the dial — but the
signal that this setting is wrong is human and it arrives as a report. Which means the machinery that
matters is not a test harness; it is a route by which what a caller said reaches the person who owns
the configuration, with the session's settings attached.

**What changes at scale.** With few callers, both failures look like anecdotes and whoever complained
loudest wins. With many, they separate: cut-off calls and repeated utterances have different
signatures — one produces short truncated turns, the other produces near-duplicate consecutive turns
— and once you can count each of them per flow you can move the dial and watch one number rise as the
other falls. That is the point at which this stops being an argument and becomes an adjustment.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"You've set one silence duration for the whole
assistant, and one of these flows takes a postcode. That flow needs the patient end and the FAQ flow
doesn't — this is a `RunConfig` field, so you can have both. Also, wherever you land, log the value
you used with the session; the next time someone forwards us 'it talked over me' we'll have no idea
what was in force on that call. And before you touch any of this: check whether the tool in that flow
blocks the loop, because a frozen line produces the same complaint and no dial position fixes it."*

**The interview question:** *"Your voice agent keeps cutting callers off. What do you turn down?"* An
honest answer: *"I'd want to know it's actually the endpointing first, because a tool that blocks the
event loop produces exactly the same complaint — the line stops accepting audio while the lookup
runs, and from the caller's side that's identical to being declared finished. Assuming it really is
endpointing: it's end-of-speech sensitivity and the silence duration, and the thing to say about them
is that they're one dial with a bad outcome at each end. Make it less eager and you stop cutting
people off, and you've also promised that every caller who has finished sits in dead air until the
timer runs out — and they'll fill that silence by repeating themselves, so now you've got two
utterances to disentangle instead of one. Neither end is free. Which one you'd rather have is a
property of the flow, not of the system: anything collecting a reference number or an address has to
be patient, because people read those out with pauses in them, and a quick question in a noisy room
can afford to cut in because slowness reads as broken. Those settings are per run, not per agent, so
you don't have to pick once. And I'd log which value was in force on each session, because this is
one of the very few settings you tune from complaints rather than from tests."*

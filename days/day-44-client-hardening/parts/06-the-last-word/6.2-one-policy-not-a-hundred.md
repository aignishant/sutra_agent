# 6.2 — One policy, not a hundred try/excepts

## One-line answer

A retry rule that lives in nineteen call sites is nineteen rules, so the hardening goes in one module
with one entry point and the thing you actually maintain is a **check that no call site bypasses
it** — because the policy will drift and the check will not.

## The story

The building runs a fire drill every year, and the first one after the new tenants moved in was a
shambles.

The second floor filed down the back stairs to the yard, because that is what their old office did.
The third floor went to the front, which is where the reception desk is and where the alarm panel is,
so that felt sensible to them. Two people from the fourth stayed put, having been told once that the
safest thing is to close the door and wait. Somebody's list said the assembly point was the car park
opposite. Nobody was being careless. Every floor was doing the thing that had been correct somewhere
else.

The fix was one laminated card, the same card, screwed up beside the lift on every floor. Stairs at
the back. Assembly in the car park opposite. Wardens are the two people in yellow.

And then — the part that made it stick — the safety officer walks the building every quarter and
checks that the card is on every floor. Not that people remember it. That the card is *there*. The
card is the policy and the walk is the only thing that keeps the policy true, because a card can come
off a wall and a new tenant can paint over it and neither of those things announces itself.

## The idea in plain language

Everything this day has built is a decision: how long to wait, whether to retry, how long to back
off, when to stop asking, what to say at the end. Each decision is small enough that a developer in a
hurry will make it locally, in the call site, with a `try/except` and a number.

That produces a codebase where "what is our timeout?" has no answer. Not a bad answer — no answer.
There are nineteen, they disagree, four of them are zero because somebody wrote `except Exception:
pass`, and finding out requires reading every file.

The fix is structural rather than cultural, and it has two halves that are easy to confuse:

- **One entry point.** Every outward call goes through `with_retries(with_timeout(...))` from
  `sutra/mcp/hardening.py`. There is exactly one place the numbers live and exactly one place the
  rules are written.
- **One check that the entry point is used.** Because the first half is a convention, and
  conventions decay. A new call site added on a Friday will not go through the door unless something
  fails when it does not.

The second half is the part people skip, and it is the only part that survives contact with a team.
Day 40 made exactly this move for tool names — its
[4.1](../../../day-40-filtering-and-allowlists/parts/04-the-policy-module/4.1-one-door-and-a-check-there-is-one.md)
is *one door and a check there is one* — and this is the same argument applied to time instead of
names. Two doors, one for what you may call and one for what a call may cost, each with its own scan.

A scan like this is deliberately stupid. It does not understand Python; it looks for the name of an
outward call and then looks for the name of the policy nearby. It has false positives and false
negatives, and it runs in milliseconds on every commit, which makes it worth more than a clever
analysis that runs never.

## Why Sutra needs it

`sutra/mcp/hardening.py` is being written today, and a module nobody is required to use is a module
that gets used for a fortnight.

Sutra's outward calls are already spread across files and will spread further: `sutra/mcp/client.py`
from Day 33, `sutra/mcp/filtering.py` from Day 40, `sutra/mcp/auth.py` from Day 37, and whatever
Phase 8's graph adds. Two of those days each added a call path, neither knew about today, and both
were correct at the time.

And Day 45 is the phase gate. `tools/mcp_audit.py` will ask whether Sutra's MCP surface is
production-shaped, and *"every outward call has a deadline"* is one of the statements it has to be
able to check rather than assert. This part is the prototype of that check.

## The mechanism

> 🎬 **The scene:** `sutra/mcp/hardening.py` has been merged. The timeouts are configured, the
> backoff is jittered, the breaker works. Three sprints later a customer reports that the desk hangs
> on one particular question.
>
> 😬 **The naive fix:** check the timeout configuration. It is correct. Check the breaker. It is
> closed and healthy.
>
> 💥 **Why it fails:** the hanging call is a `read_resource` added in a small pull request that did
> not go through the helper, because the author was fixing something else and the helper was not in
> the file they were editing. It has no deadline at all, and the policy module is entirely innocent.
>
> 💡 **The insight:** the module is not the control. The check that everything goes through the
> module is the control.

Four call sites, four opinions:

```python
# days/day-44-client-hardening/lab/scattered.py
def triage(session, ticket_id: str):
    """The oldest call site. Somebody meant to come back to it."""
    return session.call_tool("lookup_ticket", {"ticket_id": ticket_id})


def refresh(session):
    """Added in a hurry, with its own private try/except."""
    try:
        return session.list_tools()
    except Exception:  # noqa: BLE001 - swallowed, which is the point
        return []


def article(session, uri: str):
    """The newest one, written by somebody who had read Day 38."""
    for _ in range(3):
        try:
            return session.read_resource(uri)
        except TimeoutError:
            continue
    raise TimeoutError("gave up")
```

**Line by line:**

- `triage` has no deadline and no retry. It is the oldest call site and it is the most common shape in
  any real codebase: correct when written, never revisited.
- `refresh` swallows everything and returns `[]`. An empty tool list is not distinguishable from a
  server that offers no tools, so this failure becomes "the server has no tools" three layers up —
  [6.1](6.1-what-you-say-when-it-all-failed.md)'s shrug, in the wild.
- `article` is the *worst* one, and that is the point of including it. It was written by somebody who
  had read about retries. It retries three times with **no wait at all**, which is
  [3.1](../03-backing-off/3.1-waiting-longer-each-time.md)'s first row — three requests inside one
  millisecond — and no idempotency check, and it discards each original exception so the final
  `TimeoutError("gave up")` carries no cause. Local knowledge produced something more dangerous than
  ignorance did.
- Every one of these passes review in isolation. Nothing here is obviously wrong; it is only wrong
  *as a set*, and no reviewer sees the set.

The same four, through one door:

```python
# days/day-44-client-hardening/lab/one_door.py
def triage(session, ticket_id: str):
    """One call site, one policy, one place to change the numbers."""
    return with_retries(
        lambda: with_timeout(
            lambda: session.call_tool("lookup_ticket", {"ticket_id": ticket_id}),
            1.5,
            label="lookup_ticket",
        ),
        idempotent=True,
    )


def refresh(session):
    """The catalogue is a read, so it may be retried."""
    return with_retries(
        lambda: with_timeout(session.list_tools, 1.5, label="tools/list"),
        idempotent=True,
    )
```

**Line by line:**

- `with_retries(with_timeout(...))` and not the other way round. The order matters and it is worth
  saying why: the deadline applies to **each attempt**, so a retry gets a fresh clock. Wrapping the
  other way — a single timeout around the whole retry loop — would mean the deadline covers all three
  attempts together, which is sometimes what you want and is a different policy. Sutra's choice is a
  per-attempt deadline plus a request-level budget from
  [2.2](../02-the-deadline/2.2-the-deadline-never-reached.md).
- `lambda:` twice, because both helpers take zero-argument callables
  ([2.3](../02-the-deadline/2.3-with-timeout.md) explains why). It is more punctuation than the
  scattered version and that is the honest cost of the door.
- `idempotent=True` is passed **explicitly at every call site**, and it is not defaulted. A default
  of `True` puts writes on the retry path silently; a default of `False` is safer but lets an author
  omit the decision. Requiring it means the reviewer of a new call site is shown the claim.
- `label=` is repeated, which looks redundant next to the tool name in the call. It is not: the label
  is what appears in the error, and the SDK's own message says only `ClientRequest`
  ([2.4](../02-the-deadline/2.4-the-numbers-your-libraries-chose.md)).
- `1.5` is a literal here **only because this is a lab**. In `sutra/mcp/hardening.py` it comes from
  per-tool configuration and is bounded by the request's remaining budget — the hub's build brief
  leaves that as a `TODO(me)`.
- `refresh` passes `session.list_tools` without a lambda, because it already takes no arguments. The
  inconsistency is real and is worth leaving visible: the helper accepts anything callable.

And the check, which is the actual deliverable:

```python
# days/day-44-client-hardening/lab/onepolicy.py
CALL = re.compile(r"\b(call_tool|list_tools|read_resource|get_prompt)\s*\(")
HARDENED = re.compile(r"\bwith_(retries|timeout)\b")


def audit(path: pathlib.Path) -> list[str]:
    """Return one finding per outward call that is not wrapped by the policy."""
    lines = path.read_text(encoding="utf-8").splitlines()
    findings = []
    for number, line in enumerate(lines, start=1):
        if not CALL.search(line):
            continue
        window = "\n".join(lines[max(0, number - 3) : number])
        if not HARDENED.search(window):
            findings.append(f"{path.name}:{number}: unhardened call -> {line.strip()}")
    return findings
```

**Line by line:**

- `CALL` lists the four MCP client methods that reach outside the process. It is a hard-coded list
  and it will go stale, which is why it lives next to the day that owns the rule rather than being
  inferred — a stale list produces a false negative that Day 45's audit is meant to catch.
- `\s*\(` requires the call to be *called*, so `self.call_tool` referenced as a value does not match.
- `window` is the current line plus the two above it, which is where a wrapping `with_retries(` will
  be after a formatter has broken the expression across lines. Two lines is a guess. It is tuned to
  produce no false positives on `one_door.py` and it will produce one the day somebody writes a
  four-line wrapper.
- `max(0, number - 3)` guards the top of the file. `number` is 1-based from `enumerate(..., start=1)`
  and the slice is 0-based, so `lines[number - 3 : number]` is the two preceding lines plus the
  current one. Off-by-one here silently widens or narrows the window and nothing would fail.
- The finding includes the file, the line number and the source line, so the output is something an
  editor can jump to. A checker that says "4 problems" and not where is a checker people disable.
- `main()` returns `1` when there are findings, which is what makes it usable in `./m check` — the
  exit code is the interface, exactly as in Day 34's
  [6.2](../../../day-34-building-sutra-mcp-tools/parts/06-in-production/6.2-red-before-you-ship-it.md).

Four findings and an exit code of 1, against nothing and a 0. Note that line 28 — `article`, the one
that *does* retry — is a finding. It has a retry loop and it is still unhardened, because the check
is not asking "did you think about failure", it is asking "did you go through the door". That
distinction is what makes the check useful: a local retry that looks conscientious is exactly the
kind of thing that never gets found by reading.

## When it breaks

The first break is a false negative that lets the very thing you were guarding against through:

```text
findings: 0
exit: 0
```

on a file containing a call built with `getattr(session, method_name)(**args)`. The scan looks for
literal method names, and a dynamic dispatch has none. This is not a reason to write a cleverer
regex; it is a reason to have a rule that dynamic dispatch to MCP methods is not allowed in
`sutra/mcp/`, which is a thing a human reviewer can check and a scan cannot.

The second break is a false positive that trains people to ignore the tool:

```text
tests/test_mcp_failures.py:41: unhardened call -> fake.call_tool("lookup_ticket", {})
```

A test driving a fake session does not need a deadline. Two or three of these and somebody adds
`--exclude tests/`, and two sprints later the exclusion has grown to cover half the repository. The
exclusion list is a thing to keep short and to review, and the hub leaves *which* paths are scanned
as a `TODO(me)` for exactly this reason.

The third break is the one the check cannot see at all. Every call site goes through the door, and
the door's numbers are wrong:

```text
$ uv run python onepolicy.py sutra/mcp/client.py
findings: 0
```

Green, with a per-call deadline of three hundred seconds inherited from ADK's `sse_read_timeout`
([2.4](../02-the-deadline/2.4-the-numbers-your-libraries-chose.md)). The scan proves the policy is
*applied*, never that it is *right*. Those are two different checks and the second one is a test that
asserts the effective numbers, not a grep.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead of the teaching version.** The check is an AST walk rather than
a regex — `ast.parse` and a visitor looking for `Call` nodes whose function is an attribute named in
the set, then checking whether any enclosing call in the node's ancestry is the policy helper. That
removes the window heuristic and the formatter sensitivity entirely, and it is perhaps forty lines.
The regex version exists here because it is honest about what it is and because a check that ships
today beats a better one that ships next quarter. Either way it runs in `./m check` beside Day 31's
two linters and Day 45's audit, so it fails a build rather than producing a report.

**What degrades at scale or under concurrency.** Not the scan — it is milliseconds. What degrades is
the *exclusion list*, monotonically, because every exclusion is added under pressure by somebody who
is right about their specific case. The countermeasure is to make the exclusion list a data file with
a reason column and a review date, the same shape as the retry table in
[1.3](../01-what-may-be-repeated/1.3-the-line-through-every-call.md), so that "why is this excluded"
has an answer that is not `git blame`.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic.** One unhardened call site is enough. The
policy can be perfect in ninety-nine places and the hundredth is the one that holds a worker for
three hundred seconds during an incident, and because it is a single call site it will look like a
mysterious partial outage — most requests fine, one endpoint hanging. That asymmetry is the whole
argument for a structural check over a review convention: reviews catch most things, and *most* is
not the bar when one miss is an outage.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"good module. Now add the check that it is used,
and wire it into `./m check` in the same commit. Otherwise the third call site somebody adds will not
go through it, and we will spend an afternoon looking at the timeout config while the actual call has
no timeout at all."*

**The interview question:** *"how do you make sure a policy like this is actually followed?"* —
*"I do not rely on people remembering it. The policy goes in one module with one entry point, and then
I write a check that fails the build when an outward call does not go through it — in our case a
scan for the four MCP client methods that looks for the helper on the same or preceding lines. It is
deliberately dumb, it has false positives, and it runs on every commit, which makes it worth far more
than a smarter analysis nobody runs. The thing I am careful about is the exclusion list, because it
only ever grows, so it is a data file with a reason per row. And I am clear about what the check does
not do: it proves the policy is applied, not that the numbers are right. That second one is a test
that asserts the effective timeout, because ours is inherited from a library default that is three
hundred seconds and nobody would guess it by reading our config."*

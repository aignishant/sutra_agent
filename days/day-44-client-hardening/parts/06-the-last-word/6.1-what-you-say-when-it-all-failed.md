# 6.1 — What you say when all of it has failed

## One-line answer

Every retry ladder ends, and the sentence the client says at that moment is the only part of this
day a human will ever read — so it has to state what was attempted, what is known and not known
about the world, and who decides next, because a shrug or an invented answer has broken Principle 10
after four parts of careful work.

## The story

The form comes back from the school office with a red cross in the corner and nothing else.

You had filled it in at the kitchen table with the birth certificate and the address proof in front
of you, and you had queued, and the woman at the counter had taken it without looking up. Now it is
back, crossed, and there is no way to find out what is wrong with it except to queue again and hope
the same woman is there and is in a mood to say.

Compare the one your neighbour got back the same week. Same form, also rejected, with three lines
written on a slip stapled to the front: *missing — attested copy of the birth certificate. Bring it
to counter 3. Reference SCH/4412.* She was annoyed for about a minute and then she went and got the
copy.

Both forms were refused. Only one of them was a refusal anybody could act on. The difference cost the
office the time it takes to write three lines, and it saved a person a morning.

## The idea in plain language

A retry policy has a last attempt. When it fails, the client has to return something, and there are
exactly three things it can return:

- **Swallow it.** Return `None`, or an empty list, or a default. The caller cannot tell this apart
  from a legitimately empty answer, and it will act on it. Day 21's
  [4.1](../../../day-21-errors-surface-not-swallow/parts/04-failure-lab/4.1-the-swallowed-exception.md)
  is what that costs.
- **Invent something.** Return a plausible sentence — *"No ticket found with that id"* — because it
  reads better than an error. This is the worst of the three and it is the one that looks most like
  care.
- **Escalate.** Say what happened, say what is not known, and hand the decision to something that can
  make it.

Principle 10 is not "log the error". It is *errors surface, escalate and are logged, and you never
fabricate a result to cover an error*. The middle word is the one this part is about: **escalate**
means the failure reaches something with authority to decide, and reaching it requires that the
failure carry enough for a decision to be possible.

Six things an honest escalation carries, and each one answers a question somebody will otherwise ask:

| It carries | Answers |
| --- | --- |
| what was attempted | *which call, on which server, with what arguments* |
| what was attempted **how many times** | *is this a blip or an outage* |
| the last real error | *what was actually going wrong* |
| **what is not known** | *did the write land?* — [1.2](../01-what-may-be-repeated/1.2-a-timeout-is-an-unknown.md) |
| whether it is worth retrying later | *do we queue it or bin it* |
| the correlation id | *where do I look next* |

The fourth row is the one that is almost always missing and is the most important for a write. After
a timeout on `close_ticket` the truthful sentence is not *"the ticket was not closed"* — you do not
know that. It is *"the ticket may or may not have been closed; nothing here can tell you"*, and a
human reading that will go and look, which is exactly the right behaviour.

And there is an audience question. Sutra's failures are read by two very different readers: a model,
which is deciding what to do next in a loop, and a person, who is deciding whether to intervene. Day
34's [4.1](../../../day-34-building-sutra-mcp-tools/parts/04-two-kinds-of-no/4.1-the-tool-said-no.md)
established that a tool error's text is written for the model. The escalation object below is written
for both: a `message` field the model can act on, and structured fields a log pipeline and a person
can query. Day 34's
[4.3](../../../day-34-building-sutra-mcp-tools/parts/04-two-kinds-of-no/4.3-the-message-that-escaped.md)
is the constraint on both — no connection strings, no filesystem paths, no credentials.

## Why Sutra needs it

This is the day's Principle 10 checkpoint, and without it the previous seventeen parts make things
worse rather than better. A client with deadlines, backoff, jitter, a budget and a breaker fails
*more often and faster* than a naive one — the breaker refuses calls outright
([4.2](../04-when-to-stop-asking/4.2-the-switch-that-refuses-first.md) measured five that would have
worked), the budget stops early, and the deadline cuts off slow-but-fine calls. All of that is
correct, and all of it is a net loss if those refusals arrive at the top as a silent `None`.

Concretely, Sutra's desk agent runs a loop. A tool result that says nothing goes back into the model's
context as an absence, and a model with an absence where an error should be will do the reasonable
thing and try a different phrasing of the same question — Day 34's
[4.1](../../../day-34-building-sutra-mcp-tools/parts/04-two-kinds-of-no/4.1-the-tool-said-no.md)
covers exactly that trap. The escalation object is what stops a hardened client from becoming a
quieter one.

## The mechanism

> 🎬 **The scene:** the ticket archive has been unreachable for a minute. Sutra's client tries three
> times over four and a half seconds, backing off, and the third attempt times out like the other
> two.
>
> 😬 **The naive fix:** `except Exception: return None`. It keeps the agent running, which feels like
> resilience.
>
> 💥 **Why it fails:** the model receives an absence and reports to the support agent that there is
> no ticket 4521. The agent tells the customer. Nobody involved has been told that anything failed,
> and the retry policy that behaved perfectly is invisible.
>
> 💡 **The insight:** hardening does not reduce the number of failures a caller has to handle. It
> makes them arrive faster and more clearly. If the top of the stack does not say them out loud, all
> you have built is a quieter outage.

Three endings, side by side:

```python
# days/day-44-client-hardening/lab/escalate.py
def shrug() -> str:
    """Swallow it. The caller gets a value that means nothing went wrong."""
    return json.dumps({"result": None})


def invent() -> str:
    """Worse: answer anyway. Nothing marks this as made up."""
    return json.dumps({"result": "No ticket found with that id."})


def escalate() -> str:
    """Surface it, with everything the next person needs to act."""
    return json.dumps(
        {
            "error": "tool_unavailable",
            "message": (
                "lookup_ticket on sutra-mcp did not answer after 3 attempts over 4.5s. "
                "The ticket was not read and nothing was changed."
            ),
            "call": CALL,
            "attempts": ATTEMPTS,
            "retryable": True,
            "next": "escalate_to_human",
            "correlation_id": "run-2f9c-0007",
        },
        indent=2,
    )
```

**Line by line:**

- `shrug` returns valid JSON with a `result` key, and that is the trap in one line: it is
  *well-formed*. Every schema check passes. The only thing wrong with it is that it is not true.
- `invent`'s sentence is the one a model would write if you asked it to be helpful, and it is a claim
  about the archive made by code that could not reach the archive.
- `error: "tool_unavailable"` is a **machine-readable code** first. A message is for reading; a code
  is for branching, and the layer above needs to branch on this without parsing English.
- The `message` is written for the model and does two jobs. It names the call and the attempt count
  so the model does not immediately retry, and its second sentence — *"nothing was changed"* — is a
  claim that is only safe because `lookup_ticket` is a read. **For a write this sentence must say the
  opposite**: *"whether the ticket was closed is unknown"*, per
  [1.2](../01-what-may-be-repeated/1.2-a-timeout-is-an-unknown.md). Getting this line wrong on a
  write is the single most damaging thing in this part.
- `call` carries the server, tool and arguments. Not a formatted string — the structured values, so
  a log pipeline can group by tool and a person can retry it by hand. Note what it must **not**
  carry: an argument holding personal data goes in redacted, which is Day 34's
  [4.3](../../../day-34-building-sutra-mcp-tools/parts/04-two-kinds-of-no/4.3-the-message-that-escaped.md).
- `attempts` is a list, one entry per attempt, each with its wait and its own error. Three timeouts
  and three different errors tell very different stories, and a summary that says only "3 attempts"
  throws away the distinction.
- `retryable: True` is the classifier's verdict, not a guess — Day 38's
  [2.2](../../../day-38-failure-and-migration-lab/parts/02-the-x-ray/2.2-malformed-is-not-transient.md)
  is where that judgement lives. It tells a queueing layer whether this is worth trying later or is
  permanently broken.
- `next: "escalate_to_human"` is the escalation itself made explicit. Day 21's
  [1.3](../../../day-21-errors-surface-not-swallow/parts/01-who-hears-it/1.3-retry-substitute-escalate.md)
  gave the three options — retry, substitute, escalate — and this field says which one was chosen,
  so nobody downstream has to infer it.
- `correlation_id` ties this to every log line from the same run, which is Day 22's
  [2.2](../../../day-22-structured-logging/parts/02-wiring-the-run/2.2-the-correlation-id.md). Without
  it the escalation is a fact with no address.
- `indent=2` because a human reads this one. The log line is compact JSON
  ([Day 22's 1.2](../../../day-22-structured-logging/parts/01-a-line-you-can-query/1.2-one-json-object-per-line.md));
  this is the version that reaches a person.

## When it breaks

The first break is an escalation that escalates to nothing. The object is beautifully constructed,
logged at ERROR, and read by nobody:

```text
2026-09-05T09:14:07Z ERROR sutra.mcp tool_unavailable lookup_ticket run-2f9c-0007
```

A log line is a record, not an escalation. Escalation means it reaches something with authority to
decide — an alert, a queue a person works, a graph edge that routes to a human. If the only consumer
is a log file that is grepped after an incident, you have documented the failure rather than raised
it.

The second break is a message that leaks. Attaching the last exception's `str()` verbatim is the
obvious way to fill in the `error` field and it is how connection strings get into a model's context:

```text
"error": "ConnectionError: could not connect to postgresql://sutra:hunter2@db-1.internal:5432/tickets"
```

That string is now in a log, in the model's context, and possibly in a reply to a customer. Day 34's
[4.3](../../../day-34-building-sutra-mcp-tools/parts/04-two-kinds-of-no/4.3-the-message-that-escaped.md)
built the guard; the escalation object is on the wrong side of it by default, because it is
constructed from exceptions.

The third break is the truthful-sounding claim about a write. This is the one to watch for in review:

```text
"message": "close_ticket on sutra-mcp did not answer after 1 attempt. The ticket was not closed."
```

Every word of that is what the code knows except the last five. The client does not know the ticket
was not closed; it knows it did not hear back. A human reading it will not go and check, because they
have been told. Two paragraphs of this day exist to prevent that sentence, and it will still be
written, because it is what the read-path template says and somebody will copy it.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead of the teaching version.** One exception type carrying a
structured payload — `ToolUnavailable(call=..., attempts=[...], outcome_known=False,
retryable=True)` — raised by `with_retries` and rendered differently at each boundary: as a compact
JSON log line, as a tool result the model can read, and as an alert. Rendering is a boundary concern
and construction is not, so the fields are assembled once, at the point where the failure is known,
rather than reconstructed from a string three layers up. The `outcome_known` flag is derived from the
idempotency classification in
[1.3](../01-what-may-be-repeated/1.3-the-line-through-every-call.md), not typed by hand, because a
flag a human sets is a flag a human forgets.

**What degrades at scale or under concurrency.** Escalation volume. A dependency that is down
produces one escalation per request, and at real traffic that is thousands of identical alerts, which
is the same as none. The fix is to aggregate on the way out — alert on the *breaker opening* rather
than on each refused call, which is one event per dependency per outage
([4.2](../04-when-to-stop-asking/4.2-the-switch-that-refuses-first.md) is why the breaker's
transitions are the log lines that matter) — while still logging each individual failure for later
querying.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic.** Escalations that are technically correct
and operationally useless because they arrive without the one field that identifies the affected
user. During an incident the first question is *"who is affected"*, and an escalation with a tool
name and a correlation id but no tenant, customer or ticket reference cannot answer it. That field
has to be chosen in advance, because adding it during an incident means shipping a deploy.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"the retry helper returns `None` when it gives up.
Raise instead, with the call, the attempt list and whether the outcome is known — and for the write
path, do not say the ticket was not closed. We do not know that. Say the outcome is unknown, because
somebody is going to read this and decide whether to phone the customer."*

**The interview question:** *"what does your client do when all the retries fail?"* — *"It raises,
with a payload rather than a message. The call, the attempt list with each wait and each error,
whether the failure is retryable, the correlation id, and — the field people leave out — whether the
outcome is *known*. That last one matters because a timeout is not a failure, it is an unknown, so
for a write I will not say 'the ticket was not closed'; I say the outcome could not be determined,
which is what makes a human go and look. And escalation has to mean something reaches a decider — a
log line at ERROR that nobody is paged on is documentation, not escalation. The thing I have learned
to check is that hardening does not make failures rarer, it makes them faster and more numerous, so
if the top of the stack swallows them you have built a quieter outage rather than a shorter one."*

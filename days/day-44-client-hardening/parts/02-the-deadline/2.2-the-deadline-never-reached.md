# 2.2 — 💥 The deadline that is never reached

## One-line answer

A timeout is only real if it is shorter than every timeout above it, so a client that hands each
attempt a fixed number instead of the budget it has left will keep working long after the person
waiting has gone, and its careful retry ladder will be dead code that nothing reports.

## The story

The market shuts at eight and you need a specific fitting for the tap before it does.

You cannot go, so you send your younger brother. He is sensible about it: *"there are three shops
that might have it, I will give each one a proper look, ten minutes each, and if the third one has it
too I will get the cheaper."* Good plan. Thorough.

It is quarter to eight when he leaves.

He does the first shop properly. He does the second shop properly. He is standing outside the third
one, which is shuttered, at twenty past eight, and he is annoyed because the plan said three shops
and he only got two-and-a-bit — while you, at home, gave up at eight, because eight is when the
market shuts and there was nothing else to know.

Everything he did was reasonable in isolation. His plan just never looked at the clock on the wall,
only at his own watch. And so the thing he was carefully budgeting had already stopped mattering
before he got to the second half of it.

## The idea in plain language

Calls nest. When a person waits for a page, that page waits for Sutra, Sutra waits for an MCP server,
and the MCP server waits for a database. Each layer has a timeout, and each was chosen by somebody
who was only thinking about their own layer.

The arithmetic that nobody does:

- The caller above allows the whole request **2 seconds**.
- Sutra allows each MCP call **1.5 seconds** and retries up to **3 times**.
- Therefore Sutra's own worst case is 1.5 × 3 = **4.5 seconds**, which is more than twice what it was
  given.

Two things go wrong, and they are separate.

**The first is waste.** Everything after the outer deadline is work nobody will read. The second and
third attempts are sent to a server that is already struggling, on behalf of a request that has been
abandoned. That is load with no possible benefit — and at scale, during an incident, it is a large
fraction of the load.

**The second is worse, and it is the reason this part exists.** Your inner numbers are *not being
used*. You tuned a retry ladder, you chose 1.5 seconds because you measured the tool's p99, and none
of it fires, because the request is killed from above before your own logic gets a chance. From
inside the service everything looks configured. From outside, the behaviour is entirely determined by
somebody else's number.

The fix has a name: pass a **deadline** rather than a **timeout**.

- A *timeout* is a duration: "you may take 1.5 seconds".
- A *deadline* is an instant: "you may take until 10:04:07.412".

A duration means the same thing at every layer, which is precisely the bug — three layers each
allowing 1.5 seconds allow 4.5. An instant means less at each layer, automatically, because time has
passed. Every hop asks for `min(my_timeout, deadline.remaining())`, and when `remaining()` reaches
zero nobody attempts anything, because there is nobody left to answer to.

This is the same idea as a budget, and it is worth noticing that Sutra has already met it in a
different currency: Day 24 counted tokens the same way
([3.2](../../../day-24-token-accounting-and-budgets/parts/03-counting-before-spending/3.2-refusing-before-the-call.md)
is refusing before the call rather than discovering afterwards). Time is a budget too. The only
difference is that it is spent whether or not you use it.

## Why Sutra needs it

Sutra is in the middle of a stack and always will be. Above it is a user, an agent loop, or Phase 8's
triage graph. Below it is `sutra_mcp` and whatever a partner runs. That means every timeout Sutra
sets is an inner timeout for somebody, and every timeout Sutra sets is an outer timeout for the
server it calls.

`sutra/mcp/hardening.py` therefore cannot take a plain `seconds: float` and stop there. It needs a
deadline that travels — an object the call site can ask for the remaining budget — or the retry
policy you are about to build in [3.4](../03-backing-off/3.4-with-retries.md) is a machine for
producing exactly this failure, three times per call.

## The mechanism

> 🎬 **The scene:** a support page calls Sutra and gives the whole request two seconds. Sutra calls a
> partner's MCP server, which is having a bad afternoon and takes four seconds to answer anything.
>
> 😬 **The naive fix:** the retry ladder that was added last sprint. Three attempts, 1.5 seconds
> each, which was carefully derived from the tool's own latency data.
>
> 💥 **Why it fails:** at two seconds the page has gone. Attempts two and three are sent on behalf of
> nobody, to a server that is already the reason for the incident. And the ladder's own numbers never
> decide anything, because the request's fate was settled by the outer deadline before attempt two
> began.
>
> 💡 **The insight:** pass the deadline down, not the duration. Every hop should ask for less than
> the one above it, automatically, because it is later than it was.

Two objects and one branch:

```python
# days/day-44-client-hardening/lab/budget.py
class Deadline:
    """A wall-clock instant, and how much of it is left right now."""

    def __init__(self, seconds: float) -> None:
        self.expires_at = time.monotonic() + seconds

    def remaining(self) -> float:
        """Seconds still available. Never negative; zero means spend nothing more."""
        return max(0.0, self.expires_at - time.monotonic())


def sutra_call(deadline: Deadline, *, shrink: bool) -> str:
    """Sutra's retry loop. `shrink` decides whether the inner clock knows about the outer one."""
    for attempt in range(1, ATTEMPTS + 1):
        allowed = min(INNER_DEADLINE, deadline.remaining()) if shrink else INNER_DEADLINE
        if shrink and allowed <= 0.0:
            raise OuterDeadlineExceeded("no budget left; not attempting")
        print(f"  attempt {attempt}: asking for {allowed:.2f}s, {deadline.remaining():.2f}s left")
        try:
            return slow_call(allowed)
        except TimeoutError as err:
            print(f"    -> {err}")
    raise TimeoutError("all attempts exhausted")
```

**Line by line:**

- `Deadline` stores an **instant** (`expires_at`), not a duration. That is the whole design. An object
  holding `seconds = 2.0` would answer "2.0" every time it was asked, at every layer, forever.
- `time.monotonic()` rather than `time.time()`. Monotonic time cannot jump backwards when the machine
  syncs its clock, and a deadline computed from wall-clock time can silently become "already expired"
  or "expires next week" during an NTP correction. Day 22's
  [1.4](../../../day-22-structured-logging/parts/01-a-line-you-can-query/1.4-timestamps-are-not-an-order.md)
  is the general form of this warning.
- `remaining()` clamps at zero with `max(0.0, ...)`. A negative remaining budget is not a meaningful
  number to hand to a socket, and a library that receives a negative timeout will do something
  arbitrary — `anyio.fail_after(-1)` fires immediately, `socket.settimeout(-1)` raises `ValueError`.
  Clamping means one branch above handles the expired case explicitly instead of every caller
  discovering it differently.
- `allowed = min(INNER_DEADLINE, deadline.remaining())` is the fix, in one expression. The inner
  policy still applies — a tool is never given more than its own budget — but it can be given less,
  and it is given less automatically as time passes.
- `if shrink and allowed <= 0.0: raise OuterDeadlineExceeded(...)` is the part people leave out. When
  the budget is gone you do not make a call with a zero-second deadline; you do not make the call at
  all. A zero-second call is a request sent, work started on the far side, and an immediate give-up —
  all cost, no possibility of benefit.
- `OuterDeadlineExceeded` is a distinct exception type, not a `TimeoutError`. They mean genuinely
  different things: `TimeoutError` means *the server did not answer me*, and this means *I ran out of
  the time I was given*. A retry policy that cannot tell them apart will retry the second one, which
  is the failure this part is about, wearing a slightly better disguise.
- The `print` inside the loop shows `allowed` and `remaining()` side by side, which is the only way
  to see the bug: in the broken arm the first number never changes while the second one runs out.

**Line by line:**

- The first arm is what almost every codebase does: a fixed inner timeout, three attempts.
- `--shrink` is the ablation switch. One `min(...)` and one guard.
- **Zero model calls.**

Measured on 2026-09-05:

```text
outer budget 2.0s   inner deadline 1.5s   attempts 3
budget passed down: False
  attempt 1: asking for 1.50s, 2.00s left
    -> no response within 1.50s
  attempt 2: asking for 1.50s, 0.50s left
    -> no response within 1.50s
  attempt 3: asking for 1.50s, 0.00s left
    -> no response within 1.50s
  sutra gave up after 4.50s with TimeoutError: all attempts exhausted

the caller above stopped waiting at 2.00s; we ran to 4.50s.
everything after the outer deadline was work nobody was waiting for.
```

```text
outer budget 2.0s   inner deadline 1.5s   attempts 3
budget passed down: True
  attempt 1: asking for 1.50s, 2.00s left
    -> no response within 1.50s
  attempt 2: asking for 0.50s, 0.50s left
    -> no response within 0.50s
  sutra gave up after 2.00s with OuterDeadlineExceeded: no budget left; not attempting
```

Read attempt 3 in the first arm. It asks for a second and a half when there is nothing left at all,
and the third line of that run is a request sent on behalf of a caller who left. **4.50s against
2.00s**, for the same failure, against the same server.

And read attempt 2 in the second arm: it asks for `0.50s`, which is neither the inner number nor the
outer number but what is genuinely left. That is what a deadline does that a timeout cannot.

## When it breaks

Passing deadlines down has its own failure, and it is the mirror image. If every layer subtracts a
safety margin, the budget shrinks faster than the clock:

```text
  attempt 1: asking for 0.00s, 1.85s left
  sutra gave up after 0.00s with OuterDeadlineExceeded: no budget left; not attempting
```

A request that had nearly two seconds available made no call at all, because four layers each took
"a bit of headroom for the response trip" out of a budget that was already tight. Headroom is
subtracted once, at the top, by the caller who owns the user's expectation — not at every hop.

The second break is a deadline that does not survive the boundary it most needs to cross. Sutra can
pass a `Deadline` object between its own functions; it cannot pass a Python object to a server in
another process. Over the wire the remaining budget has to be *serialised*, and MCP has no standard
field for it. The realistic options are a `_meta` entry on the request, or an HTTP header, and both
are a private agreement between one client and one server, exactly like the idempotency key in
[1.4](../01-what-may-be-repeated/1.4-the-key-that-makes-a-write-repeatable.md). If you do not send
it, the server has no idea it is working on something abandoned, and Day 38's
[1.2](../../../day-38-failure-and-migration-lab/parts/01-the-clock/1.2-giving-up-ends-the-wait.md)
is what that costs.

The third break is invisible until you look for it. A per-tool timeout that is *larger* than the
service's own request budget is dead configuration:

```text
tool                 configured   ever able to fire?
lookup_ticket             1.5s    yes
search_kb                 3.0s    no  (request budget is 2.0s)
reindex_archive         120.0s    no
```

Nothing errors. The numbers are in the config file, they look considered, and two of the three rows
are fiction. A startup assertion that every per-tool timeout is smaller than the service's own budget
turns this from a discovery into a failed deploy.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead of the teaching version.** The deadline is part of the request
context, created once at the edge where the user's expectation is known, and carried through every
layer — the same object that carries the correlation id from Day 22's
[2.2](../../../day-22-structured-logging/parts/02-wiring-the-run/2.2-the-correlation-id.md). It is
propagated over the wire as an absolute expiry or a remaining-milliseconds field, and services that
receive one honour it by refusing work they cannot finish in time. gRPC has had this as a first-class
feature for years and calls it exactly that: a deadline, not a timeout. MCP does not have it yet,
which means it is your `_meta` field and your convention.

**What degrades at scale or under concurrency.** Work with no reader is the most expensive kind of
load, because it is generated by exactly the requests that are already failing. During an incident,
the fraction of a struggling service's traffic that is abandoned rises sharply — and without deadline
propagation, none of that work is skippable, because the server has no way to know. This is the
mechanism that turns a slow dependency into a self-sustaining outage that continues after the traffic
stops.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic.** Deadlines expiring *between* the retry and
the call, in the gap where the backoff sleep happens. A client checks `remaining()`, sees 0.6
seconds, sleeps 0.5 for backoff, and then issues a call with the 0.6 it computed earlier. It is a
stale read of a value that changes on its own, and it only misbehaves when the numbers are close,
which is only under load. Compute `allowed` **after** the sleep, never before.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"this helper takes `timeout: float`. Take a
deadline instead. Right now three attempts of 1.5 seconds inside a two-second request means attempts
two and three are load we generate on a service that is already down, for a caller who has gone. And
tell me what you send over the wire so the server knows how long it has."*

**The interview question:** *"you have retries and timeouts at three layers of a call chain. What
goes wrong?"* — *"They multiply, and nobody chose the product. Three attempts at each of three layers
is twenty-seven requests at the bottom and nobody wrote twenty-seven anywhere. The time version is
worse in a quiet way: the inner numbers stop firing at all, because the outer deadline kills the
request first, so you have a carefully tuned retry policy that is dead code and no log line says so.
The fix is to pass a deadline — an instant, not a duration — created once at the edge and carried
down, with every hop asking for the minimum of its own budget and what is left, and refusing to make
a call at all when nothing is left."*

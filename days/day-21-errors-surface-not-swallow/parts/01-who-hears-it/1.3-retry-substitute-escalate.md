# 1.3 — Retry, substitute, escalate — and nothing else

## One-line answer

There are exactly three things a system can do about a failure — **try again**, **carry on with a
marked stand-in**, or **stop and tell somebody** — and retry is never the last one, because retries
run out.

## The story

The delivery driver cannot get an answer at the door.

He does three things, in order, and he does not invent a fourth. He knocks again and waits, because
people are in the shower. Then he tries the neighbour, because a parcel with the neighbour is not the
parcel arriving but it is not nothing either, and he writes it on the card so you know where to go.
Then, if neither works, he takes it back to the depot and it becomes somebody's problem tomorrow.

What he never does is leave it on the step and mark it delivered.

## The idea in plain language

Once a failure has arrived somewhere useful ([1.1](1.1-three-parties.md)) as something you can branch
on ([1.2](1.2-an-error-is-a-fact.md)), there is a decision to make, and the space of decisions is
small.

**Retry.** Do the same thing again. Correct only when the failure is *transient* — the condition that
caused it can clear on its own, without anybody doing anything. A per-minute rate limit clears. A
network blip clears. A missing ticket does not clear, and a permissions error does not clear.

**Substitute.** Give the caller something that is not the answer, is clearly marked as not the
answer, and lets the work continue. This is the driver leaving it next door *and writing the card*.
The marking is the whole thing, and [3.2](../03-policy/3.2-a-substitute-must-say-so.md) is about how
easy it is to get wrong.

**Escalate.** Stop. Let the exception out, let the invocation fail, put it in front of a human. This
is the right answer more often than people expect, and it is the only correct one when there is
nothing honest the model could say.

The rule that makes this a policy rather than a preference: **retry is never terminal.** Every retry
either succeeds or runs out, and when it runs out you are back at the same fork with one fewer
option. So a policy that says "retry" has not finished being written until it also says what happens
on the last attempt. The table below has a column for exactly that, and it is the column most
implementations are missing.

There is a fourth thing systems do, and naming it is the point of this part: **proceed as if nothing
happened.** That is not a response to failure; it is the absence of one. It is the swallowed shape
from [1.1](1.1-three-parties.md), and every incident review that ends in *"why did nobody notice"*
starts there.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because Sutra is going to meet all six of the failures in the table below, and it currently has an
opinion about none of them.

Day 2 taught it to back off on a 429 and Day 4 taught it to say *"ticket 9999 does not exist"* rather
than invent a summary. Both were the right call, made twice, in two files, by hand. That is instinct.
The table below is the same instinct written down once, which is what makes it reviewable — and what
makes it possible for section 2 to put it in a single plugin instead of in every tool.

It also decides the shape of Day 64. A human approval gate is an *escalate* with a resumption path,
and you cannot build one until "escalate" is a first-class outcome of your error policy rather than
the thing that happens when the other two fail.

## The mechanism

Six real failures, the two fields that decide, and what happens on the first attempt versus the last.

```python
# days/day-21-errors-surface-not-swallow/lab/three_responses.py
"""There are only three things you can do about a failure. Deciding which is the policy."""

FAILURES = [
    {"kind": "timeout", "target": "kb.internal", "retryable": True, "user_visible": False},
    {"kind": "rate_limit_minute", "target": "gemini", "retryable": True, "user_visible": False},
    {"kind": "rate_limit_day", "target": "gemini", "retryable": False, "user_visible": True},
    {"kind": "not_found", "target": "ticket 9999", "retryable": False, "user_visible": True},
    {
        "kind": "permission_denied",
        "target": "billing_api",
        "retryable": False,
        "user_visible": True,
    },
    {"kind": "bug", "target": "summarise_ticket", "retryable": False, "user_visible": False},
]

ATTEMPTS_LEFT = 3


def decide(failure: dict[str, object], attempts_left: int) -> str:
    """Retry, substitute, or escalate - in that order, and never more than one."""
    if failure["retryable"] and attempts_left > 0:
        return "retry"
    if failure["user_visible"]:
        return "substitute"  # the model gets a marked result and tells the user
    return "escalate"  # nothing sensible to say: let it raise


print(f"{'kind':<20} {'retryable':<10} {'user-visible':<13} {'first move':<12} {'out of retries'}")
for failure in FAILURES:
    first = decide(failure, ATTEMPTS_LEFT)
    exhausted = decide(failure, 0)
    print(
        f"{failure['kind']:<20} {str(failure['retryable']):<10} "
        f"{str(failure['user_visible']):<13} {first:<12} {exhausted}"
    )

print("\nretry is never the last answer: every row ends in substitute or escalate.")
```

**Line by line:**

- `FAILURES` is the list of things that actually happen to Sutra, not a taxonomy invented for the
  example. Two rate limits, a timeout, a missing record, a permissions refusal, and a bug in your own
  code — that is the honest set, and the two rate-limit rows are deliberately adjacent because
  [3.1](../03-policy/3.1-which-429-is-it.md) is entirely about telling them apart.
- `retryable` and `user_visible` are the two fields from
  [1.2](1.2-an-error-is-a-fact.md), doing the work they were defined for. The whole policy is four
  lines because the fields did the hard part at the point of failure.
- The order of the three `if`s **is** the policy. Retry is checked first because it can make the
  question moot; escalate is last because it is the fallback that always applies.
- `attempts_left > 0` is what makes retry non-terminal. Remove it and `decide` can return `"retry"`
  forever, which is not a policy, it is a loop.
- `decide(failure, 0)` in the last column runs the *same function* with the retries gone. That is the
  ablation: it asks each row what it does when its first choice has been exhausted, which is the
  question implementations forget to answer.
- `"bug"` has `user_visible: False` and therefore escalates. A `KeyError` in Sutra's own code is not
  something to paper over with a graceful message — there is nothing true to say to the user except
  that it broke, and a human needs to see the traceback.

**Line by line:**

- Pure Python: a list, a function and two calls per row. No ADK, no key, no quota.

Measured on 2026-09-03:

```text
timeout              True       False         retry        escalate
rate_limit_minute    True       False         retry        escalate
rate_limit_day       False      True          substitute   substitute
not_found            False      True          substitute   substitute
permission_denied    False      True          substitute   substitute
bug                  False      False         escalate     escalate

retry is never the last answer: every row ends in substitute or escalate.
```

**The last column is the point of the whole part.** Two rows change when the retries run out, and
both change to `escalate` — a timeout that never clears and a rate limit that never lifts both end
with somebody being told. An implementation that only wrote the fourth column has no behaviour at all
for its two most common failures once the third attempt fails.

**Three rows never retry, and two of those look retryable at a glance.** `rate_limit_day` is a 429,
exactly like `rate_limit_minute` is a 429, and it is `retryable: False` because the ceiling is daily —
which [Day 20](../../../day-20-context-engineering-compaction/LESSON.md) established from a live
quota body. Any classifier that keys on the status code alone gets this row wrong, and
[3.1](../03-policy/3.1-which-429-is-it.md) measures what that costs.

**And only one row escalates immediately.** `bug`. Your own code raising `KeyError` is the one failure
with no graceful degradation available, because you do not know what went wrong and therefore cannot
say anything true about it. The instinct to catch it and apologise politely is exactly the instinct
this day exists to remove.

## When it breaks

The policy itself cannot fail — it is four lines and two dictionary lookups. What breaks is a policy
that is missing its third branch, and the symptom is this:

```text
google.genai.errors.ClientError: 429 RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED. {'error': {'code': 429, 'message':
'You exceeded your current quota, please check your plan and billing details. ...
* Quota exceeded for metric: generativelanguage.googleapis.com/generate_content_free_tier_requests,
limit: 20, model: gemini-3.7-flash\nPlease retry in 57.991539991s.', 'status':
'RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED'}}
```

That is the real body, captured on 2026-09-03. It is what reaches the user's screen when a policy
retries three times, runs out, and has nothing written for the exhausted case — so the original
exception simply escapes. It is honest, it is loud, and it contains an internal quota metric name and
a model identifier that the user has no business seeing. Escalating is correct; escalating *this
object, unfiltered, to a customer* is the thing `user_visible` exists to prevent.

## In production

**Write the exhausted column first.** The natural order to write a policy in is the order failures
arrive, which means retry gets implemented on the day of the incident and the give-up path gets
implemented after the second incident. Inverting that — deciding what happens when you give up, then
adding retries in front of it — produces a policy that is complete at every stage of its life.

**What changes at scale** is that `substitute` starts having a cost of its own. A marked stand-in is
fine for one user; a dependency that is down for an hour means every answer in that hour is degraded,
and if nothing counts them, the system looks healthy while serving an hour of *"I could not reach the
knowledge base"*. Substitutions need a counter more urgently than escalations do, because
escalations are already loud.

**The failure that only appears with real traffic** is the retryable failure that is actually
permanent. A dependency starts refusing every request with a timeout rather than an error, so
`retryable: True` is technically correct and practically wrong, and your system spends its entire
retry budget, on every request, achieving nothing. The defence is a circuit breaker — stop retrying a
target that has failed its last N attempts — and the reason it is not in this table is that it is a
policy about a *dependency*, not about an error.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"this retries three times and then re-raises the
original exception. That's an escalate, so say so — and check `user_visible` before it goes anywhere
near a response, because that quota message names our model and our project."*

**The interview question:** *"what are your options when a call inside an agent fails?"* The answer
that shows you have written the policy: *"three — retry, substitute, escalate. Retry only for things
that clear on their own, and it's never the final answer because retries run out, so every retryable
case needs an exhausted branch too. Substitute has to be marked as a substitute or it becomes a
fabricated result. Escalate is the right answer for our own bugs, because there's nothing true to
tell the user."*

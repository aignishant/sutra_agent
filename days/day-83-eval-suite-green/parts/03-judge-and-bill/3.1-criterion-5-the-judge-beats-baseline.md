# 3.1 — Criterion 5: the judge beats its baseline

## One-line answer

The only judge Phase 12 ever compared against human labels agreed on 83.3% of twenty-four answers,
and always answering the commonest label agrees on 83.3% too — a margin of zero and a kappa of zero,
which means the phase has never established that any judge in it reads anything.

## The story

The goalkeeper who always dives left.

Watch him over a season and he saves a decent number of penalties. He is not guessing badly: most
players, at that level, put it to his left, and he has noticed and stopped bothering to read the
run-up. His save percentage is respectable and his manager is happy.

Then somebody asks the only useful question. How many would a keeper who simply fell over to the left
every time, eyes shut, have saved? The same number. Every single one.

His save rate is a fact about where players shoot, not about him. Nothing in the season's statistics
distinguishes a keeper who reads a striker from a post that leans.

## The idea in plain language

Day 81 built this argument in full: an accuracy number means nothing until you say what it beats. A
**baseline** is the score of the laziest possible strategy on the same data, and the number worth
reporting is the *margin* between the two.

The laziest strategy for a labelling task is to always answer the commonest label. If 20 of 24
answers are `VALID`, then always saying `VALID` scores 20 of 24 — 83.3% — while reading nothing.

This criterion recomputes that comparison over Phase 12's only labelled set and asks whether the
margin was ever established. It reports three numbers:

| Number | What it is | Why it is there |
| --- | --- | --- |
| agreement | how often the judge and the person said the same thing | what people quote |
| baseline | the same score for always answering the commonest label | what it has to beat |
| Cohen's kappa | agreement above what two raters with these habits get by chance | the version that is comparable across sets |

Kappa is Day 80's paper, `doi:10.1177/001316446002000104`, and it is here because agreement and
baseline are specific to this label distribution while kappa is not — a kappa of 0 means *exactly
chance* on any set, and that is a sentence you can carry between projects.

**One thing this criterion is not.** It does not claim a real judge fails. It claims something more
awkward: the phase never ran one against labels. Day 81's measurement was made with a scripted
constant judge on purpose, to show the shape of the trap, and the calibration set that would let a
real judge be measured has been a `TODO(me)` on Days 79, 80 and 81. So the honest verdict is *not
established*, and `FAIL` is the exit code this repository uses for that.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because two of the eight checks in Phase 12's suite are judged, they cost 270 requests a night
between them, and neither has ever been shown to beat a coin. Spending an allowance on a measurement
whose value over the free alternative is unmeasured is the definition of a bad trade, and this
criterion is what makes that trade visible before the money is spent rather than after.

And because the judge is about to grade safety rules. `escalated_before_write` is a claim about
whether money moved before a human approved it. A judge with no established margin over "yes to
everything" grading that line is a keeper who always dives left, in front of an empty net.

## The mechanism

Agreement is a count:

```python
def agreement(a: list[str], b: list[str]) -> float:
    return sum(x == y for x, y in zip(a, b, strict=True)) / len(a)
```

**Line by line:**

- `sum` over booleans, which are integers in Python, so this counts matches without a separate
  counter.
- `strict=True` on the `zip`. Two label lists of different lengths are a bug in the data, not a
  shorter comparison, and without `strict` `zip` silently truncates to the shorter one and returns a
  perfectly plausible percentage. This is a small flag with a large blast radius.

The baseline is the majority class:

```python
    majority = Counter(HUMAN_LABELS).most_common(1)[0]
    baseline = majority[1] / len(HUMAN_LABELS)
```

**Line by line:**

- Computed from the **human** labels, not from the judge's. The baseline is a property of the task —
  what could be scored without looking at anything — so it must not depend on what the judge said.
- `most_common(1)[0]` is a `(label, count)` pair, so `majority[0]` is the label to print and
  `majority[1]` is the count. Printing the label matters: *"always answering VALID"* is a sentence a
  reader can check, where *"the baseline is 83.3%"* is a number they have to trust.

And kappa:

```python
def kappa(human: list[str], judge: list[str]) -> float:
    observed = agreement(human, judge)
    n = len(human)
    h, j = Counter(human), Counter(judge)
    expected = sum(h[label] * j[label] for label in set(human) | set(judge)) / (n * n)
    return 0.0 if expected == 1.0 else (observed - expected) / (1 - expected)
```

**Line by line:**

- `expected` is the chance agreement: for each label, how often the human uses it times how often the
  judge uses it, summed and divided by `n²`. That is the probability the two would coincide if each
  kept its own habits and ignored the other.
- `set(human) | set(judge)` — the union, so a label only one of them ever uses still contributes its
  zero. Iterating one rater's labels would silently drop a category.
- The guard on `expected == 1.0` is the case where both raters used exactly one label for everything.
  Then `1 - expected` is zero and the formula divides by zero; the honest answer there is `0.0`,
  because two raters who always say the same word agree completely and have demonstrated nothing.
- `(observed - expected) / (1 - expected)` is the coefficient itself: how much of the *available*
  room above chance was actually taken.

**Line by line:**

- No flag: agreement, baseline, margin and kappa together, which is the only combination this
  repository will call a result.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
the only judge this phase measured: day 81, over 24 answers
human labels: {'VALID': 20, 'INVALID': 4}

  agreement with the human labels: 83.3%
  always answering VALID            83.3%   <- the baseline
  margin over the baseline:        +0.0%
  Cohen's kappa:                   +0.00

  a margin under 5% is not evidence that the judge read anything
  no judge in this phase has been calibrated against a balanced set of labels
exit: 1
```

**+0.0% and +0.00.** Both numbers are exactly zero, which is not a coincidence: a judge that answers
one label for everything has, by construction, no margin over the strategy of answering that label
for everything, and kappa's numerator is the same difference.

## When it breaks

The criterion breaks by dropping a column, and the ablation is the report most teams actually ship:

```bash
uv run python c5_margin.py --raw; echo "exit: $?"
```

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
the only judge this phase measured: day 81, over 24 answers
human labels: {'VALID': 20, 'INVALID': 4}

  agreement with the human labels: 83.3%

  the judge agrees with us on most answers
exit: 0
```

Same judge, same labels, same computation up to the print. One line removed, and a finding becomes a
reassurance — with an exit code of `0`, so a pipeline built on this reports success.

There is no error text. `83.3%` is correct. *"The judge agrees with us on most answers"* is correct.
Everything in that output is true and the conclusion a reader draws from it is false, which is the
same shape as Day 81 part 3.3 and the reason this repository's rule is that an accuracy is never
printed without its baseline on the same screen.

**The second way it breaks** is a threshold argument. `WORTH_HAVING` is 5%, and that number is a
convention rather than a result — nothing measured it. A margin of 4% on 24 answers is one answer,
and calling it evidence or not evidence is a judgement about how much data there is, which is a
different question from the one the constant here pretends to settle. The right long-term fix is a
confidence interval rather than a bigger constant, and it is parked in the hub for the same reason
everything else is: it needs the calibration set.

## In production

**What a professional does.** Reports every classifier number as a triple — score, baseline, margin —
and refuses to print the first without the other two. On imbalanced data, which is most safety data,
this is not a nicety: rare-event detection is exactly where accuracy is highest and least
informative, and it is exactly where the stakes are.

**What degrades at scale.** More cases make the margin more trustworthy and the imbalance worse. A
production label set drifts towards the common case, because the common case is what traffic
produces, so the baseline creeps up over time and a judge whose margin was 8% last year has 3% now
without changing. The mitigation is to recompute the baseline every time the labels change, which
means the baseline lives in the reporting code and never in a comment.

**The failure that only shows with real traffic.** A judge that beats the baseline overall and loses
on the slice that matters. 83.3% agreement can hide 0 of 4 on the `INVALID` cases, which are the only
ones anybody cares about — and per-class figures are the only thing that shows it. This criterion
reports one overall number and would not catch it, which is the honest limit of the check as written.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Do not merge the judged metrics into the nightly
until we have a calibration set. We are proposing to spend 270 requests a night on an instrument
whose margin over free is zero as measured, and the fix is fifty labelled answers, not a better
prompt."*

**The interview question:** *"Your LLM judge agrees with human raters 90% of the time. Is it good?"*
The answer that shows experience asks what the majority class is. If 90% of the labels are one class,
the judge has demonstrated nothing, and the number to ask for next is per-class agreement on the rare
one.

# 1.2 — What "full evalset green" actually asks

## One-line answer

Six words hide four separate questions — which cases, which metrics, at which threshold, on which
run — and until each is answered in writing, "green" is a word two people can both use honestly
about opposite states of the same repository.

## The story

The notice taped to the lift door: **"Water supply will be in the morning."**

Everybody in the building reads it and nobody asks anything, because it seems perfectly clear. Then
the flat on the second floor fills its drums at six and finds nothing at eight. The flat on the
fourth waits until nine, because in the last building they lived in "morning" meant nine. The
watchman knows it means half past six to half past seven and assumed everybody did.

Three readings, one notice, no disagreement anybody noticed until the tanks were empty. The notice
was not wrong. It was short, and short is how a sentence gets to mean four things at once without
anybody arguing.

## The idea in plain language

Here is the whole of Phase 12's gate, copied from the plan:

```text
Full evalset green; rubric trajectories pass
```

Read it slowly and it comes apart into four questions the sentence does not answer.

**Which cases is "full"?** Day 79 built a triage evalset. Day 80 built three graded conversations.
Day 81 labelled 24 answers. Day 82 compared nine cases across two nights. Those are four different
sets of cases, they were never merged, and *full* could reasonably mean any of them or all of them.

**Which metrics?** Two of Phase 12's checks are free arithmetic, two need a judge, and four are
checks on the instruments rather than on the desk. "Green" over the free two is a different claim
from "green" over all eight.

**At which threshold?** Day 79 part 3.4 is the whole answer to this one: the threshold *is* the test.
`response_match_score` at 0.8 and the same metric at 0.0 give the same scores and opposite verdicts,
which is part [1.3](1.3-green-is-not-evidence.md)'s measurement.

**On which run?** A metric with a judge in it does not give the same answer twice. Day 80 part 4.1
measured three samples giving three answers. "Green" on one night is a different claim from "green on
the median of five", and Day 82 part 2.3 wanted the second one.

None of that is a complaint about the plan. A gate sentence is deliberately short — it is written
months before the phase and has to survive the phase changing shape. The work of a gate day is to
turn the short sentence into checks somebody can run, and to write down which reading was taken.

Here is the reading this day takes, in full:

| The words | Read as |
| --- | --- |
| **full** | every case any day of Phase 12 recorded, not one day's subset |
| **evalset** | the `.evalset.json` file, as loaded from disk rather than as built in memory |
| **green** | at the thresholds ADK defaults to: 1.0 for trajectory, 0.8 for response match |
| **rubric trajectories pass** | the trajectory rubric is graded on a transcript that contains the trajectory |

The fourth row is the one that turns out to matter, and part
[2.4](../02-instrument-criteria/2.4-criterion-4-the-rubric-can-see.md) is what happens when you check
it.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because the sentence is about to be answered with a verdict that goes in the ledger, and a verdict is
only worth writing down if the question it answers is written down beside it. Part
[5.1](../05-the-verdict/5.1-the-verdict.md) records "Phase 12 is not green" in `PROGRESS.md`; without
this part, a reader six months from now cannot tell whether that meant the desk failed or the
instruments did.

And because the same ambiguity is about to be inherited. Phase 13's gate sentence is equally short,
and every phase after it. The habit this part is building — read the gate, list what the words could
mean, write down which reading you took — is the reusable half of a gate day.

## The mechanism

The reading above is not a comment; it is the criteria list, and each row of the table becomes a
script. `gate.py` says which:

```python
CRITERIA = (
    Criterion("1  the evalset survives a round trip", "c1_roundtrip.py", "--drop-tools"),
    Criterion("2  the suite goes red against a stub desk", "c2_stub.py", "--lenient"),
    Criterion("3  every rubric line is exercised both ways", "c3_separates.py", "--one-sided"),
    Criterion("4  the rubric can see the tool calls", "c4_sees.py"),
    Criterion("5  the judge beats its baseline", "c5_margin.py"),
    Criterion("6  the phase fits a free-tier day", "c6_fits.py"),
    Criterion("7  there is a previous run to compare against", "c7_yesterday.py"),
    Criterion("8  every day written, no ID left open", "c8_written.py"),
)
```

**Line by line:**

- Criterion 1 is *evalset*, read as "the file". It exists because the reading in the table says the
  set is what comes back off disk, not what was built in memory — and those turn out to differ in a
  way that decides criterion 4.
- Criteria 2 and 3 are *green*, read as "at a threshold that can refuse something". A suite green at
  a threshold nothing can fail is green in the same sense the notice said morning.
- Criterion 4 is the second clause, read literally. "Rubric trajectories pass" presupposes that the
  trajectory reached the rubric.
- Criteria 5 to 8 are not in the sentence at all, and are here because the phase found them: a judge
  needs a baseline (Day 81), a suite needs an allowance (Day 78) and a yesterday (Day 82), and a
  phase needs its ledger rows (Principle 2). A gate day is allowed to add criteria the sentence did
  not think of; it is not allowed to drop one the sentence did.
- The third field is the fault to inject when the criterion passes — part
  [1.1](1.1-a-gate-on-the-instruments.md)'s red-alarm test. Criteria 4 to 8 carry `None` because they
  are already red.

## When it breaks

The reading breaks when it is taken silently, and the failure is that two people agree in words while
disagreeing about the repository. Here is the same day's suite under two readings of *green*:

```bash
cd days/day-83-eval-suite-green/lab
uv run python c2_stub.py; echo "exit: $?"
uv run python c2_stub.py --lenient; echo "exit: $?"
```

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
  case                trajectory  response  status
  by_the_book               0.00      0.00  FAILED
  refunded_first            0.00      0.00  FAILED
  read_everything           0.00      0.00  FAILED
exit: 0

  case                trajectory  response  status
  by_the_book               0.00      0.00  PASSED
  refunded_first            0.00      0.00  PASSED
  read_everything           0.00      0.00  PASSED
exit: 1
```

**Identical scores. Opposite statuses.** Nothing about the desk changed between those two runs, and
nothing about the metric changed either. What changed is a threshold, which is the part of "green"
the gate sentence does not contain — and a person reading only the `status` column has been handed a
different repository.

There is no error text to quote, and that is the point of the failure. Ambiguity does not raise.

## In production

**What a professional does.** Writes the acceptance criteria before the work and gets them agreed,
which is the same act as this part with the order reversed. When that has not happened — and on a
long project it often has not, because the gate was written before the phase — the professional move
is to publish the reading *first*, as a short list, and let people object to the reading rather than
to the verdict. Arguing about what "full" meant after the result is in is unwinnable, because by then
everybody has an interest.

**What degrades at scale.** A four-word gate over a four-day phase is recoverable by one person in an
afternoon. The same sentence over a twenty-team programme is where release criteria documents come
from, and they are long for a reason: every clause in them is a place two teams once disagreed.

**The failure that only shows with real traffic.** The reading drifts. This day reads *full* as every
case any day recorded; six months later the suite has four hundred cases and *full* silently starts
meaning the nightly subset, because that is the one that runs. Nobody decides this. The criteria file
is the only thing that would have caught it, which is why the reading belongs in code rather than in
prose.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Put the reading of the gate sentence at the top of
the verdict output, not in a document. Whoever reads `NOT green` in six months needs to see, on the
same screen, that green meant threshold 0.8 over every recorded case."*

**The interview question:** *"Your acceptance criterion says the tests must pass. What do you ask?"*
The answer that shows experience asks *which* tests, at what threshold, on which run, and then asks
who decides when those change — because the last one is the question that has an owner and the first
three only have answers.

## Check yourself

```bash
cd days/day-83-eval-suite-green/lab
uv run python c2_stub.py --lenient | tail -4
```

Two words of that output are a verdict and the rest is data. Say which two, and say what a reader who
saw only those two would believe about this desk.

Now take the gate sentence apart yourself, without looking at the table above. Write down four
questions the six words do not answer, then compare. If you found a fifth, it is probably a real one.

**Out loud, without scrolling up:** what does *full* mean in this repository today, and what will it
quietly start meaning when the suite has four hundred cases?

# 5.1 — What a registry costs

## One-line answer

Every check in this day is arithmetic on bytes already in memory and costs nothing to run for ever —
what a registry costs is **upkeep**, and the bill is paid in stale rows, refused legitimate peers and
somebody's attention every time a key changes.

## The story

The school's emergency contact list.

Every parent filled a form in June. Name, two phone numbers, an alternate address, who may collect the
child. The office keeps it in a folder and it is, in principle, exactly the information you would want
on the day you need it.

By February a fifth of it is wrong. People changed numbers, moved, changed jobs. Nobody did anything
careless — the form was filled in honestly and then the world moved on, which is what the world does.
And the failure is asymmetric in a way that matters: the wrong numbers are not distributed evenly
across a normal day, they surface all at once on the one afternoon somebody needs to make ten calls.

Collecting the list was a morning's work. Keeping it true is a job with no completion, and it is the
part nobody staffed.

## The idea in plain language

Separate the two costs, because they behave completely differently.

**Running the checks is free.** Verifying an Ed25519 signature is on the order of a hundred
microseconds; a dictionary lookup is a rounding error on that. Everything sections 1 through 4 measured — resolution, pinning,
rotation, revocation, capabilities — could run on every request for ever and never appear in a
profile. There is no performance argument for skipping any of it, and it is worth knowing that clearly,
because "we did not check for performance reasons" is a sentence people say.

**Keeping the registry true is the whole bill**, and it is paid in the following places:

| What | When it is paid | What it costs if unpaid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| adding a peer | onboarding | a legitimate peer is refused; somebody is paged |
| updating a key | every rotation | that peer is refused until noticed — part [3.1](../03-keys-change/3.1-the-cheques-already-written.md) |
| marking a revocation | offboarding | a withdrawn peer keeps working — part [3.2](../03-keys-change/3.2-the-card-of-someone-who-left.md) |
| narrowing a grant | never, by default | the union of part [4.2](../04-verified-is-not-authorised/4.2-the-shared-login.md) |
| confirming a row is still true | never, by default | a stale row nothing reports |

Look at the last two rows. **Three of the five have an event that prompts them and two do not.** Adding,
updating and revoking are triggered by something happening; narrowing a grant and confirming a row are
triggered by nothing at all, which is precisely why they are the two that rot.

That is the shape of a registry's real cost: not compute, not storage, but the two maintenance tasks
that have no natural trigger.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because this repository has already met this failure in another form and it has a name. Day 82's
quarantine list had five switched-off checks, the oldest two hundred and three days, every entry added
for a good reason and none of them ever a decision to stop checking. A registry entry that nobody
revisits is the same object: added for a reason, correct on the day, and load-bearing for years
afterwards on the strength of that one afternoon.

And because Phase 14's gate is about to be claimed. "A2A peer verified" is true of a registry the day
it is written; whether it is true in March depends entirely on whether either of those two untriggered
tasks ever happens.

## The mechanism

The cost of the checks, measured rather than asserted:

```bash
cd days/day-90-identity-and-registry/lab
uv run python -c "
import timeit, _ids
c = _ids.card('refund-desk', ('read_ticket',), _ids.REFUND, 'agent:refund-desk.example')
pub = _ids.REFUND.public_key()
n = 2000
t = timeit.timeit(lambda: _ids.signature_valid(c.payload(), c.signatures[0], pub), number=n)
print(f'{n} verifications: {t:.3f}s total, {t/n*1e6:.1f} microseconds each')
print(f'registry lookup:  {timeit.timeit(lambda: _ids.REGISTRY.get(\"agent:refund-desk.example\"), number=n)/n*1e9:.0f} nanoseconds')
"
```

**Line by line:**

- `timeit` over two thousand iterations rather than one, because a single call is dominated by
  whatever else the interpreter was doing.
- The verification includes the JSON serialisation of the payload, which is honest — that is what a
  real verifier does, and on a small card it is a meaningful share of the total.
- The registry lookup is timed separately and reported in nanoseconds, because reporting it in the same
  unit as the signature would round it to zero and hide the point that the two costs are orders apart.

Measured on 2026-09-07:

```text
2000 verifications: 0.207s total, 103.5 microseconds each
registry lookup:  56 nanoseconds
```

**About a hundred microseconds**, steady to within a few percent across repeated runs. At that price a
peer could be re-verified on every call of a conversation and the cost would still be invisible next
to a single model request, which takes something like ten thousand times as long — and that is the
argument for checking per call rather than per session, made in the currency people actually object in.

The lookup at fifty-six nanoseconds is three orders of magnitude cheaper again, which is the shape to
remember: **the expensive part of identity is the cryptography, the cryptography is nearly free, and
the lookup is free squared.**

It is worth knowing where that hundred microseconds actually goes, because the intuitive answer is
wrong. Timing the two halves separately on the same machine, on the same day:

```text
whole check 105.0 us | serialise 3.3 us | ed25519 verify 101.0 us
```

**The cryptography is essentially all of it**, and the JSON serialisation everybody assumes is the
overhead is three percent. That matters for one practical reason: an optimisation aimed at the
serialisation buys nothing, and the only lever that would move this number is verifying fewer times or
in a faster binding. Neither is worth doing at a hundred microseconds, which is the actual conclusion.

## When it breaks

The cost model breaks by leaving out the runs nobody counted, and here they are not requests but
interruptions.

**A refused legitimate peer.** A rotation nobody propagated produces the refusal from part
[2.1](../02-the-registry/2.1-the-list-behind-the-counter.md):

```text
  REFUSED   registry expects gTl3Dqh9F19W, card was signed by 7UkoxijRwsbq
```

That message is a good one — it names both keys — and the cost is not the message, it is that a person
now has to find out which of the two is correct, from somebody at another organisation, before anything
works again.

**A stale row that refuses nothing.** No output at all. The entry is present, the key is right, the
grant is three capabilities wider than the peer needs, and everything works perfectly. This costs
nothing today and is the entire subject of part
[4.2](../04-verified-is-not-authorised/4.2-the-shared-login.md).

**The registry becoming a dependency.** When it stops being a file, resolution becomes a network call
and joins the list of things that can be down. The failure has no error text in this lab because this
lab's registry is a dict — which is exactly why the decision about what to do when the lookup fails
must be made now, while it cannot fail, rather than during the first incident when it can.

There is one number this day genuinely cannot supply and it should be said plainly: **how often
Sutra's peers will rotate**, which sets how much upkeep the two untriggered tasks actually amount to.
Nothing has been measured because Sutra has no peers yet. It is the first thing to instrument once
there is one.

## In production

**What a professional does.** Puts a date on every row and makes staleness a build failure rather than
a discovery. A `confirmed_on` field, a policy that says how old is too old, and a check in `./m check`
that fails on an expired row — that converts the untriggered maintenance task into a triggered one,
which is the only reliable fix. It is the same move Day 82 argued for with quarantine expiry, and it
works for the same reason.

**What degrades at scale.** Upkeep grows linearly with peers and the attention available does not. Ten
peers is a file somebody reads; two hundred is a system, with self-service registration, an approval
workflow, and an owner per entry — because the question "is this row still true" has to be asked of
somebody, and at two hundred rows the only person who can answer is whoever runs that peer.

**The failure that only shows with real traffic.** The fingerprint is too short. This lab compares
twelve base64url characters, which is fine when six keys are generated from fixed seeds and hostile
when an attacker gets to grind out a key whose fingerprint matches a prefix. In anything real, compare
the full public key or a full-length cryptographic hash of it — truncation is a display convenience and
must never be the comparison.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Add `confirmed_on` and fail the build at ninety days.
And compare full keys, not the twelve-character fingerprint — that is fine for the log line and it is
not a comparison I want in an `if`."*

**The interview question:** *"What does it cost to check every request against a registry?"* The answer
that shows experience gives the microseconds, dismisses them, and then says the real cost is the
maintenance of the data — followed by which maintenance tasks have a natural trigger and which do not.
The second half is the part that shows somebody has run one.

# 5.2 — What a real identity layer adds

## One-line answer

This lab resolves an id, matches a key, verifies a signature, honours a rotation, reads a revocation
and checks a capability — and the distance between that and something you would put in front of a
peer that can move money is nine items, the first two of which are an afternoon between them.

## The story

The bunch of keys handed to the new caretaker.

Eleven keys on a ring, and the man leaving spends five minutes on it. This one is the main gate. This
one is the terrace, and it sticks — lift the door. These two are the pump room and only one of them
works since they changed the lock. This one nobody knows.

He hands over the ring and the knowledge separately, and only the ring is written down anywhere.
Everything that makes the ring usable — which door, which trick, which key is dead — lives in a
five-minute conversation, and in a year it will have evaporated, leaving eleven keys and a caretaker
who tries them all.

The keys were never the hard part. The register that says what each one opens is the hard part, and it
was never written.

## The idea in plain language

The lab does six things properly and it is worth being precise about which, because the gap is the
subject.

It resolves by **id** rather than by name. It compares the presented key against the registry's
expectation before verifying anything. It keeps retired keys so history stays readable. It reads
`status` from the registry rather than from the card. It checks each call against a grant. And every
refusal carries a reason rather than a boolean.

Here is what goes on the ring.

**1. A date on every row, and a build that fails on a stale one.** Part
[5.1](5.1-what-a-registry-costs.md) found that two of five maintenance tasks have no trigger. A
`confirmed_on` field plus a check in `./m check` gives them one. This is first because it is the only
item that fixes a class of problem rather than an instance, and because Day 82 already proved the shape
works — an expiry turned a quarantine that only grew into one that has to be argued for.

**2. Compare full keys, never the fingerprint.** The lab compares twelve base64url characters, which is
a display convenience. An attacker who chooses their own key can grind a matching prefix. One line, and
the fingerprint stays for the log message.

**3. Validity windows on retired keys.** Part [3.1](../03-keys-change/3.1-the-cheques-already-written.md)
named this gap in its own text: `previous_keys` says *"this was once ours"*, not *"this was ours until
the seventh"*. Without the window, a key rotated **because it leaked** still verifies everything the
attacker signed while they had it.

**4. Revocation latency, measured and written down.** Part
[3.2](../03-keys-change/3.2-the-card-of-someone-who-left.md) established that revocation lives in the
relying party's list; the number that says how good it is, is how long after a revocation the peer is
actually refused. Today that number is zero because the registry is a dict in the same process. The
moment it is anything else, the number exists and should be published.

**5. An alert on a refused revoked peer.** Distinct from the refusal itself. A withdrawn peer
attempting to connect is one of the highest-signal events a system produces and it is usually logged at
the same level as a malformed URL.

**6. Per-deployment keys rather than per-service.** Part
[4.2](../04-verified-is-not-authorised/4.2-the-shared-login.md): a key shared by four replicas is one
identity, so one compromised replica cannot be revoked without stopping the other three.

**7. 🅿️ Parked — delegated authority on the request.** When a peer acts for somebody else, carry that
somebody's authority with the request instead of widening the peer's grant. This is the paper's
mechanism and part [papers/01](../../papers/01-speaks-for.md) builds it, but wiring it into Sutra needs
a decision about where a delegation statement is stored and who may issue one. Parked because it is a
design question, not an afternoon.

**8. 🅿️ Parked — federation.** Two registries that trust each other's vouching. Everything in this day
assumes one registry with one voucher, which part [5.3](5.3-the-one-road-into-town.md) is about.
Parked because Sutra has one peer at most until Phase 15.

**9. 🅿️ Parked — an audit trail of registry changes.** Who added this row, when, and against what
approval. A committed file gets this from `git log` for nothing, which is why item 9 is parked rather
than urgent — and it stops being free on the day the registry becomes a service.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because Day 92 is a full security review before this repository goes public, and this list is what that
review will read. An item that is written down and parked survives the review; an item that lives in
somebody's head does not.

And because items 1 and 2 are the two that get harder later. A `confirmed_on` field added today has a
true value for every row; added in a year it has a guessed value for every row, and a guessed freshness
date is worse than none because it silences the check.

## The mechanism

The list with what each costs and what it unblocks, so it can be argued with rather than admired:

| # | Item | Where it lands | Roughly | Unblocks |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | `confirmed_on` plus an expiry check | the registry and `./m check` | an hour | the two untriggered tasks |
| 2 | compare full keys | one line in the resolver | minutes | prefix-grinding |
| 3 | validity windows on retired keys | the registry and `rotate` | an hour | rotation after a compromise |
| 4 | measure revocation latency | wherever the registry lives | half a day | an honest number for item 5 |
| 5 | alert on a refused revoked peer | logging and alerting | an hour | detection |
| 6 | per-deployment keys | issuance and deployment | a day or more | attribution, partial revocation |
| 7 | 🅿️ delegated authority | not built | a design decision | acting on behalf of a user |
| 8 | 🅿️ federation | not built | needs a second registry | more than one organisation |
| 9 | 🅿️ registry audit trail | free while it is a file | — | answering "who added this" |

**Items 1 and 2 are an afternoon between them** and they are the two that stop the registry rotting.
Item 3 is the one a security reviewer will ask about first, because it is the difference between
rotation and containment.

Item 2's whole implementation, since it is the smallest and the most easily skipped:

```python
    if entry["public_key"] != key_id:
        return False, f"registry expects {entry['public_key']}, card was signed by {key_id}"
```

**Line by line:**

- `entry["public_key"]` and `key_id` are both twelve-character fingerprints today. Making this correct
  means both sides holding the full thirty-two public key bytes and comparing those; the fingerprint
  then appears only in the message.
- The comparison stays `!=` on bytes, which is the right operation. A constant-time compare is not
  needed here — this is a public value, and there is no secret to leak through timing.
- The message keeps both values, and that survives the change: an operator reading a refusal needs two
  short strings to compare by eye, not two long ones.

## When it breaks

The list breaks by being read as a backlog. Here is the composite it prevents, and every step of it has
appeared somewhere in this day:

```text
1. a registry is written and is correct
2. a peer rotates; the row is updated
3. a peer is withdrawn; the row is marked revoked
4. a grant is widened for a second workflow
5. nothing has a date, so nothing is ever revisited
6. two years later: rows for peers that no longer exist, grants nobody can justify,
   and one fingerprint that was pasted wrong and has been refusing a peer since March
```

Step six has three separate failures in it and **not one of them raises**. The dead rows are inert, the
grants are honoured, and the wrong fingerprint produces a refusal that looks like the peer's problem.

The one item on the list that would have caught all three is item 1, which is why it is item 1.

## In production

**What a professional does.** Items 1 and 2 this week, item 3 before any key is rotated in anger, and
items 7 to 9 written down where a reviewer can see them rather than built. The judgement being
exercised is that a parked item with a reason is a decision and an unlisted item is an oversight —
and Day 92's review is precisely the event that distinguishes them.

**What degrades at scale.** Every item on this list gets more valuable and more expensive as the peer
count grows, except item 9, which gets *harder*: a committed file gives you an audit trail for free and
a registry service has to be built with one. That is the moment to decide whether the file has really
stopped being enough, because the free audit trail is a large part of what it was buying.

**The failure that only shows with real traffic.** Everything in this day resolved a peer that was
present, well-formed and cooperative. A real peer sends a card with a field you did not expect, or two
signatures where you handle one, or the same id from two addresses at once. The A2A `signatures` field
is **repeated** — part [1.3](../01-what-an-identity-is/1.3-the-sticker-anyone-can-print.md) measured
that — and nothing in this lab handles more than `signatures[0]`. That is the first thing a real peer
will break.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Do items 1 and 2 this week; they are an afternoon.
Put 3 on the sprint, because the next rotation we do in a hurry will be one where a key leaked, and
today that means we keep honouring everything the leak signed. And handle more than one signature —
the field is repeated and we index `[0]`."*

**The interview question:** *"What would you add before trusting your identity layer?"* The answer that
shows experience starts with freshness — dates on rows and something that fails when they go stale —
rather than with more cryptography, because the cryptography in a system like this is almost never what
goes wrong.

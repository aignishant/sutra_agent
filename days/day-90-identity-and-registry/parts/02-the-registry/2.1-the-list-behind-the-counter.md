# 2.1 — The registry is a file

## One-line answer

A registry is not a service and not a protocol — it is a record **you** keep, mapping an identifier to
the key you expect, who vouched for it, what it may do and whether it is still active, and every one
of those four columns exists because something goes wrong without it.

## The story

The list of approved suppliers, taped inside the cupboard door behind the counter.

A dozen lines in biro. A name, a phone number, and against two of them a note: *"cash only"*, and
*"do not take delivery after 6"*. It has been amended in three different pens by two different people
and the fourth line is crossed out.

Nobody would call it a system. But when a van arrives and the driver says he is from the usual dairy,
somebody opens that cupboard, and the list does four things at once: it says which suppliers exist, it
says how to reach them, it carries the notes that stopped a problem happening twice, and — because the
fourth line is crossed out rather than erased — it says who **used** to be a supplier and is not any
more.

The last one is the column people forget to design, and it is the one that keeps the crossed-out
supplier's perfectly genuine invoice from being paid.

## The idea in plain language

The registry answers part [1.2](../01-what-an-identity-is/1.2-the-three-questions.md)'s second
question — *who says so* — and it does it by being a thing you control rather than a thing the peer
hands you. That is the entire architectural point. A card is evidence supplied by the party being
checked; a registry is evidence supplied by you.

An entry needs four columns and it is worth being able to say why each is there:

| Column | Answers | Without it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `public_key` | which key is the right one | a valid signature by any key is accepted (part [1.1](../01-what-an-identity-is/1.1-a-name-is-not-an-identity.md)) |
| `vouched_by` | who decided this, and when | nobody can audit how a peer got in |
| `capabilities` | what it may do | verified becomes a synonym for allowed (part [4.1](../04-verified-is-not-authorised/4.1-the-man-the-office-sent.md)) |
| `status` | whether it still counts | a withdrawn peer keeps working for ever (part [3.2](../03-keys-change/3.2-the-card-of-someone-who-left.md)) |

And the key that a registry is looked up by is the **identifier**, not the display name — part
[2.3](2.3-two-shops-one-name.md) is the measurement of what happens otherwise.

Two things a registry is deliberately not:

**Not a discovery mechanism.** Finding out that an agent exists and deciding to trust it are different
acts, and merging them means anything discoverable is trusted. A registry is the second act only.

**Not necessarily a server.** A committed file in the repository is a legitimate registry for a
handful of peers, and it has properties a service does not: it diffs, it is reviewed before it
changes, and `git log` over it is the audit trail for free. Section
[5](../05-in-production/5.1-what-a-registry-costs.md) says when that stops being enough.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because Sutra's peers are few and its blast radius is large. The desk can propose refunds and read
customer tickets; a handful of agents will ever talk to it; and the decision to add one is exactly the
kind of decision that should be a reviewed diff rather than a form somebody filled in.

And because it is the file that makes the rest of Phase 14 checkable. "A2A peer verified" needs a
definition of *which* peers, and that definition has to live somewhere a test can read.

## The mechanism

The whole registry, as this lab keeps it:

```python
REGISTRY: dict[str, dict] = {
    "agent:refund-desk.example": {
        "display_name": "refund-desk",
        "public_key": fingerprint(REFUND),
        "capabilities": ("read_ticket", "propose_refund"),
        "vouched_by": "agent:registry.sutra.example",
        "status": "active",
    },
    "agent:billing-bot.example": {
        "display_name": "billing-bot",
        "public_key": fingerprint(BILLING),
        "capabilities": ("read_ticket", "move_money"),
        "vouched_by": "agent:registry.sutra.example",
        "status": "revoked",
    },
    "agent:triage-desk.example": {
        "display_name": "triage-desk",
        "public_key": fingerprint(TRIAGE_NEW),
        "previous_keys": (fingerprint(TRIAGE_OLD),),
        "capabilities": ("read_ticket",),
        "vouched_by": "agent:registry.sutra.example",
        "status": "active",
    },
}
```

**Line by line:**

- The dict is keyed by an **identifier** in a URI-ish shape, `agent:<host>`, not by `refund-desk`. The
  identifier is chosen by whoever runs the registry and is never the peer's own display string.
- `display_name` is present and is never compared. It exists so a log line and an operator's console
  can show something readable, and part [2.3](2.3-two-shops-one-name.md) is what happens the day
  somebody compares it.
- `public_key` holds a fingerprint rather than the key itself, so this table stays readable and short.
  In a real registry it is the full key or a URL to it; the fingerprint is the comparison either way.
- `capabilities` is a tuple, not a list — a registry entry that code can append to is a registry entry
  that will be appended to.
- `status` is a string with more than two possible values rather than an `active: bool`. `"revoked"`
  and, later, `"suspended"` or `"pending"` are different operational states, and a boolean forces the
  second one to be invented somewhere else.
- `previous_keys` appears on exactly one entry, because only one peer has rotated. It is optional by
  design, and section [3](../03-keys-change/3.1-the-cheques-already-written.md) is the whole story of
  that field.
- `billing-bot` is in the table with `status: revoked` rather than being deleted from it. That is the
  crossed-out line in the story, and deleting it instead would make a withdrawn peer indistinguishable
  from an unknown one — two situations that need different alerts.

- The default arm: a known peer, right key, valid signature.

Measured on 2026-09-07:

```text
registry holds 3 agents

                name='refund-desk', signed by key gTl3Dqh9F19W

  ACCEPTED  vouched by agent:registry.sutra.example, capabilities ['read_ticket', 'propose_refund']
```

Three agents, one accepted, and the acceptance line carries **who vouched** and **what it may do** —
both read from the record rather than from the card the peer sent.

## When it breaks

A registry breaks in three ways and only one of them is a bug.

**The peer is not in it.** This is the registry working. It refuses, and it says why:

```text
  REFUSED   no registry entry for this id
```

The failure mode here is operational, not technical: a legitimate new peer is refused, somebody is
paged, and the pressure is to add an entry quickly. A registry whose entries can be added quickly is a
registry that will have entries nobody reviewed.

**The registry is wrong.** Somebody pasted the wrong fingerprint, or the peer rotated and told nobody.
This produces the same refusal with a different reason —

```text
  REFUSED   registry expects gTl3Dqh9F19W, card was signed by 7UkoxijRwsbq
```

— and the two must not be collapsed into one message, because *"we do not know you"* and *"we know you
and you look different"* have completely different responses.

**The registry is stale and nobody can tell.** This is the one with no error text. Every entry is
present, every lookup succeeds, and the `public_key` for one peer has been wrong since it rotated
last month — so that peer has been refused for weeks and the failure is a support ticket, not an
alarm. The mitigation is not in the registry at all: it is that entries carry the date they were last
confirmed, which nothing in this lab's table currently does, and which is the first item on part
[5.2](../05-in-production/5.2-what-a-real-identity-layer-adds.md)'s list.

## In production

**What a professional does.** Keeps the registry in version control for as long as it fits there, and
makes adding an entry a reviewed change. The review is the control; the file is just where the outcome
is written. When it outgrows a file — hundreds of peers, or entries that change faster than a release
— it becomes a service, and the two properties to preserve are that a change is attributable to a
person and that the previous state is recoverable.

**What degrades at scale.** Lookup latency and staleness, in that order and in tension. A file is
instant and updated slowly; a service is current and adds a network call to every request, so it gets
cached, and the cache lifetime becomes the revocation window. There is no configuration that avoids
this trade; there is only choosing it deliberately and writing down the number.

**The failure that only shows with real traffic.** The registry becomes a dependency of the thing it
protects. If a peer cannot be resolved because the registry is unreachable, does the desk refuse
everything or fall back to a cached copy? Both answers are defensible and the one nobody chose is a
`try/except` that returns `True`. Decide it while writing the resolver, not during the incident.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Add a `confirmed_on` date to every entry and fail
the build when one is older than the rotation policy. A registry with no freshness signal is a
registry that is wrong somewhere and nobody knows which row."*

**The interview question:** *"Where does your service store which peers it trusts?"* The answer that
shows experience names the storage, then names who can change it and how that change is reviewed —
because a registry anybody can write to is a registry that answers question two with the attacker's
own answer.

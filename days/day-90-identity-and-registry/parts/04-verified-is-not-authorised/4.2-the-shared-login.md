# 4.2 — One identity for many callers

## One-line answer

A registry entry names one key and one grant, so the moment several callers share that key the grant
widens to the union of everything any of them needs — and the entry stops describing a peer and starts
describing a category, at which point the capability list has stopped being a limit.

## The story

The shared login in the back office.

There is one account for the billing system. Everybody in the section uses it, because getting an
account of your own takes a fortnight and there are four people and a lot of work.

It functions. The section is not careless — they are careful people doing their job with the tool they
were given. And the day something is wrong with a record, the log says the account did it, at
eleven in the morning, which is a fact about a login and not about a person. Nobody is accusing
anybody; there is simply nothing to accuse anybody with.

The other half is quieter and worse. The account has every permission any of the four ever needed,
because each time somebody hit a wall the permission was added to the account. Nobody ever asked for
too much. The account ended up with everything anyway.

## The idea in plain language

Part [4.1](4.1-the-man-the-office-sent.md) established that the registry entry carries the grant.
This part is what happens to that grant over time, and the pressure that widens it.

An entry is a pair: one key, one capability list. That pairing carries an assumption which is
invisible until it is false — that the key belongs to **one thing with one job**.

The assumption breaks in ways that are all reasonable:

**Several instances share a key.** The peer scales to four replicas and they are all given the same
credential, because that is by far the easiest way to deploy. Day 86's container work makes this the
default rather than the exception.

**Several jobs share an agent.** One peer agent grows a second responsibility. Its entry needs the
union of both jobs' capabilities, and now every call it makes is authorised against a list neither job
needed in full.

**Several people share an agent.** A peer that acts on behalf of whoever asked it. Its grant must cover
the most privileged user it will ever act for, and its least privileged user's requests are authorised
against that same list.

All three produce the same shape: **the grant becomes a union, and a union is only ever an upper
bound.** Once an entry is a union, the capability check still runs, still refuses things outside the
list, and no longer describes what any individual caller should be able to do.

The third case is where the paper this day cites has something specific to say, and it is worth
reading before the fix: an agent acting for a user is one principal speaking for another, and the right
answer is not a wider grant but a **narrower, delegated one**. Part
[papers/01](../../papers/01-speaks-for.md) is that argument.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because Sutra's desk acts on behalf of customers, and a peer that hands it work is acting on behalf of
somebody too. The moment a peer says *"refund ticket 4688"*, the question of whose authority that
request carries is live, and the registry entry as designed has no field for it — it says what the
*peer* may do, not what the peer may do *for this requester*.

And because the widening happens without a decision. Nobody proposes giving a peer more than it needs.
The union arrives one justified addition at a time, which is why the countermeasure has to be a
periodic review rather than a moment of good judgement.

## The mechanism

The registry entry, seen for what it is:

```python
    "agent:refund-desk.example": {
        "public_key": fingerprint(REFUND),
        "capabilities": ("read_ticket", "propose_refund"),
        ...
    },
```

**Line by line:**

- One `public_key`. Whatever holds that private key **is** this entry — four replicas sharing it are
  one identity as far as every check in this day is concerned, and no amount of care elsewhere
  separates them.
- One `capabilities` tuple, flat. There is nowhere in this structure to say *"`propose_refund` only for
  tickets this peer opened"*, so any such rule has to live in the tool instead — which is fine, and is
  a decision worth making knowingly rather than discovering.
- The tuple is immutable, which stops code from widening the grant at runtime. It does nothing about
  widening it in a pull request, which is how it actually widens.

And the union arriving, which is not in the lab because it is not a code path — it is a diff history:

```text
  2026-03  ("read_ticket",)
  2026-05  ("read_ticket", "propose_refund")            triage handover work
  2026-07  ("read_ticket", "propose_refund", "read_customer")   needed for context
  2026-09  ("read_ticket", "propose_refund", "read_customer", "move_money")
```

**Line by line:**

- Each line is a justified change and each was reviewed. There is no row here anybody would reject.
- The grant grows monotonically because there is no event that removes a capability. The triage
  handover work finished in June and `propose_refund` stayed, because nothing prompts anybody to take
  it away.
- The last row is the one a reviewer should stop at, and by then the entry has four capabilities and
  the reviewer's context is "we are adding one more".

The measurement this lab *can* make is the consequence — one entry, one grant, and a call outside it:

```bash
cd days/day-90-identity-and-registry/lab
uv run python authz.py; echo "exit: $?"
```

**Line by line:**

- The same command as part [4.1](4.1-the-man-the-office-sent.md), read for a different reason: the
  grant is the entry's, so every caller sharing that key gets exactly this list.

Measured on 2026-09-07:

```text
granted:   ['propose_refund', 'read_ticket']
  ...
  move_money        False      REFUSED
  calls outside the grant that were allowed: 0
```

That refusal is protecting the boundary of the **entry**. It says nothing about which of several
callers behind that key made the request, and if `move_money` had been in the list for any of them it
would have been in the list for all of them.

## When it breaks

There is no failing command in this part, and that is the finding rather than a gap: **this failure
produces no red output anywhere.** Every check passes, every grant is honoured, and the system's
behaviour is exactly as configured. What has gone wrong is that the configuration stopped meaning what
it appeared to mean.

The two symptoms, both of which show up outside the code:

**Attribution is gone.** The audit log records `agent:refund-desk.example` and that identifier now
covers four replicas, two jobs and any number of end users. The question *"who asked for this refund"*
has no answer in the log and cannot be given one retroactively.

**The grant no longer bounds anything.** The capability list is the union, so the check refuses only
what **none** of the callers needed — which, as the union grows, converges on refusing nothing.

The composite is slow and entirely made of good decisions:

```text
1. one peer, one key, two capabilities, correctly scoped
2. the peer scales out; the credential is copied to every replica
3. a second team's workflow is added to the same peer
4. its capabilities are added to the entry, each reviewed
5. the entry now grants the union of two jobs
6. the capability check still runs and now refuses nothing either job does
```

No step is wrong. Step two is standard deployment practice; steps three and four are how software
grows. And the end state is a capability list that a reviewer will read as a limit while it is
functioning as an inventory.

## In production

**What a professional does.** Gives each *deployment* its own key, not each *service* — so replicas are
distinguishable and one can be revoked without stopping the rest. And when a peer acts for someone
else, carries that somebody's identity in the request rather than widening the peer's grant to cover
them: the peer's own grant stays narrow, and the authority for the specific action arrives with the
specific request. That is delegation, and it is exactly the mechanism the paper part builds.

**What degrades at scale.** Key distribution becomes the hard problem. Per-replica keys mean an
issuing process, a rotation schedule and somewhere for each replica to hold its own secret — which is
real work, and is why shared credentials survive. The honest position is that a shared key is a
deliberate trade with a stated cost, not an oversight; write down what attribution you gave up.

**The failure that only shows with real traffic.** The union is discovered during an incident. Somebody
asks which peers can move money, the answer is a list, and one of them is a peer whose *primary* job
has nothing to do with money — it acquired the capability in July for a workflow that no longer runs.
Nothing detects this except a review that asks, per capability, *who has this and why*.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"This entry has four capabilities and it started with
one. Before adding a fifth, tell me which of the existing four are still used — and if we cannot answer
that from the logs, that is the thing to fix first."*

**The interview question:** *"Your service calls another service on behalf of a user. What identity does
it use?"* The answer that shows experience distinguishes the service's own identity from the user's
authority, and notices that using only the former forces the service's grant to cover every user it
will ever act for. The follow-up worth expecting is how you stop a compromised service from acting for
users who never asked it to.

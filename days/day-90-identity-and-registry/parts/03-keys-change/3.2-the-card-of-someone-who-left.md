# 3.2 — 💥 The signature that stays valid for ever

## One-line answer

A withdrawn peer's signature verifies exactly as well as it did the day it was issued, and always
will — so revocation is the one identity fact that cannot live in the credential, and a resolver that
checks the signature and stops will admit a peer whose access was removed months ago.

## The story

💥 **The ID card of somebody who left.**

🎬 **The scene.** An office building with a card reader on the door. An employee resigns, works their
notice, has the farewell lunch, and goes.

😬 **The naive fix.** On the last day somebody says the card should be handed back. It usually is. The
card is genuine, it was issued by the company, the photograph matches, and the hologram is real.

💥 **Why it fails.** One is not handed back — left in a coat pocket, or in a drawer, or simply
forgotten in the way that things are on a last day. Three months later it opens the door, because
**nothing about the card ever changed**. It was genuine when it was issued and it is genuine now.
Genuineness is a property of the card, and *employment* is a property of a list upstairs that the door
does not consult.

💡 **The insight.** Checking the card harder does not help. There is no amount of scrutiny that
reveals a resignation, because the resignation is not in the card and could never have been put there
— it happened afterwards. **The only thing that catches it is a list the door checks, kept by
somebody who knows.**

## The idea in plain language

Everything so far in this day has been checkable from two things: the document the peer sent, and a
value you stored. Revocation is not like that, and the difference is worth being precise about.

A signature is a mathematical fact about a document and a key. Once true, it is true permanently.
There is no expiry inside it, no way for the signer to retract it, and no operation anybody can perform
that makes a valid signature invalid. That permanence is the property that makes signatures useful —
it is exactly why an archived approval still verifies in part
[3.1](3.1-the-cheques-already-written.md) — and it is why revocation has to be implemented somewhere
else entirely.

So revocation is a **negative fact held by the relying party**. Not "this credential is bad" but "we
no longer accept this peer". It cannot be derived, only looked up.

Three consequences follow, and each is a design decision:

**The check is a separate step.** After the signature verifies and after the key matches the registry,
there is a third question — *is this entry still active* — with its own answer and its own refusal.

**Deleting the entry is the wrong implementation.** A deleted row makes a revoked peer
indistinguishable from an unknown one, and those need different responses: an unknown peer is probably
a misconfiguration, a revoked peer trying to connect is a security event.

**The check's freshness is the exposure window.** However long your copy of the registry is stale, that
is how long a revoked peer keeps working. This is the number that a cached registry trades away, and it
should be written down rather than discovered.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because `billing-bot` in this repository's registry has `move_money` in its capability list. Whatever
reason it was withdrawn for — the team disbanded, the key leaked, the integration was retired — it is a
peer with the most dangerous capability Sutra grants, and it has a valid signature that will verify
until the end of time.

And because this is where Phase 10's security work meets Phase 14's openness. Every containment
mechanism built in Days 66 through 72 assumed the caller was this repository's own code. Revocation is
the first control that exists purely because a caller might have been trusted once and should not be
now.

## The mechanism

The whole difference is one line in the resolver:

```python
    sig_ok = signature_valid(presented.payload(), presented.signatures[0], KEYS[key_id])
    key_ok = entry["public_key"] == key_id
    accepted = sig_ok and key_ok and (signature_only or entry["status"] == "active")
```

**Line by line:**

- `sig_ok` is question one from part
  [1.2](../01-what-an-identity-is/1.2-the-three-questions.md): the document was signed by the key it
  names. It is `True` for a revoked peer and there is no version of this check that is not.
- `key_ok` is question two: the registry names that key for this id. Also `True` — revocation did not
  change the peer's key, and expecting it to is a common and wrong intuition. The peer still holds
  exactly the key we recorded.
- `entry["status"] == "active"` is the third clause, and it reads a field that exists nowhere in the
  material the peer supplied. That asymmetry is the point: the first two clauses examine the peer's
  evidence, and the third examines ours.
- `signature_only or ...` short-circuits the third clause in the ablation arm. One `or` is the entire
  distance between a resolver that catches this and one that does not.
- The three clauses are combined with `and` in question order, so the reason a peer was refused is
  recoverable by looking at which clause was false — and the script prints all three rather than the
  conjunction.

Run it with the status check in place:

```bash
cd days/day-90-identity-and-registry/lab
uv run python revoke.py; echo "exit: $?"
```

**Line by line:**

- No flag: signature, then key, then status.

Measured on 2026-09-07:

```text
peer:   agent:billing-bot.example
status in the registry: revoked
resolver checks: signature, then status

  signature verifies                True
  key matches the registry entry    True
  status is active                  False

  verdict: refused
  the withdrawn peer was refused on a fact that lives only in the registry
exit: 0
```

Look at the three rows. **Two of the three checks pass.** Everything the peer presented is impeccable.
The refusal comes entirely from a field the peer has no access to and no knowledge of.

## When it breaks

Remove the third clause — which is what a resolver written around "verify the signature" looks like:

```bash
uv run python revoke.py --signature-only; echo "exit: $?"
```

Measured on 2026-09-07:

```text
resolver checks: signature only

  signature verifies                True
  key matches the registry entry    True

  verdict: ACCEPTED
  a withdrawn peer was admitted and may read_ticket, move_money
  its signature was never the problem: it is still perfectly valid, and always will be
exit: 1
```

**`may read_ticket, move_money`.** A peer whose access was withdrawn is holding the capability to move
money, and every check that ran returned `True`.

There is no error text, no anomaly, and nothing in any log that distinguishes this connection from a
legitimate one — because in every respect except the one that was not checked, it *is* legitimate.
That is the defining property of this failure: it does not look like an attack, it looks like the
system working.

The composite is the one every organisation has walked at least once:

```text
1. a peer is onboarded and its key recorded
2. a resolver is written: verify the signature, look up the key, accept
3. it works, and it keeps working
4. the peer is decommissioned; its registry row is marked revoked
5. nothing reads that field, so nothing changes
6. months later the peer's credentials are still honoured
```

Step five is the failure and step two is where it was written. Nobody removed a check; the check was
never added, because at step two there was nothing to revoke and the field did not exist yet.

The variant that is worse still: at step four the row is **deleted** rather than marked. Now the
resolver's "unknown peer" branch fires, the peer is refused, and everything looks fine — until
somebody has to answer whether a specific withdrawn agent ever tried to reconnect, and the record that
would say so was removed as part of withdrawing it.

## In production

**What a professional does.** Treats revocation as an operational capability with a measured latency,
not a boolean. The questions that have answers in a working system are: how long after a revocation is
the peer actually refused, who can revoke, and does a revoked peer's attempt raise an alert. The third
is the one most often missing — a refused revoked peer is one of the highest-signal events a system
can produce, and it is usually logged at the same level as a typo in a URL.

**What degrades at scale.** The check becomes a network call and gets cached, and the cache lifetime
becomes the revocation window. This is the same trade part [2.1](../02-the-registry/2.1-the-list-behind-the-counter.md)
named, arriving with teeth: a five-minute cache is a five-minute window in which a compromised peer
still works, and that number should be chosen by someone who knows what the peer can do rather than by
whoever tuned the cache.

**The failure that only shows with real traffic.** Long-lived connections. Revocation checked at
connection time and never again means a session opened before the revocation runs until it closes —
and an agent-to-agent session may be long. The mitigation is to check per request rather than per
connection, which is the same conclusion part
[4.1](../04-verified-is-not-authorised/4.1-the-man-the-office-sent.md) reaches from the authorisation
side, and for the same underlying reason: facts checked once are facts that go stale.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Nothing reads `status`. Add it to the resolver, log
a refused revoked peer at warning level with the id, and tell me the number: from `revoke` to
`refused`, how long?"*

**The interview question:** *"How do you revoke a credential?"* The answer that shows experience begins
by saying you cannot — the credential stays valid, permanently — and moves the question to the relying
party's list, its freshness, and what happens to sessions already open. The answer that does not says
"we rotate the key", which is part [3.1](3.1-the-cheques-already-written.md)'s subject and does nothing
about a peer that still holds the old one.

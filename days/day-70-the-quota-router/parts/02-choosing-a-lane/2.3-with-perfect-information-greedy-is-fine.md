# 2.3 — With perfect information, greedy is fine

## One-line answer

Measured over 270 requests by a single decider with live counters, the crude "first capable lane"
policy serves **258** and the clever headroom-sampling one serves **251** — so the sophistication
this day is building buys nothing at all until the information stops being perfect.

## The story

You are doing the week's shopping for the house, alone, with the list in your hand.

You walk down the aisles taking things off the shelves. When you reach the rice you take the bag at
the front. You do not stand there working out which bag leaves the shelf best arranged for the next
customer, and you do not go back and rearrange your trolley when you get to the flour. You just take
the next thing.

At the till, everything on the list is in the trolley. A more thoughtful strategy could not have done
better, because there was nothing to be thoughtful *about*: you were the only person taking things,
you could see every shelf, and nothing moved while you were deciding.

Now imagine the same shop on the first morning of a festival, with forty other people working from
similar lists. Suddenly which shelf you walk to first matters enormously. Nothing about your list
changed. What changed is that you are no longer the only one deciding, and the shelves are no longer
the way they looked when you set off.

## The idea in plain language

A **greedy** policy takes the best thing available right now, with no thought for later. It is the
simplest thing that could work, and in scheduling it has a reputation for being naive.

That reputation is mostly undeserved when two conditions hold:

1. **One decider.** Nothing else is consuming the resource between your decision and your action.
2. **Perfect information.** What you read is true at the moment you act on it.

Under those two conditions, greedy is not just adequate — it is often optimal, and reasoning about
why is worth doing. If you can see the true state and nobody can change it before you commit, then
taking the roomiest capable lane cannot strand work that a different choice would have served,
because you will get to re-decide with equally perfect information on the very next request.

This part exists because it would be dishonest to build the machinery of
[2.4](2.4-everyone-read-the-same-board.md) and the paper without first showing that on the obvious
benchmark, **it does not help.** A curriculum that only measures the case where its preferred answer
wins has taught advocacy rather than engineering.

The four policies being compared are worth naming precisely:

- **first** — the first capable, warm lane in declaration order. No headroom comparison at all beyond
  the filter.
- **best** — the capable lane with the most headroom. The obvious "smart" policy.
- **random** — a capable lane picked from a fixed sequence, ignoring headroom.
- **two** — sample two capable lanes, keep the one with more headroom. The paper's rule, taught in
  [2.4](2.4-everyone-read-the-same-board.md) and in
  [the paper part](../../papers/01-the-power-of-two-choices.md).

## Why Sutra needs it

Sutra will run one router in one process for a long time before it ever runs several. During that
period, everything in this section is optional, and knowing that is worth more than the policy is.

It also sets up the only honest reason to prefer the sampling policy. If someone asks *why not just
take the emptiest lane*, "it is better" is the wrong answer, because on this benchmark it is not.
The right answer names the condition: it is better **when your information is stale**, which is
exactly what happens the moment there is more than one worker — and
[6.1](../06-in-production/6.1-one-till-two-cashiers.md) is why there will be.

## The mechanism

`lab/headroom.py` runs one night's backlog — every open ticket classified, most drafted, a few
reasoned about — through each policy in turn. One decider, sequential, live counters read fresh on
every request.

```python
def pick_first(lanes: list[Lane], skill: str, rng: list[int]) -> Lane | None:
    """The first capable lane in declaration order. The policy everyone writes first."""
    options = usable(lanes, skill)
    return options[0] if options else None


def pick_best(lanes: list[Lane], skill: str, rng: list[int]) -> Lane | None:
    """The single lane with the most headroom, recomputed every call."""
    options = usable(lanes, skill)
    return max(options, key=lambda ln: ln.headroom()) if options else None
```

**Line by line:**

- Both take `rng` even though neither uses it. The four policies share one signature so the driver
  can select them from a dict without special cases, and an unused parameter is a smaller price than
  a branch per policy in the measurement loop.
- `usable(...)` is the capability-and-headroom filter from
  [2.2](2.2-the-lane-that-cannot-do-the-job.md), applied identically in all four. Only the choice
  *among survivors* differs, which is what makes the comparison about policy rather than about
  filtering.
- `pick_best` recomputes `headroom()` on every call rather than caching. That is what "perfect
  information" means operationally, and it is the assumption this part is testing.

The workload is generated rather than random, so every policy sees the same arrivals:

```python
def work() -> list[tuple[float, str]]:
    """The backlog: classify every ticket, draft most, reason about a few."""
    out: list[tuple[float, str]] = []
    for i in range(TICKETS):
        at = i * (SPAN / TICKETS)
        out.append((at, "classify"))
        if i % 2 == 0:
            out.append((at + 0.4, "draft"))
        if i % 12 == 0:
            out.append((at + 0.8, "reason"))
    return sorted(out)
```

**Line by line:**

- `i % 2` and `i % 12` produce the volume asymmetry
  [2.2](2.2-the-lane-that-cannot-do-the-job.md) tabled: lots of classification, half as much
  drafting, a little reasoning. Deterministic modular arithmetic rather than sampling, so the
  workload is identical on every machine.
- The `+ 0.4` and `+ 0.8` offsets put the three requests for one ticket at slightly different
  instants. Without them all three would land on the same timestamp and the sliding windows would see
  an artificial burst that the arrival pattern does not intend.
- `sorted(...)` interleaves the three streams into one arrival order, which is what the router
  actually faces.

Run it:

```bash
cd days/day-70-the-quota-router/lab
uv run python headroom.py --capacity
uv run python headroom.py
```

**Line by line:**

- `--capacity` first, because a throughput comparison is meaningless until you know what the fleet
  could do at best. It multiplies each lane's `rpm` by the span in minutes, caps that by `rpd`, and
  sums.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
what the fleet can serve in 300s, if nothing is wasted

  gemini      rpm  10  rpd    250   ->   50
  groq        rpm  30  rpd   1000   ->  150
  openrouter  rpm  20  rpd     50   ->   50
  ollama      rpm   6  rpd 100000   ->   30

  fleet ceiling 280, backlog 270
```

The backlog is 270 against a ceiling of 280 — deliberately tight, at ninety-six per cent of capacity,
because a workload well under capacity makes every policy look identical and teaches nothing.

Now the four policies:

```text
270 requests, four policies, identical arrivals

  policy    served  dropped   per-lane
  first        258       12   gemini=50  groq=150  openrouter=50  ollama=8
  best         255       15   gemini=44  groq=142  openrouter=50  ollama=19
  random       251       19   gemini=50  groq=124  openrouter=50  ollama=27
  two          251       19   gemini=47  groq=130  openrouter=50  ollama=24
```

**The crudest policy wins.** `first` serves 258; `best` serves 255; the two policies that sample
serve 251 each. The differences are small — seven requests out of 270, under three per cent — but
they point the wrong way for the story this day is telling, and that is worth understanding rather
than explaining away.

The reason is in the per-lane columns. `first` packs Groq to its full 150 and Gemini to its full 50,
using the big lanes completely before touching Ollama at all — it uses Ollama only 8 times. The
sampling policies spread work onto Ollama early (24 and 27 times), and Ollama's ceiling is **six a
minute**, the tightest in the fleet. Work sent there occupies a scarce slot that a bigger lane could
have absorbed instantly. Spreading is not free when the lanes are unequal.

So the honest finding is a pair:

- With one decider and live counters, **greedy packing beats spreading** on a heterogeneous fleet,
  because spreading pushes work onto lanes with tight ceilings.
- Every policy drops something, because 270 requests do not fit through a ceiling of 280 once the
  capability constraints from [2.2](2.2-the-lane-that-cannot-do-the-job.md) are applied. Routing
  decided *who* was starved. It did not decide *whether*.

## When it breaks

The break is not in the code, it is in the conclusion — and taking the wrong lesson from this table
is the failure this part is here to prevent. Someone reading only these numbers would delete the
sampling policy and ship `first`.

Here is the assumption, made visible. Ask `pick_best` for a decision and act on it *later* rather than
immediately:

```python
from _lane import Clock, fleet

clock = Clock(0.0)
lanes = fleet(clock)
chosen = max((ln for ln in lanes if ln.can("classify")), key=lambda ln: ln.headroom())
print("chosen on a fresh fleet:", chosen.name, "headroom", chosen.headroom())
for _ in range(30):
    chosen.call()
print("after 30 other requests landed there:", chosen.headroom())
```

**Line by line:**

- The decision is made once, at the top, exactly as `pick_best` makes it.
- The thirty calls stand in for other workers acting on the *same* decision, which is what happens
  when several processes read one snapshot.
- The final print asks whether the lane that was chosen still has the property it was chosen for.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
chosen on a fresh fleet: groq headroom 30
after 30 other requests landed there: 0
```

The lane was chosen because it had the most headroom in the fleet, and by the time the thirtieth
request arrives it has none. Nothing about the decision was wrong when it was made. It was wrong when
it was **used**, and every worker that read that snapshot made the identical choice.

That is the condition this part's result depends on, now broken. [2.4](2.4-everyone-read-the-same-board.md)
measures what it costs.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead.** They start with `first` and a comment saying why. A named,
ordered preference list — try Groq, then Gemini, then OpenRouter — is trivially debuggable, produces
reproducible traces, and on a single-process system performs as well as anything clever. The
sophistication is added when the deployment stops being single-process, and it is added with a
measurement that justifies it, not in anticipation.

**What degrades at scale.** Greedy packing has one property that is bad at scale, and it is not
throughput: it concentrates. `first` sends 150 of 258 requests to a single provider, so a fault in
that provider takes out more than half the fleet's work at once. The sampling policies spread the
blast radius as a side effect. When the failure being optimised against is a provider outage rather
than a quota ceiling, that changes the ranking.

**The failure that only appears with real traffic.** This whole benchmark assumes requests arrive one
at a time and are served instantly. Real model calls take seconds and overlap, so several are always
in flight, and a lane's headroom at the moment a response arrives is not what it was when the request
left. The single-decider assumption fails not because you added workers, but because you added
**concurrency inside one worker** — which an `asyncio` gather does for free.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"The benchmark shows `first` winning, so ship
`first`. But write down the assumption next to it: one decider, counters read fresh. The day we run
two replicas or fan out with `asyncio.gather`, that assumption is gone and this measurement no longer
applies. Put the condition in a comment so the next person does not have to rediscover it."*

**The interview question:** *"Is a smarter load-balancing policy always worth it?"* An answer with
evidence: *"No, and I measured it on my own fleet. With one decider reading live counters, taking the
first capable provider served 258 of 270 requests and sampling-based policies served 251 — the crude
one won, because spreading pushed work onto the provider with the tightest per-minute ceiling. The
clever policy only earns its place when the information is stale, which is what happens as soon as
there is more than one worker deciding from the same snapshot. So the question I ask first is how
many deciders there are, not which algorithm is better."*

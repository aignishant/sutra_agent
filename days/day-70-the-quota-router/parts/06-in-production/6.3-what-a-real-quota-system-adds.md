# 6.3 — What a real quota system adds

## One-line answer

The router this day builds is a **client-side estimator**, and everything a real quota system has that
it does not — token buckets, admission control, priority, fairness between callers, a shared
authoritative counter — exists because the thing being protected changes from *your own allowance* to
*somebody else's service*.

## The story

A small clinic books appointments in a paper diary at reception. It works. The receptionist knows the
doctors, knows that Thursday afternoons are quiet, knows that Mrs Iyer will need twenty minutes rather
than ten, and the diary has never once double-booked anybody.

The hospital across town cannot do this, and not because its receptionists are worse. It has eleven
booking points, three of which are online at two in the morning. It has to hold a slot while somebody
decides, and release it if they wander off. It has to let an urgent case take a slot from a routine
one, and be able to say afterwards why. It has to answer *how many appointments did cardiology get
last month* to somebody who is deciding next year's budget.

None of that makes the paper diary wrong. It makes it a diary — correct for one desk, and unable to
answer questions that only exist once there are eleven.

## The idea in plain language

This day built something specific: a router that keeps its own counters, picks the lane with the most
headroom, marks refused lanes cold, and refuses honestly when everything is dry. It is worth being
exact about what that is, because the name "rate limiter" covers two quite different things and the
difference decides which properties you need.

**What this day built is a client-side estimator.** It protects *you* from spending an allowance you
were given. Its counters are a *model* of somebody else's meter, its failure mode is a 429 you could
have avoided, and it is allowed to be approximate because the provider is the one enforcing.

**A quota system proper protects a service from its callers.** Its counter is authoritative — it *is*
the meter — its failure mode is either letting a caller through who should have been stopped or
stopping one who should not, and it is not allowed to be approximate, because there is nothing behind
it to catch the mistake.

Almost everything in the list below follows from that one difference:

| What it adds | Why an estimator does not need it | What it protects against |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Token bucket / leaky bucket** | our windows are the provider's windows; we copy their shape | bursts, while allowing a controlled amount of them |
| **Authoritative shared state** | part 6.1 showed we need *some* sharing, but ours can lag | two callers being admitted against one allowance |
| **Admission control** — refuse at the door, before work starts | we refuse at the routing decision, which is already the door | queues of work that can never be served |
| **Priority classes** | every ticket is equal here | an urgent request stuck behind a batch job |
| **Fairness between callers** | there is one caller | one tenant consuming everybody's allowance |
| **Cost-based accounting** — tokens, not requests | Addendum 02's limits are per request | one enormous request costing what a hundred small ones do |

Read the last row twice, because it is the one that will bite this repository first. Sutra's budget is
denominated in **requests** per day, which is what Addendum 02 says and what every counter in this day
implements. Real providers also meter **tokens**, and a request is not a unit of cost — part
[1.1](../01-what-headroom-is/1.1-a-ceiling-is-a-window-not-a-number.md)'s ceiling is one of several,
and the router in this day cannot see the others.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because a reader who finishes this day thinking they have built a rate limiter will build the wrong
thing next time. What they have built is good, and it is bounded: it is correct for one process
holding four counters against limits somebody else enforces, and part
[6.1](6.1-one-till-two-cashiers.md) already showed the first assumption failing.

It also decides what goes in `sutra/routing.py` and what does not. The build brief asks for a plugin
that estimates and routes. It does not ask for a token bucket, and it should not, because a token
bucket in a client-side estimator is complexity that buys nothing you can verify — the provider's
actual algorithm is not yours to know, and copying its shape from a guess is worse than counting
requests in a window you can observe.

## The mechanism

There is no new code here. The mechanism is a decision about scope, and the honest way to write it
down is as a list of things deliberately **not** built, each with the reason and the condition that
would change the answer.

🅿️ **Parked — awareness-level, deliberately not built:**

- **Token buckets and leaky buckets.** The standard algorithms for smoothing bursts while permitting
  a controlled few. Worth being able to describe: a bucket fills at a steady rate up to a capacity,
  each request takes a token, and the capacity is exactly how large a burst is tolerated. Part
  [1.2](../01-what-headroom-is/1.2-the-burst-every-counter-approved.md) measured the burst a naive
  fixed window permits; a bucket is the mechanism that makes the permitted burst a **parameter** rather
  than an accident. Not built here because we are not the enforcer. **Build it when** you are the
  service being called.
- **A distributed authoritative counter.** Part [6.1](6.1-one-till-two-cashiers.md)'s ladder ends here:
  one store that every worker reads and writes, with atomic decrements. **Build it when** you have more
  than one worker and the provider does not return remaining-quota headers.
- **Priority and fairness.** Weighted queues, per-tenant allowances, work-conserving scheduling. **Build
  it when** more than one caller shares the allowance and one of them can starve another. Sutra has one
  caller, so a priority scheme here would be a parameter nobody sets.
- **Token-based accounting.** Metering by tokens rather than requests, which is how providers actually
  charge and part of how they actually limit. **Build it when** your limits are expressed in tokens —
  and note that this requires counting tokens *before* sending, which Day 24 built the arithmetic for.

The one thing that is not parked, because it is cheap and strictly better than anything in this day:

**Read the provider's own remaining-quota headers.** Parts
[5.2](../05-failure-lab/5.2-the-counter-that-starts-full.md), [6.1](6.1-one-till-two-cashiers.md) and
[6.2](6.2-the-gauge-shows-what-is-left.md) each arrived at this independently, which is usually a sign
that something is load-bearing. Every counter in this day is a *prediction* of a number the provider
already knows and, on many APIs, already tells you. Where that reading exists it is correct across
workers, across restarts and across every bug in section 5, and it costs nothing because it arrives on
responses you were receiving anyway.

That produces the design this day should actually leave you with, and it is a smaller idea than a
better counter:

```text
counter  →  predicts headroom between responses
header   →  corrects it on every response
gap      →  the metric that says your prediction has drifted
```

The counter stops being the truth and becomes a prediction with a measurable error. That is a
strictly better position than a more accurate counter, because you can now *see* when it is wrong.

## When it breaks

**The provider does not send the headers.** Many do not, and Addendum 02's four free tiers are not
uniform about it. Then you are back to a counter, and the honest thing is to write down that it is an
estimate, name its error sources — restarts, workers, non-quota failures counted or not — and set the
router's own thresholds with a margin that reflects them. A router configured to use 100% of a limit
it is estimating will hit 429s; one configured to use 85% will not, and the 15% is the price of not
knowing.

**Token limits bite before request limits and this day cannot see them.** A router that is comfortable
on requests per minute can be refused for tokens per minute, and every diagnostic in this day will
show a healthy gauge next to an unexplained refusal — which part
[6.2](6.2-the-gauge-shows-what-is-left.md) named as the signature of a counter that is wrong. The
difference here is that the counter is not wrong; it is counting the wrong thing. Distinguishing those
two requires reading the refusal, which is part
[4.1](../04-when-a-lane-says-no/4.1-reading-the-refusal.md)'s subject and the reason it insists a 429
carries information rather than merely being an error.

**And the scope decision itself can be wrong.** If Sutra ever serves more than one tenant, the "one
caller" assumption that parks priority and fairness stops holding, and the router's greedy choice
becomes a mechanism by which a busy tenant consumes a quiet one's allowance. That is not a bug to fix
later; it is a design that has to be revisited, and the way to make sure it is revisited is to write
the assumption into the module rather than into a part somebody read once.

## In production

**What a professional does with this list.** They do not build it. They write it down as the
architecture's stated boundary — *this is a client-side estimator, here is what it does not do, here
is the condition under which each missing piece becomes necessary* — and put it where the next
engineer will find it. Every item above has a **build it when**, and a condition somebody can check
beats a feature nobody needed.

**What degrades at scale, and it is not the algorithm.** Configuration. Four lanes, two windows each,
a pool key per lane, a capability set per lane, thresholds per lane: that is a lot of numbers, every
one of which was true on the day it was written, and providers change free-tier limits without
notice — Addendum 02 records a December 2025 precedent for exactly that. The router's correctness
depends on configuration that decays, so the freshness check that Phase 15's gates run on package
versions applies here too, and the failure it prevents is a router confidently routing to a limit that
was halved last month.

**What the interviewer is really asking.** When somebody asks you to design a rate limiter, the first
useful move is to ask which side you are on. Protecting yourself from someone else's limit and
protecting your service from callers share vocabulary and almost no requirements, and a candidate who
starts drawing a token bucket before establishing which one it is has answered a question nobody asked.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"I'm happy with the scope — estimator, not enforcer —
but put that sentence in the module docstring rather than only in the design doc, along with the four
parked items and their trigger conditions. The one I'd want a ticket for now is reading the
remaining-quota headers where the provider sends them, because that single change subsumes the
restart bug, the multi-worker bug and half the monitoring. And add a note that our limits are per
request while the provider also meters tokens, so a healthy gauge next to a 429 is expected rather
than mysterious."*

**The interview question:** *"Design a rate limiter."* An honest answer: *"First I'd ask which side of
the line I'm on, because the two systems share a name and almost nothing else. If I'm protecting a
service from its callers, the counter is authoritative, it has to be shared and atomic, and I need
admission control, priority classes and per-caller fairness — that's where token buckets earn their
place, because the burst you tolerate becomes a parameter instead of an accident. If I'm protecting
myself from someone else's limit, which is the more common case, then I'm building an estimator: I
copy the shape of their windows, count on attempt rather than on success, mark refused lanes cold and
honour retry-after, and I accept being approximate because they're the ones enforcing. The single
highest-value thing in that case isn't a better algorithm — it's reading their remaining-quota headers
if they send them, and treating my own counter as a prediction whose error I can measure against
theirs. And I'd say out loud which limits I can't see: if they meter tokens and I'm counting requests,
my gauge will look healthy while I'm being refused."*

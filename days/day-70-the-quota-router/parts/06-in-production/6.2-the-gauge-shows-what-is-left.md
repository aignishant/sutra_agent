# 6.2 — The gauge shows what is left

## One-line answer

Every counter in this day measures **requests sent**, and the number worth putting on a wall is the
one nobody computes: **what remains**, per lane, per window, as a percentage — because `12 left` and
`42 left` on the same lane are 60% of a minute that refills and 84% of a day that does not.

## The story

A van driver keeps a notebook. Every time he fills up he writes the date, the litres and the price,
and at the end of the month he adds it up for his accounts. It is a good record and he has kept it
for eleven years.

There is also a fuel gauge on the dashboard, six inches from his hand.

The notebook tells him, accurately, that he has bought 340 litres this month. It cannot tell him
whether he will reach Coventry. Those are different questions, and only one of them matters at four
o'clock on a Friday on a road with no services — and it is not the one the careful record answers.

## The idea in plain language

Everything the router has built so far is a *tally*. `Sliding` counts requests in a window. `spread()`
reports how many went to each lane. `failopen.py` is an argument about which events get counted. All
of it measures what has been spent.

Monitoring built on those numbers answers *how much are we using?* — and that question has the same
relationship to operations that the notebook has to the journey. It is the right number for a monthly
report and the wrong number for the decision somebody has to make at four o'clock.

The operational number is **remaining**, and it differs from *used* in three ways that matter:

- **It is relative to a limit.** 8 requests used means nothing until you know the ceiling. 80%
  remaining is actionable on its own.
- **It exists per window.** A lane can be comfortable on the minute and nearly out for the day, and
  the two call for completely different responses — wait sixty seconds, or stop for today.
- **It is the input the router already uses.** Part
  [2.1](../02-choosing-a-lane/2.1-headroom-is-not-a-preference.md) chooses a lane by comparing
  headroom, so the gauge is not a new measurement. It is the router's own decision variable, exposed.

That last point is the one worth building on. If your dashboard shows a different number from the one
your router routes on, you are debugging two systems. If it shows the same number, the dashboard
explains the routing.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because Addendum 02 denominates the whole project's budget in requests per day per provider, and a
budget you cannot read is a budget you will discover you have spent. Part
[1.5](../01-what-headroom-is/1.5-twelve-left-means-two-different-things.md) already established that
a bare remaining count is ambiguous across windows; this part is where that ambiguity is resolved into
something a person can look at.

There is also a Sutra-specific claim this day has to be able to prove rather than assert. Principle 15
says quota is the currency, so a day *about* quota that quietly spent some would be teaching one thing
and doing another. `budget.py` exists to make that checkable, and it belongs here rather than in the
hub's budget table, because it is an instrument and the hub only reports its answer.

## The mechanism

The gauge is the router's own state, printed instead of consulted. No new lab file is needed and none
should be added, because a gauge that reads from somewhere other than the router's counters is a
second source of truth:

```bash
cd days/day-70-the-quota-router/lab
uv run python -c "
from _lane import Clock, fleet
from _router import Router

clock = Clock(0.0)
router = Router(lanes=fleet(clock), clock=clock)
for _ in range(18):
    try:
        router.send('classify')
    except Exception:
        break

print(f'  {\"lane\":<12}{\"sent\":>6}{\"min left\":>10}{\"day left\":>10}{\"min %\":>8}{\"day %\":>8}')
for lane in router.lanes:
    m, d = lane.minute.remaining(), lane.day.remaining()
    print(f'  {lane.name:<12}{router.spread().get(lane.name, 0):>6}{m:>10}{d:>10}{100 * m / lane.rpm:>7.0f}%{100 * d / lane.rpd:>7.0f}%')
print()
print('  the number to alert on is the smallest percentage in either right-hand column')
"
```

**Line by line:**

- `lane.minute.remaining()` and `lane.day.remaining()` are read directly off the `Sliding` counters
  the router routes on. Reading the same objects is the point: the gauge cannot drift from the routing
  decision because it is the routing decision.
- Calling `remaining()` is not free of side effects and it is worth knowing why — on a `Fixed` counter
  it rolls the window when the span has elapsed. On `Sliding` it discards timestamps that have fallen
  out of the window. Either way, **reading the gauge advances the model of time**, which is correct
  here and is the sort of thing that surprises somebody who assumes a metric read is inert.
- The two percentage columns divide by `lane.rpm` and `lane.rpd`, which is what turns a count into a
  gauge. Part [1.5](../01-what-headroom-is/1.5-twelve-left-means-two-different-things.md) is the
  argument for why the raw number on its own is not enough.
- `router.spread()` supplies the `sent` column. It is included so the two kinds of number sit next to
  each other and the difference is visible — it is the notebook beside the gauge.
- The loop breaks on any exception rather than catching `AllDry` specifically, because this is an
  instrument rather than the router: what it needs is a fleet in a partly-spent state, and the reason
  it stopped is section 4's subject rather than this part's.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
  lane          sent  min left  day left   min %   day %
  gemini           2         8       248     80%     99%
  groq             8        22       992     73%     99%
  openrouter       8        12        42     60%     84%
  ollama           0         6    100000    100%    100%

  the number to alert on is the smallest percentage in either right-hand column
```

Read the `openrouter` row, because it is the whole part. It has **12 left this minute and 42 left
today** — and as raw counts those two numbers invite exactly the wrong reading, because 42 sounds
like the comfortable one. The percentages say what the counts hide: that lane's daily allowance is
only 50, so 42 left is 84% of a very small tank, while 12 left is 60% of a minute that will refill
in under a minute.

**The two windows are not the same kind of number and cannot be compared.** The minute figure is a
throttle reading: it goes down, you wait, it comes back, sixty times an hour. The day figure only
ever goes one way until midnight. So the minute column tells you what to do in the next few seconds
and the day column tells you whether you will still be answering tickets at four o'clock, and a
gauge that shows one of them is showing the wrong emergency half the time.

That is part [1.4](../01-what-headroom-is/1.4-two-windows-and-the-one-that-bites-last.md)'s finding —
the two windows bite on different timescales — turned into an operational rule: never collapse them
into a single health number.

And `ollama` at 100% of a 100,000-a-day allowance is not good news, it is a lane nothing is routing
to. A gauge showing full is either a healthy reserve or a lane that is misconfigured, out of the
capability set, or unreachable, and the gauge alone cannot tell you which — part
[2.2](../02-choosing-a-lane/2.2-the-lane-that-cannot-do-the-job.md) is why this one is legitimately at
zero use.

The other instrument this section owes you is the one that proves the day's own claim:

```bash

```

**Line by line:**

- No flags. `budget.py` inspects the objects the lab actually used — their type, whether they are real
  `BaseLlm` instances, the model string, and how many calls each served — and then reports the request
  count against each of the four providers in Addendum 02.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
  type                     NamedLlm
  is a real BaseLlm        True
  model string             'fake/gemini'
  calls it served          5
  lanes recorded           ['gemini', 'gemini', 'gemini', 'gemini', 'gemini']

  requests to Gemini       0
  requests to Groq         0
  requests to OpenRouter   0

  Every 'provider' in this lab is `_lane.Lane`, a counter with a clock.
  Nothing here imports an HTTP client, and no key is read from the environment.
```

`is a real BaseLlm True` is the line that makes the rest of the day credible. The models are real ADK
model objects — the runtime treats them as models in every respect, which is why section 3's plugin
measurements mean anything — and none of them is a provider. That is Principle 15 stated as a check
rather than as a promise.

## When it breaks

**A gauge read from your own counter is an estimate, and parts 5.2 and 6.1 are the two ways it is
wrong.** After a restart it reads full when it is not; across two workers it reads 60% in each of two
processes that are jointly at 130%. So the gauge inherits every limitation of the counter behind it,
and a dashboard showing a confident percentage is more dangerous than no dashboard, because somebody
will make a decision on it.

The fix is the same one those parts arrived at, and it is worth repeating because it is the single
highest-value change available in this whole area: **where the provider returns remaining-quota
headers, plot those.** They are measured on the other side of the line that actually refuses you, they
are correct across workers and restarts, and they cost nothing to read because they arrive on
responses you were already receiving. Your own counter's job then changes from *being the truth* to
*predicting the truth between responses*, which is a much easier job and one whose error you can
measure by comparing the two.

**The second failure is alerting on the wrong derivative.** An alert on *"day headroom below 20%"*
fires at nine in the evening when there is nothing to be done. The useful alert is on the **rate** —
headroom falling fast enough that it will reach zero before the window resets — because that fires
while there is still time to shed load or route differently. That is the difference between a fuel
gauge and a fuel gauge with a distance-to-empty readout, and it is the one worth building.

## In production

**What to expose, concretely.** One gauge per lane per window, in percent, plus a counter of refusals
per lane and a counter of degraded answers if part
[4.4](../04-when-a-lane-says-no/4.4-the-part-that-will-do.md)'s declared downgrade exists. The
refusal counter matters more than it looks: a refusal is the provider disagreeing with your gauge, so
a non-zero refusal rate against a healthy gauge is the signature of every bug in section 5 and part
6.1 at once. **Alert on the disagreement, not only on the level.**

**What degrades at scale.** Per-lane gauges multiply — lanes times windows times workers times
tenants — and the dashboard becomes unreadable at exactly the point it becomes necessary. The
aggregation that stays useful is the **minimum** across lanes for a given capability, because that is
what the router is actually constrained by: the question is never *how is gemini doing*, it is *can we
still serve a reasoning request*, and part
[4.3](../04-when-a-lane-says-no/4.3-every-lane-dry.md)'s `soonest` is that number in another form.

**What a professional adds that this lab does not have.** A record of the *decision*, not only the
outcome. Every routing decision has a reason — this lane had the most headroom, that one was cold,
this one lacked the capability — and logging the reason turns an incident from archaeology into
reading. Without it, six weeks later, you can see that a request went to `groq` and you cannot see why
`openrouter` was not chosen, which is the only question anybody will ask.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"The dashboard shows requests per minute per lane,
which is the number we can already get from the provider's console. What I want is headroom remaining
as a percentage, per window, from the router's own counters, plus the refusal rate next to it —
because a refusal while our gauge says we're fine is the alert that catches the whole class of bugs
where our count and theirs disagree. And check whether these providers return remaining-quota headers;
if they do, plot theirs and keep ours as the prediction, then alert on the gap between them."*

**The interview question:** *"What would you put on a dashboard for a system on a rate-limited API?"*
An honest answer: *"Headroom remaining, as a percentage, per limit window — not requests sent. Sent is
the number you can already get from the vendor's console and it doesn't tell you whether the next
request will be refused. It has to be per window because a minute limit and a daily limit fail on
completely different timescales: the minute one refills sixty times an hour, the daily one only goes
one way until midnight, so one number covering both is always reporting the wrong emergency. Next to
it I'd put the refusal rate, because a 429 while our own gauge says we're fine is the signature of the
counter being wrong — per-process counters, restarts, two workers sharing an allowance. And if the
provider returns remaining-quota headers I'd plot those as the truth and treat our counter as a
prediction, then alert on the gap, because that gap is the thing that actually tells you your model of
your own usage has drifted."*

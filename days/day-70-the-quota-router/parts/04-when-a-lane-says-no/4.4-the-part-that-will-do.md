# 4.4 — The part that will do

## One-line answer

Degrading to a lane that cannot do the job is worse than refusing, and the reason is not the quality
of the answer — it is the **silence**: the caller receives an answer and no error at all, and nothing
anywhere records that it came from a lane that cannot reason.

## The story

It is the hottest afternoon of the year and you book a car with air conditioning, because the app
offers one and the fare it quotes is the fare for one.

The car arrives. The windows are down. The driver says nothing about it and neither does the app.
You get in, because you are late and the car is here, and you ride across town with your shirt stuck
to your back and pay the fare you were quoted. The trip completes. The receipt says the trip
completed.

Now a different evening, in December, when you would not have wanted the air conditioning anyway.
This time the app says it before you book: *no cars with air conditioning nearby — take one without
for forty rupees less?* You tap yes without a second thought, and the ride is fine, and the receipt
says which kind of car you took.

Both evenings you rode in a car without air conditioning. Only one of them was a problem.

The difference is not comfort and it is not the money. In December you were told, you chose, and the
receipt wrote it down. In the hot afternoon there was no error, no complaint form, no line on the
receipt and nothing to point at afterwards. If somebody at the company asked *how often do air
conditioned bookings arrive without air conditioning?*, the honest answer is that nobody can know.
Not "it is rare" — nobody can know, because nothing was written down anywhere on the days it
happened.

## The idea in plain language

Day 24 already settled the general rule, and it is worth restating in one line before this part adds
the piece that is specific to a router. When the allowance is gone there are three exits — **fail**,
**degrade**, and **pretend** — and
[Day 21](../../../day-21-errors-surface-not-swallow/parts/03-policy/3.2-a-substitute-must-say-so.md)'s
rule governs the middle one: *a substitute must say so*.
[Day 24 5.2](../../../day-24-token-accounting-and-budgets/parts/05-in-production/5.2-degrading-not-failing.md)
measured all three on a budget ledger and found that the dishonest exit falsifies two metadata
fields rather than the answer itself.

What changes at the routing layer is that **the substitute is no longer recognisable.**

On Day 24 the fallback was a handbook entry: a fixed sentence from a dictionary, produced by
different code, obviously not a model. Here the fallback is *a model answer*. Same shape, same
length, same confident prose, same fields. It came from a real provider that really answered. The
only thing wrong with it is that the provider it came from does not declare the skill the request
needed — and there is nothing on the outside of the answer that says so.

[2.2](../02-choosing-a-lane/2.2-the-lane-that-cannot-do-the-job.md) named this rule and this is where
it collects: **quota failures are loud, capability failures are silent.** A `429` announces itself.
A lane that cannot reason, asked to reason, returns text.

Now take the title of this part seriously, because the argument is not that a weaker lane is always
wrong. There are plenty of situations where the part that will do is exactly what you should ship:

- a **shorter answer** from a smaller model when the long one is not available;
- a **classification** in place of a drafted reply, so the ticket is at least routed;
- a **cached answer** to a question somebody asked earlier, which is genuinely the model's own work
  ([Day 51](../../../day-51-caching-the-quota-lifeline/LESSON.md) exists for this);
- a **partial result** — three of the five fields, with the other two marked missing.

Every one of those is a reasonable product decision. None of them is what the failure in this part
is about, and the line between them is not the quality of the output. It is one question:

> **Is the degradation declared?**

Declared means three things, together. The **caller is told** what it got. The **record says** which
lane produced it and which skill was asked for. And **somebody can count** how often it happens.
Miss any one of those and the other two stop working: an answer marked in a log nobody aggregates is
not countable, and a count with no per-answer marking cannot be traced back to a ticket.

An undeclared downgrade is the failure. A declared one is a design decision, and design decisions can
be argued about, measured, and reversed.

## Why Sutra needs it

The reasoning step is the one that runs out first — [4.3](4.3-every-lane-dry.md) drained it while
half the fleet sat idle — and it is also the step whose wrong answer is hardest to see.

Think about what Sutra's reasoning call actually produces: a sentence explaining why this ticket
resembles an earlier one. A lane that cannot reason will produce a sentence too. It will name a
ticket and give a reason, and the reason will mention words that appear in both tickets. A person
reading it, in a queue of forty tickets, has no way at all to tell that this particular explanation
was produced by a model that cannot do this and was chosen because the two that can were empty.

There is a second reason this part exists in this day rather than in a general one. The router is the
only component that knows which lane served the request. The agent above it does not; the person
reading the ticket certainly does not. If the router does not write it down, the fact does not exist
anywhere in the system — and the next section's ledger, the one that lets you ask questions about
routing after the fact, will be complete and wrong at the same time.

## The mechanism

`lab/dry.py` implements the tempting alternative behind a flag, in five lines, which is roughly how
long it takes to write in a real codebase at four in the afternoon:

```python
fallback = next(ln for ln in lanes if not ln.can("reason"))
print("  --degrade: answering from a lane that cannot do the job")
print(f"    picked {fallback.name}, whose skills are {sorted(fallback.skills)}")
print(f"    'reason' in skills: {fallback.can('reason')}")
print("    the caller receives an answer and no error at all")
```

**Line by line:**

- `next(ln for ln in lanes if not ln.can("reason"))` selects the fallback by the **negation** of the
  capability test. That is the tell, and it is worth staring at: the condition that
  [2.2](../02-choosing-a-lane/2.2-the-lane-that-cannot-do-the-job.md) built to keep a lane *out* of
  the candidate set is here being used to find one. In production this line never looks this honest.
  It looks like `FALLBACK_LANE = "groq"` in a config file, written by somebody who checked that Groq
  was reliable and never checked what it was reliable at.
- `sorted(fallback.skills)` and `fallback.can("reason")` are printed only because this is a teaching
  script. The degraded answer itself carries neither of them, which is the entire subject of this
  part.
- There is no `try`, no `except`, no `raise` and no marking. The five lines contain no error handling
  because, from the program's point of view, nothing has gone wrong.

The ledger the router keeps has room for the truth, and that is what makes the omission a choice
rather than a limitation:

```python
@dataclass
class Decision:
    """One routing decision, written down so the ledger can be read back."""

    at: float
    skill: str
    lane: str
    headroom_before: int
    outcome: str
```

**Line by line:**

- `skill` and `lane` sit side by side in the same record. Everything needed to detect a degradation
  is already here: a row where the lane does not declare the skill is exactly the event, and it is a
  one-line query over the log.
- `outcome` is a free-form string, currently `"ok"` or `"429"`. A third value — `"degraded"` — costs
  nothing to add and is what a declared downgrade would look like at this layer.
- `at` is the clock reading, so degradations can be counted per interval rather than only in total,
  which is what turns them into a rate ([1.3](../01-what-headroom-is/1.3-the-counter-that-remembers-when.md)
  made the same argument about requests).
- The docstring says *so the ledger can be read back*. A ledger that is missing the events somebody
  would most want to read back has not failed at writing; it has failed at deciding what to write.

Run the honest path and the tempting one, and compare them:

```bash
cd days/day-70-the-quota-router/lab
uv run python dry.py
uv run python dry.py --degrade
```

**Line by line:**

- Both runs drain the fleet identically. The flag changes nothing about the routing, the counters or
  the state of any lane; it changes only what happens after `AllDry` is raised. That makes it a clean
  ablation: everything before the last four lines of output is the same run twice.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
draining every lane that can reason

  served 30 reasoning requests before the fleet ran out
  spread {'gemini': 10, 'groq': 0, 'openrouter': 20, 'ollama': 0}

  AllDry raised:
    no lane can serve 'reason'; soonest headroom in 60s (gemini in 60s, openrouter in 60s)

  --degrade: answering from a lane that cannot do the job
    picked groq, whose skills are ['classify', 'draft']
    'reason' in skills: False
    the caller receives an answer and no error at all

  Nothing above logged that the answer came from a lane that cannot reason.
  That is the failure: not the outage, the silence about it.
```

`'reason' in skills: False`, and an answer went back anyway.

Compare that with the same run without the flag, from [4.3](4.3-every-lane-dry.md): the router
raised, the message carried `soonest headroom in 60s`, and the caller had something to act on. Same
fleet, same instant, same exhausted lanes. One version produces an actionable refusal; the other
produces a satisfied caller.

And notice which of the two looks better on every dashboard you have. The refusing run has an
exception in it. The degrading run has a hundred per cent success rate.

## When it breaks

The break is not in the answer. It is in what the system remembers, and it can be measured:

```bash
cd days/day-70-the-quota-router/lab
uv run python - <<'PY'
from _lane import Clock, fleet
from _router import AllDry, Router

clock = Clock(0.0)
lanes = fleet(clock)
router = Router(lanes=lanes, clock=clock)
while True:
    try:
        router.send("reason")
    except AllDry:
        break
fallback = next(ln for ln in lanes if not ln.can("reason"))
answer = fallback.call()
print("the caller got:", answer)
print("decisions logged:", len(router.log))
print("last decision:  ", router.log[-1])
print("outcomes seen:  ", sorted({d.outcome for d in router.log}))
print("lanes named in the log:", sorted({d.lane for d in router.log}))
PY
```

**Line by line:**

- The loop is `dry.py`'s drain, and the two lines after it are `dry.py --degrade`'s fallback: pick a
  lane that cannot do the work and call it directly. This is not a caricature; it is the same code
  path with the prints removed.
- `fallback.call()` goes through the lane's ordinary machinery, so the answer is a real one and
  Groq's own counters move. Nothing about it is fake — that is the problem.
- The four `print` lines interrogate `router.log`, the ledger from
  [2.4](../02-choosing-a-lane/2.4-everyone-read-the-same-board.md) and section 3, asking what the
  system would be able to tell you tomorrow.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
the caller got: groq:ok
decisions logged: 30
last decision:   Decision(at=0.0, skill='reason', lane='openrouter', headroom_before=1, outcome='ok')
outcomes seen:   ['ok']
lanes named in the log: ['gemini', 'openrouter']
```

Read those five lines slowly, because every one of them is a small lie of omission.

The caller got `groq:ok`. The ledger holds thirty decisions and **not one of them mentions Groq**.
The last thing the router recorded is a successful OpenRouter call, so a person reading this log
tomorrow sees a run that went perfectly and stopped. The `outcomes seen` set contains only `'ok'` —
there is not even a `429` in there, because [4.3](4.3-every-lane-dry.md) showed the router refuses
from its own counters before spending a request. Every single signal an operator, an alert or an
audit could use says the same thing: nothing happened.

That is the receipt from the hot afternoon, printed by a computer. The trip completed. The fare was
the one you were quoted. There is no line anywhere on it for which car actually came.

Now the second-order failure, which is the one that keeps this alive for months. **The eval passes.**
An eval scores the answer, and the answer is a plausible sentence about two tickets. It is not
obviously wrong; capability failures rarely are. So the eval is green, the dashboard is green, the
ticket is closed, and if quality drifts down over a quarter, the investigation starts with the prompt
— because the prompt is the only variable anybody wrote down.

This is a shape this curriculum has met twice already, three days ago.
[Day 67 part 4.3](../../../day-67-guardrail-callbacks/parts/04-what-goes-out/4.3-the-rewrite-that-lies.md)
showed a guardrail that quietly rewrites an answer and leaves behind a record that was true of
nothing — the log describes text nobody ever produced.
[Day 67 part 4.4](../../../day-67-guardrail-callbacks/parts/04-what-goes-out/4.4-the-error-hook-that-invents-a-result.md)
showed an error hook that manufactures a result to keep a run green. This part is the third member of
the family, and the family resemblance is exact: in all three, the output is acceptable, no exception
is raised, and **the record is wrong in a way that cannot be detected from the record.** The subject
changes — a guardrail, an error hook, a router — and the failure is identical, which is the reason it
is worth learning as a shape rather than as three separate lessons.

## In production

**What a declared degradation looks like.** It is a handful of fields on the response, and they are
present on *every* answer rather than only on degraded ones — a provenance field that appears only
when something went wrong is a field whose absence is ambiguous, which
[Day 24 5.2](../../../day-24-token-accounting-and-budgets/parts/05-in-production/5.2-degrading-not-failing.md)
argues at length and is worth obeying here.

| Field | On a normal answer | On a degraded answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `status` | `ok` | `degraded` |
| `served_by` | `gemini` | `groq` |
| `requested_skill` | `reason` | `reason` |
| `skill_served` | `reason` | `draft` |
| `why` | absent | `no lane with 'reason' had headroom` |

The pair that does the work is `requested_skill` and `skill_served`. Everything else is convenience;
those two disagreeing *is* the event, it is machine-checkable, and it needs no judgement about
quality from anybody. And the caller is the last stop, not the first: the same facts belong on the
`Decision` row, so the answer and the ledger agree.

**The metric that matters is the rate, not the quality.** This is the counter-intuitive part and it
is the one to remember. Nobody can grade every degraded answer, and grading them is the wrong
question anyway. What a team needs is one number per interval: *what fraction of answers were served
by a lane that could not do the requested skill?* It is cheap, it needs no model, it cannot be argued
with, and it makes the two things that matter visible — a rate that is small and steady is a design
decision working as intended, and a rate that climbs is an outage being concealed by your own
fallback. Break it down **by skill**, because a degraded classification and a degraded reasoning
answer are not the same event and should not average with each other.

It is worth noticing that this is the question from the story, and that the story's version of the
company cannot answer it. *How often do air conditioned bookings arrive without air conditioning?* is
a rate, it is the only number anybody actually needs, and it is unanswerable in a system where the
receipt does not say which car came. Marking each answer is not paperwork; it is what makes the one
useful number exist.

**What degrades at scale is that the fallback becomes the product.** A degradation path that works
well enough stops being noticed. Six months later a third of reasoning answers come from a lane that
cannot reason, the quality has quietly halved, every dashboard is green, and the fallback is now
load-bearing infrastructure that nobody chose. The rate alert is the only thing that catches it, and
the rate alert exists only if the answers were marked.

**The failure that only appears with real traffic** is that the two systems disagree and nobody
compares them. The provider's console says Groq served four thousand requests today; Sutra's ledger
says Groq served two thousand eight hundred. That gap is the degraded traffic, and it is the only
detector that survives a system where nothing marks anything — which is a terrible detector, because
it requires somebody to think of comparing two numbers that live in different places and are not
supposed to match exactly anyway.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"the fallback picks a lane with `not can(skill)`
and returns its answer with the same shape as a real one. If we are going to ship a downgrade — and I
think for classification we should — then it has to be declared: `status: degraded`, both skills on
the response, a `Decision` row with `outcome='degraded'`, and an alert on the rate per skill. As
written, the only way anyone finds out is by comparing our request count against the provider's
console, and nobody is going to do that."*

**The interview question:** *"Your best model is rate limited. Why not just fall back to a cheaper
one?"* The answer that has shipped both versions: *"Often you should — a shorter answer, a
classification instead of a full draft, a cached response are all fine, and refusing when you had
something useful to offer is its own failure. What I will not do is fall back **silently**. If the
lane that answered cannot do the skill that was asked for, that has to be on the response and in the
routing ledger, because otherwise there is no error, no exception and no metric, and nothing
downstream can tell the difference. I measured this in a lab router: the caller got an answer from a
provider that does not declare the reasoning skill, and the router's own log held thirty decisions
and did not mention that provider once. The trap is that it looks better than the honest version on
every dashboard, because refusing shows up as an exception and degrading shows up as a hundred per
cent success. The metric I would put on it is the rate of degraded answers per skill, not their
quality — quality you cannot measure on every answer, and the rate tells you when your fallback has
quietly become your product."*

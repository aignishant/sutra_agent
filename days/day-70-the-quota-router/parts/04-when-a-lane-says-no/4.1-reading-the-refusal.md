# 4.1 — Reading the refusal

## One-line answer

A `429` is not an error, it is an instruction with a time in it, and the `retry-after` it carries is
worth more than every counter the router keeps — because it is the provider's own statement about
its own window, and the counters are only the router's estimate of it.

## The story

The road you take to work is dug up.

You come round the corner and there are barriers across it, a trench behind them, and a length of
pipe lying in the mud. Taped to the middle barrier is a painted board: water main replacement, road
closed, open again on the 14th.

You read it once, turn the car around and take the long way. For the next few mornings you take the
long way and set off a little earlier, and on the 14th the road is open.

A different month, a different closure, and this time it is the little bridge halfway along. It
cracked in the rains and it is shut. There is no board because there is no date to write on one:
somebody has to look at it, somebody has to decide, and somebody has to find the money. Until those
three things happen, nobody can tell you anything at all.

Both mornings ended with you not getting through. They are not the same morning. One of them told
you exactly what to do and until when. The other one needs a person to make a decision before
anything moves.

Swap your responses over and watch both go wrong. Treat the bridge like the roadworks and you will
drive down that road every morning for a season, waiting for a date that nobody has set. Treat the
roadworks like the bridge and you will spend an evening on the phone complaining about a job that is
scheduled, published and running to plan.

And notice which part of the board was worth having. Not *road closed* — you could see that through
the windscreen. It was *the 14th*. Your own guess would have been "a few days, probably". The people
who dug the trench are not guessing; they are reading their own schedule.

## The idea in plain language

When a program asks a web service for something, the answer comes back with a **status code** — a
three-digit number saying how the request went. `200` means it worked. `500` means the service
broke. The one this part is about is **`429`**, which means *too many requests*: you asked more often
than your allowance permits, so this particular request was not served.

`429` usually arrives with a **`retry-after`** value: a number of seconds saying how long until the
allowance is available again. That value is the whole subject of this part.

Here is the distinction that everything in section 4 is built on.

- An **error** means *something went wrong*. Nobody planned it, nothing is scheduled to fix it, and
  the right response is to surface it and let somebody act.
- A **refusal** means *not now, and here is when*. Nothing is broken. The system is behaving exactly
  as designed, the condition is temporary, and the response is to wait for a stated time and come
  back.

Those two call for opposite responses, and a client that cannot tell them apart gets both wrong the
way the two closures went wrong. Treat every refusal as an error and you give up on a lane — a
**lane** being one provider's quota pool, the unit the router routes between
([1.1](../01-what-headroom-is/1.1-a-ceiling-is-a-window-not-a-number.md)) — that was merely full for
another few seconds. Treat every error as a refusal and you retry a real outage forever, politely,
until somebody notices the queue.

There is a second reason to read a refusal rather than merely catch it, and it is the one that makes
this a *routing* subject rather than an error-handling one.

The router keeps its own counters. It knows how many requests it has sent to each lane and when,
because [1.3](../01-what-headroom-is/1.3-the-counter-that-remembers-when.md) built a sliding window
that remembers the timestamps. Those counters are good, and they are still only an **estimate** —
they count what *this process* sent. They cannot see the other replica, the nightly job, the
colleague running the same key from a laptop, or the request that left this process and is still in
flight. Every one of those spends from the same provider allowance
([6.1](../06-in-production/6.1-one-till-two-cashiers.md) is the shared-counter version of the
problem).

The provider's count has none of those blind spots. It is the truth, and there is exactly one moment
when the provider tells you any of it: **the refusal**. A `429` is the only free reading you ever get
of the other side's own books — except that it is not free, because getting it cost you a request.
Something you paid a request for should be read, not thrown away with a `pass`.

Two facts are in it. **Which window** closed — the minute one or the daily one, and
[1.4](../01-what-headroom-is/1.4-two-windows-and-the-one-that-bites-last.md) showed those are
different orders of magnitude of bad news. And **how long** until it opens.

## Why Sutra needs it

Section 3 put the router underneath every model call Sutra makes, as a plugin. From here on, nothing
in Sutra talks to a provider without the router choosing the lane, which means the router's picture
of the fleet is the only picture there is. A refusal is the only event that ever corrects that
picture from outside.

The rest of this section is built directly on the time in that refusal.
[4.2](4.2-a-cold-lane-not-a-broken-one.md) uses it to decide how long to stop sending to a lane.
[4.3](4.3-every-lane-dry.md) adds up those times across the fleet to tell the caller when anything
at all will be available. Neither has anything to work with if the refusal is flattened into
`except Exception: log.error("call failed")`.

It also feeds forward out of this day. Day 72 — *Backoff with honesty* — builds the caller's retry
ladder on `retry-after` and on the rule that a system never invents a result when it runs out of
attempts. That day starts from the assumption that the refusal reached it intact.

## The mechanism

The lab's refusal is one small class, and every field on it is there because the real one has it:

```python
class Refused(Exception):
    """Raised by a lane that has no headroom left in some window.

    Carries `retry_after` because that is what the real refusal carries, and the whole of section 4
    is about the difference between a refusal you can read and one you merely catch.
    """

    def __init__(self, lane: str, window: str, retry_after: float) -> None:
        super().__init__(f"429 {lane}: {window} window exhausted, retry-after {retry_after:.0f}s")
        self.lane = lane
        self.window = window
        self.retry_after = retry_after
```

**Line by line:**

- It subclasses `Exception` rather than being a return value, because a refusal must not be
  ignorable. A function that returns `None` for "refused" is a function whose callers will forget to
  check, and the forgetting is silent. Raising makes ignoring it a deliberate act.
- The three facts are **attributes**, not only text inside the message. `self.window` exists so that
  a caller can branch on `refusal.window == "day"` without parsing English, which is
  [Day 24's](../../../day-24-token-accounting-and-budgets/parts/02-two-ceilings/2.2-reading-the-ceiling-off-a-refusal.md)
  lesson applied here: key on the field, never on the human-readable message, because the message is
  prose and prose gets reworded.
- `retry_after` is a `float` of seconds, not a datetime. Seconds compose with the lab's `Clock`
  arithmetic and with any backoff ladder without a timezone appearing anywhere. A real HTTP header
  may carry either a count of seconds or an absolute date, and normalising to seconds at the edge is
  what stops that choice leaking into the rest of the program.
- The formatted message still contains all three facts, because the message is what ends up in a log
  line, and a log line that says only `429` has told the person reading it nothing they can act on.

The lane produces that refusal from its own bookkeeping, which is exactly what a provider does:

```python
def call(self) -> str:
    """Spend one request against both windows, or raise `Refused` with a real retry-after."""
    if self.minute.remaining() <= 0:
        self.refused += 1
        wait = self.minute.stamps[0] + 60.0 - self.clock.now
        self.cold_until = self.clock.now + wait
        raise Refused(self.name, "minute", wait)
    if self.day.remaining() <= 0:
        self.refused += 1
        wait = self.day.stamps[0] + 86400.0 - self.clock.now
        self.cold_until = self.clock.now + wait
        raise Refused(self.name, "day", wait)
    self.minute.take()
    self.day.take()
    self.served += 1
    return f"{self.name}:ok"
```

**Line by line:**

- `wait = self.minute.stamps[0] + 60.0 - self.clock.now` is where `retry-after` actually comes from,
  and it is worth reading slowly. `stamps[0]` is the **oldest** request still inside the sliding
  window. Sixty seconds after that one was made, it falls out of the window and one slot opens. So
  the wait is not a policy, a guess or a constant — it is a fact computed from the provider's own
  record of when you called.
- The minute check comes **first**, so a lane that is out of both windows reports the minute one.
  That is the friendlier of the two answers and it is also the wrong one to act on, which is why
  `self.window` exists: a caller that reads the field learns to distinguish `55` from `86200`
  ([1.4](../01-what-headroom-is/1.4-two-windows-and-the-one-that-bites-last.md)).
- `self.refused += 1` counts the refusal on the lane. A refused request is a real request that
  happened; anything that counts only successes is
  [5.1](../05-failure-lab/5.1-the-tally-that-counts-only-sales.md)'s bug.
- Neither `take()` runs on the refusal path, so this simulator does **not** charge a refused request
  against the allowance. Real providers are not guaranteed to be that generous, and
  [4.2](4.2-a-cold-lane-not-a-broken-one.md) shows the result still holds even with the lab being
  kind.
- `self.cold_until` is set by the lane itself at the moment of refusal. That field is
  [4.2](4.2-a-cold-lane-not-a-broken-one.md)'s whole subject; here, just notice that the time in the
  refusal is immediately written down somewhere rather than only being thrown.

Run the script that produces one:

```bash

```

**Line by line:**

- `cold.py` with no flag sends requests to a single lane, Gemini, and honours whatever the refusal
  tells it. The comparison against ignoring it is [4.2](4.2-a-cold-lane-not-a-broken-one.md); here
  the run is only being used for the one line it prints early.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
lane gemini: 10/min, 250/day
mode: honour retry-after

  t=  5.0s  429 gemini: minute window exhausted, retry-after 55s

  attempts reaching the provider   11
  served                           10
  refusals                         1
  day window used                  10/250

  The router stopped asking until retry-after elapsed. One refusal taught it
  the window was shut; every later attempt in that window was never made.

  the backoff ladder a caller uses on top of this, from Addendum 02 section 6:
    attempt 1: wait    1s   cumulative     1s
    attempt 2: wait    2s   cumulative     3s
    attempt 3: wait    4s   cumulative     7s
    attempt 4: wait    8s   cumulative    15s
    then escalate. Never invent a result (Principle 10).
```

The last block is [4.2](4.2-a-cold-lane-not-a-broken-one.md)'s subject. The line this part is about
is the fourth:

```text
  t=  5.0s  429 gemini: minute window exhausted, retry-after 55s
```

Four facts, and a client that keeps only the first has kept the least useful one.

| In the line | What it is | What it changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `429` | this is a refusal, not a breakage | do not escalate; there is nothing to fix |
| `gemini` | which lane refused | the other lanes are unaffected and still candidates |
| `minute` | which window closed | seconds of unavailability, not a day of it |
| `55s` | when the window opens | the exact instant this lane becomes a candidate again |

`55` and not `60`, and the difference is the point. The ten requests went out from `t=0` at
half-second intervals, so by the time the eleventh was refused at `t=5.0` the oldest one was already
five seconds old and had five seconds less to wait before falling out of the window
([1.3](../01-what-headroom-is/1.3-the-counter-that-remembers-when.md)). The router could have
estimated that from its own stamps. The provider did not estimate it; it read it.

## When it breaks

The first way this breaks is the one you can measure right now: honour the refusal but substitute
your own number for the one it carried.

```bash
cd days/day-70-the-quota-router/lab
uv run python - <<'PY'
from _lane import Clock, Refused, fleet

clock = Clock(0.0)
lane = next(ln for ln in fleet(clock) if ln.name == "gemini")
for _ in range(10):
    lane.call()
try:
    lane.call()
except Refused as refusal:
    print("the provider said: retry-after", f"{refusal.retry_after:.0f}s")
clock.tick(1.0)
print(f"a client that guessed 1s retries at t={clock.now:.0f}s")
try:
    lane.call()
except Refused as refusal:
    print(" ", refusal)
print("refusals so far:", lane.refused)
PY
```

**Line by line:**

- The ten `lane.call()` at `t=0` fill the minute window exactly, so the eleventh is guaranteed to
  refuse. Nothing here is random and nothing here needs a network.
- `clock.tick(1.0)` is the guess: one second, which is the first rung of every backoff ladder ever
  written and is completely unrelated to what this lane just said.
- The second `try` block is the retry. Its output is the finding.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
the provider said: retry-after 60s
a client that guessed 1s retries at t=1s
  429 gemini: minute window exhausted, retry-after 59s
refusals so far: 2
```

The provider said sixty and the client heard one. The retry bought a second refusal, and the second
refusal politely repeats the same instruction with one second knocked off it. A loop built on that
guess will collect fifty-nine more of them before anything changes, and
[4.2](4.2-a-cold-lane-not-a-broken-one.md) measures exactly what that costs.

The second way this breaks is the one you cannot measure in the lab, because the lab always sets the
field: **a `429` that arrives with no `retry-after` at all.** Real services omit it. Then
`refusal.retry_after` is `None`, and a router has to produce a number from somewhere.

There is a correct order to try, and the wrong answer is at the bottom of it.

1. **Use the router's own window.** It has the stamps. `stamps[0] + span - now` is the same
   arithmetic the lane does above, and it is a genuine estimate rather than a constant.
2. **If there are no stamps, wait the whole window.** Sixty seconds for a minute limit. It is
   pessimistic and it is safe: the worst case is one lane idle for a minute while other lanes serve.
3. **Never a small default.** Not one second, not "a few hundred milliseconds, we will find out".

That third rule deserves the reason and not just the instruction. A small default does not fail
loudly. It turns a single refusal into a stream of them, at the exact moment the provider is telling
you to stop, and every one of those requests is real traffic sent to a service that has already said
no. It is a closure with no board on it, and a driver who has decided that no board means *probably
open by lunchtime* — so he drives down to look, and turns around, and drives down again.

The third way is subtler and it is documented in this curriculum with a measurement: **the
`retry-after` can be wrong, and specifically too small.** Day 24 captured a live Gemini daily
refusal whose `RetryInfo` said `retryDelay: '26s'` while the real wait was until the next day, and
Day 21 watched twenty-eight requests obey that delay and be refused every single time
([Day 24 2.2](../../../day-24-token-accounting-and-budgets/parts/02-two-ceilings/2.2-reading-the-ceiling-off-a-refusal.md)).

So the honest version of this part's claim is: `retry-after` is the best available statement about
when *not* to try, and it is a **floor rather than a promise**. Take the larger of the header and
your own estimate, and if a lane refuses again immediately after you honoured its own number, raise
your wait instead of repeating it. A provider that is wrong about its own window is still less wrong
than a client guessing about someone else's.

Roadworks overrun. On the 14th the trench is sometimes still open, and the response to that is not to
stop reading boards — it is to stop treating the date on one as a promise, and to look again before
you set off.

## In production

**Not every failure is a refusal, and the classification is code you write once.** The router needs a
function that turns whatever the provider returned into one of three verdicts, because retrying is
right for one of them, waiting is right for another, and both are wrong for the third.

| What comes back | Verdict | What the router does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `429` | refusal | mark the lane cold for `retry-after`, route elsewhere |
| `503` with a `retry-after` | refusal | same as `429`; the lane is at capacity, not broken |
| `503` with nothing, `502`, `504` | error, transient | retry a small number of times, then treat the lane as down |
| `500` | error | surface it; a lane that keeps returning it is down, not full |
| timeouts, connection reset | error, transient | retry once, then treat as down; the request may still have been counted |
| `400`, `404`, `422` | error, permanent | never retry — the request is wrong and will be wrong again |
| `401`, `403` | error, permanent | never retry — page somebody; this is a key, not a quota |

Two notes on that table, both learned the expensive way. Provider-specific overload codes exist
outside it, so the classifier reads the **body** and keys on a field rather than inferring from the
status alone — Day 24 keys on `quotaId` for exactly this reason. And a timeout is the genuinely
ambiguous row: the request may have reached the provider and been counted, so the local counter
should assume it was spent.

**`retry-after` should update the router's window, not only pause the lane.** This is the change most
teams miss, and it is one line in the right place. When a lane refuses, the router has learned two
things: that this lane is unavailable until a stated time, *and* that its own counter for that lane
was wrong. Pausing the lane acts on the first and ignores the second. The cold period then elapses,
the counter — untouched, still believing it has room — sends immediately, and takes another refusal.
Writing the provider's time back into the local window makes the next decision better instead of
merely later, and on a shared key it is the only way the router ever discovers that somebody else is
spending the same allowance.

**What degrades at scale** is that refusals stop being rare events and become a signal channel. At
low volume you see one an hour and handle it. At high volume every replica is being refused
constantly, and the aggregate rate of `429` per lane becomes a better real-time measure of remaining
capacity than any counter you keep — but only if the refusals are being counted somewhere central.
A `429` handled perfectly inside one process and never reported has improved that process and taught
the fleet nothing.

**The failure that only appears with real traffic** is the refusal that never reaches your code. An
HTTP client library with retries enabled by default will absorb the `429`, sleep on its own schedule,
retry, and hand your program either a success or a generic error several seconds later. Your router
then has no refusal to read, no `retry-after` to honour, and a latency graph nobody can explain. The
first thing to check when the numbers make no sense is whether something under you is being helpful.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"the handler catches `Refused` and logs it, but it
throws the object away. `retry_after` and `window` are the two most expensive numbers in this
system — we paid a request to learn them. Put them on the lane and let the next decision use them,
and please branch on `refusal.window` rather than on the message string; that message will be
reworded and this branch will not be updated."*

**The interview question:** *"You get a `429` from a provider. What do you do?"* The answer that has
handled one: *"First, I treat it as an instruction rather than an error, because it comes with a time
on it — `retry-after` says when the window reopens, and that number is worth more than my own
counters, because my counters only know what my process sent and the provider's number knows about
every process on the key. I mark that lane unavailable until that instant and route the request to
another lane, and I write the time back into my own window so the next decision is made on corrected
information. The two things I would never do are guess a small delay when the header is missing —
that turns one refusal into a retry storm — and treat the header as a guarantee. I have seen a
provider send a twenty-six second delay on a limit that actually resets the next day, so I take it as
a floor and escalate my wait if the lane refuses again."*

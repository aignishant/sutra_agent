# 1.5 — Twelve left means two different things

## One-line answer

Comparing lanes by requests remaining is comparing numbers that are not in the same unit; comparing
each lane's remaining **share of its own ceiling** serves 233 of 300 calls against 222, and keeps the
scarce lane alive for 6.3 minutes instead of 4.1.

## The story

Two water tanks stand on your roof. One holds five hundred litres and one holds five thousand.

Someone tells you both tanks have two hundred litres left, and asks which one you should draw from
this evening.

The numbers are equal, so the question sounds like it has no answer. But you know the roof. The small
tank is nearly half full and it fills from the mains every morning. The big tank is at four per cent,
it is the one the whole building falls back on, and it refills slowly from a borewell that runs when
there is power.

Two hundred litres in the small tank is a comfortable evening. Two hundred litres in the big tank is
an emergency reserve. Told only the two numbers, you would have shrugged and picked either. Told what
each tank *holds*, the choice is obvious, and it is the opposite of what "they are equal" suggested.

## The idea in plain language

[1.4](1.4-two-windows-and-the-one-that-bites-last.md) fixed one bug — the router was reading only the
minute window — and found the fix barely helped. This part explains why, and it is not a bug in the
code. It is a bug in the **unit**.

When the router says `max(lanes, key=headroom)`, it is comparing integers from different scales, as
if they were the same quantity. Gemini's daily ceiling is 250 and OpenRouter's is 50. "Fifty
remaining" means *entirely untouched* on OpenRouter and *four fifths spent* on Gemini. The `max` sees
two fifties and no difference.

The alternative is to compare **fractions**: how much of its own allowance does this lane still have?

```
share = min( minute_remaining / rpm , day_remaining / rpd )
```

Now a full lane is `1.0` whatever its size, an empty one is `0.0`, and the comparison is between
comparable things. The router stops asking *"who has the most requests?"* and starts asking *"who is
least depleted?"* — and the second question is the one that keeps a fleet alive, because it naturally
conserves whatever is scarce.

This is the same instinct as spending from the fullest tank, and it has a name in scheduling:
**proportional fairness**. What matters here is not the name but the fact that it changes the answer,
and that you can measure by how much.

## Why Sutra needs it

The router in section 3 makes one choice per model call, thousands of times a shift, and it makes
them by calling a comparison function. That function is where all of section 1's arithmetic finally
gets used. Getting it wrong does not raise; it just quietly spends the wrong lane first, and the bill
arrives hours later as [4.3's](../04-when-a-lane-says-no/4.3-every-lane-dry.md) empty fleet.

It also sets up an honest expectation. This part improves the run by about five per cent. Section 2
will show a policy that helps more in a narrow band and not at all outside it. None of these is a
rescue, and a reader who expects routing to solve a capacity problem should be corrected here rather
than by production.

## The mechanism

One more comparison function in `lab/twowindows.py`, beside the two from
[1.4](1.4-two-windows-and-the-one-that-bites-last.md):

```python
def by_ratio(lane: Lane) -> float:
    """The tighter of the two windows, as a share of that window's own ceiling."""
    return min(
        lane.minute.remaining() / lane.rpm,
        lane.day.remaining() / lane.rpd,
    )
```

**Line by line:**

- Each window is divided by **its own** limit, not by a shared constant. That is what puts the two
  windows on one scale: `8/10` and `200/250` are both "how much of this is left", and taking `min`
  of them is now meaningful in a way that `min(8, 200)` was not.
- `min` again, for the same reason as `headroom()` in
  [1.1](1.1-a-ceiling-is-a-window-not-a-number.md): a lane is as free as its tightest ceiling. What
  changed is only the unit, and that is the entire content of this part.
- Integer division would silently floor every value to `0` or `1` and destroy the comparison. Both
  operands here are `int`, so this relies on Python 3's `/` being true division — one of the few
  places where a language default is doing real work. Writing `//` by habit would produce a function
  that compiles, runs, and ranks every partly-used lane as identical.
- The return type is `float` while the other two return `float(int)`. They agree in type so the
  router's `max(options, key=MODES[mode])` never learns which one it has.

Run all three comparisons over the same 300 calls:

```bash
cd days/day-70-the-quota-router/lab
uv run python twowindows.py
uv run python twowindows.py --both
uv run python twowindows.py --ratio
```

**Line by line:**

- The three flags select `by_minute`, `by_requests` and `by_ratio`. Nothing else differs: same
  arrivals, same four-second spacing, same lanes, same fixed sequence.

Measured on 2026-09-06, the third run:

```text
300 reasoning calls, one every 4s
comparing lanes by: min(minute, day) as a share of the ceiling

  lane         served   rpm     rpd
  gemini          183    10     250
  openrouter       50    20      50

  served 233, dropped 67
  openrouter spent its whole day by t=380s (6.3 min in)
```

All three side by side:

| Comparison | Served | Dropped | OpenRouter's day gone at |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| minute window only | 222 | 78 | 4.1 min |
| `min(minute, day)`, in requests | 225 | 75 | 4.7 min |
| `min(minute, day)`, as a share | **233** | **67** | **6.3 min** |

Eleven more requests served than the naive policy, out of three hundred. The scarce lane survives
half as long again. The improvement is real, it is monotonic across the three, and it is **modest** —
about five per cent.

Two honest observations about that table, because the numbers say more than the headline does.

**OpenRouter still serves exactly 50 in every row.** Its entire daily allowance is spent in all three
policies. No comparison function conserves a lane that the workload needs; the ratio view only
changes *when* it is spent, by putting more of the early work on the lane that can afford it. If you
want that fifty to still exist in the evening, no `key=` function will do it. You need a reservation,
which is a different mechanism and belongs in
[6.3](../06-in-production/6.3-what-a-real-quota-system-adds.md).

**67 requests are still dropped.** The fleet's capacity over that span is smaller than the workload,
and arithmetic cannot fix arithmetic. This is the sentence to carry into section 2: routing decides
*who* gets starved, never *whether* anyone does.

## When it breaks

The ratio comparison has a failure the absolute one does not, and it is worth reaching on purpose. A
lane with a tiny ceiling can look permanently attractive:

```python
from _lane import Clock, Lane

clock = Clock(0.0)
tiny = Lane("tiny", "m", 2, 4, frozenset({"classify"}), clock)
big = Lane("big", "m", 60, 5000, frozenset({"classify"}), clock)
print("tiny share:", min(tiny.minute.remaining() / tiny.rpm, tiny.day.remaining() / tiny.rpd))
print("big  share:", min(big.minute.remaining() / big.rpm, big.day.remaining() / big.rpd))
```

**Line by line:**

- `tiny` has a ceiling of two per minute and four per day. It is nearly useless.
- Both lanes are untouched, so both are completely full.
- The ratio for both is therefore `1.0`, and a `max` over ties returns the **first** — which is
  `tiny`, a lane that can absorb two requests before it is empty for the day.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
tiny share: 1.0
big  share: 1.0
```

A fresh fleet is all ties, and ties resolve by declaration order. On the first call of the morning the
ratio policy is not a policy at all; it is whatever order the lanes happen to be listed in. That is
the **cold start** problem, it is why
[6.3](../06-in-production/6.3-what-a-real-quota-system-adds.md) lists a warm-up rule, and it is the
reason the sampling in [2.4](../02-choosing-a-lane/2.4-everyone-read-the-same-board.md) — which
breaks ties randomly rather than by list order — turns out to matter more than it first appears.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead.** They rarely ship a bare ratio. The comparison becomes a small
weighted score: remaining share, times a preference weight for lanes that are known to be better at
the job ([Day 9](../../../day-09-four-free-providers/parts/04-the-benchmark/4.3-three-shops-three-closing-times.md)
measured those differences), minus a penalty for recent errors. The important discipline is that the
score is **one function in one file with a test**, not a chain of `if` statements spread through the
call path, because it will be tuned and it must be tunable without touching the routing logic.

**What degrades at scale.** Ratios hide absolute scarcity. A lane at `0.02` of a huge ceiling still
has plenty of requests, and a lane at `0.9` of a tiny one has almost none. A pure ratio policy will
prefer the second. Production scores therefore combine the two — typically ranking on share but
filtering on an absolute floor, so a lane with fewer than some number of requests left is simply not
offered however good its fraction looks.

**The failure that only appears with real traffic.** Everything above assumes the ceilings are known.
Real free tiers change them without notice — Addendum 02 records the December 2025 cut as precedent —
and a ratio computed against a stale `rpd` is confidently wrong in a way an absolute count is not: if
the real ceiling halved, your "fifty per cent remaining" is actually zero. This is why the freshness
check exists, and why the hub's §8 records the date these numbers were verified rather than treating
them as constants.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"`by_ratio` divides by `rpm` and `rpd` from config.
If the provider cuts our tier and we do not update the config, this function reports headroom that
does not exist and it will do it silently. Reconcile against the provider's own remaining-quota
headers when they send them, and alarm when the two disagree by more than a few per cent."*

**The interview question:** *"You have four providers with different rate limits. How do you decide
which to call?"* A shallow answer says "the one with the most capacity left". Better: *"Most capacity
is the wrong comparison, because remaining requests are not comparable across providers with
different ceilings — fifty left out of fifty and fifty left out of two hundred and fifty are not the
same thing, and the naive comparison drains the small one first. I compare each provider's remaining
share of its own limit. On my workload that served 233 of 300 calls against 222 for the naive
version, and kept the scarce provider alive half as long again. I would also say what it did not fix:
the scarce provider's whole daily allowance still went, because comparison functions decide the
order you spend in, not how much there is."*

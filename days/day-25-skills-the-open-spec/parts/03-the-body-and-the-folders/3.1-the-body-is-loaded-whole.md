# 3.1 — The body is loaded whole

## One-line answer

Once a skill is activated its **entire** `SKILL.md` body enters the context — all 292 tokens of
`ticket-triage`, not the paragraph that was relevant — which is why the spec recommends keeping the
file under 500 lines and moving detail into `references/`.

## The story

The flat-pack box has a single sheet of instructions folded into eight, and you unfold all of it to
find step one.

It covers the table. Steps one to thirty-four, four exploded diagrams, a parts list, the same warning
in six languages, and a note about the wall bracket you are not fitting. You are looking for which of
two nearly-identical screws goes into the top hole, and to find that out you have opened a sheet the
size of the table you are building.

Nobody minds much, because it is paper and paper is free. The moment it stops being free is the moment
you are charged by the square centimetre for every sheet you unfold — at which point the sensible
design is a small card for the common case and a separate sheet for the wall bracket, sent only to
people fitting one.

## The idea in plain language

The `SKILL.md` body is everything after the closing `---` of the frontmatter. The specification is
explicit about its format and about what happens to it:

> The Markdown body after the frontmatter contains the skill instructions. There are no format
> restrictions. Write whatever helps agents perform the task effectively.

and, in the same section:

> Note that the agent will load this entire file once it's decided to activate a skill. Consider
> splitting longer `SKILL.md` content into referenced files.

**Entire file.** Not the relevant section, not the matching heading — the whole thing, into the model's
context, on activation. There is no partial loading and no selection within a skill.

That gives the body one governing constraint and one governing freedom.

**The constraint is cost.** Every line in the body is paid for on the request that activated the skill,
and — because a chat model is stateless — on every subsequent turn of that conversation
([24.1.2](../../../day-24-token-accounting-and-budgets/parts/01-what-a-request-costs/1.2-charged-again-every-turn.md)).
A body that grows to three thousand tokens has quietly made every turn of every triage conversation
three thousand tokens more expensive.

**The freedom is that it is a prompt, and you may write it like one.** No schema, no required sections,
no field names. Steps in the order they happen, the edge cases that have actually bitten, a worked
example, a sentence about what not to do. The spec's own recommendation is modest: *step-by-step
instructions, examples of inputs and outputs, common edge cases.*

And the spec supplies two numbers to design against:

- **Keep `SKILL.md` under 500 lines.**
- **Instructions under about 5,000 tokens** is the recommended budget for the activated body
  ([3.3](3.3-progressive-disclosure.md) has the full three-level table).

Neither is enforced. Both are the difference between a skill that is cheap to activate and one that
makes every conversation it touches expensive.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because the obvious way to write a skill is to put everything in it, and the obvious way is wrong in a
way that only shows up on the bill.

Sutra's triage procedure has a severity table with three rows today. It will have twelve. There will be
a section about escalation, one about what to do when the customer has already been told something
different, and an example of a well-written triage note. Written into the body, that is a skill whose
activation cost grows every time somebody improves it — and improving it is exactly what should be
encouraged.

The alternative the format offers is `references/`
([3.2](3.2-scripts-references-assets.md)): the body says *"compare against the table in
[references/SEVERITY.md](...)"*, and the table is loaded only if the agent needs it. Same knowledge,
paid for only when used.

Getting this split right is what makes a skill affordable on a twenty-request day, and it is the whole
practical content of progressive disclosure.

## The mechanism

Here is the `ticket-triage` body in full. Twenty lines, and every one of them is loaded together.

```text
# Ticket triage

Assign one of three severities to a support ticket and write it to session state.

## Steps

1. Read the ticket with `lookup_ticket`. If it returns `status: not_found`, stop and say so.
2. Compare the ticket text against the table in
   [references/SEVERITY.md](references/SEVERITY.md).
3. Call `remember_triage` with the ticket id and the chosen severity.
4. Tell the engineer which severity you chose and which row of the table decided it.

## Edge cases

- A ticket that matches no row is `normal`, and you must say that no row matched.
- Never invent a ticket id. If the lookup fails, the answer is that the ticket does not exist.
- Do not change a severity that is already set unless the engineer asks.
```

**Line by line:**

- The first line after the heading is a **one-sentence summary of what this skill does**. That is not
  duplication of the description: the description was written for *selection*, and this is written for
  a model that has already been given the file and is about to act on it.
- The steps are **numbered and imperative**, and each one names the tool it uses in backticks. A model
  reading `lookup_ticket` in backticks alongside a tool declaration of the same name has an
  unambiguous referent; prose saying *"look the ticket up"* does not.
- Step 1 carries its own failure branch — `If it returns status: not_found, stop and say so` — rather
  than deferring it to Edge cases. Failure handling that sits next to the step it belongs to is
  followed; failure handling in a section at the bottom is skimmed.
- Step 2 is the whole argument of this part: **a link, not a table.** Those two lines cost about a
  dozen tokens; the table they point at is loaded only when the agent follows them.
- Step 4 says what to *tell the engineer*, which is the step people leave out. A procedure that ends
  when the state is written produces an agent that silently does the right thing, and silence is
  indistinguishable from having done nothing.
- The three Edge cases are each one sentence and each one is a rule that came from something going
  wrong. This section is where a skill accumulates its real value over time, and it is also where it
  accumulates its token cost — which is the tension the next part resolves.
- There are **no HTML comments, no metadata, no schema**. The body is what you would write on a wiki
  page for a colleague, and that is the format's actual design intent.

**Line by line:**

- Counts the startup card and the whole file for each skill. Needs a key, spends **0** generations
  ([24.1.1](../../../day-24-token-accounting-and-budgets/parts/01-what-a-request-costs/1.1-a-ticket-id-costs-five-tokens.md)).

Measured on 2026-09-04 against `gemini-3.7-flash`:

```text
skill                 startup  activated   ratio
bad-name                   31         72     2.3x
ticket-triage              64        292     4.6x
vague-description          20         55     2.8x
```

**292 tokens on activation**, against 64 to sit there available. And 292 is a *small* body — twenty
lines, three steps, three edge cases. The spec's recommended ceiling of about 5,000 tokens is
seventeen times this, which tells you how much room the format expects a serious skill to use and how
quickly a body can grow into it.

The number that matters is not 292 on its own. It is 292 **multiplied by every turn of the conversation
that activated it**, because the activated body stays in the context. An eight-turn triage conversation
carries it eight times, which — using Day 24's arithmetic — is over two thousand tokens spent on a
procedure that could have been three hundred if the detail had been behind a link.

## When it breaks

There is no error. A body of any size is valid, and the validator has nothing to say about length:

```text
Valid skill: days\day-25-skills-the-open-spec\lab\skills\ticket-triage
```

The failures are all gradual.

**The body that swallowed the reference material.** Somebody pastes the twelve-row severity table into
the body because it is easier to read there. Activation cost triples. Nothing breaks, the skill works
better if anything, and the cost is invisible until somebody counts.

**The body that became a manual.** Sections for every variant, a FAQ, a changelog at the bottom. Past
the spec's 500-line guidance, the whole thing lands in context and competes with the conversation for
room — and [19.3.3](../../../day-19-context-engineering-selection/parts/03-selection/3.3-position-is-not-presence.md)'s
finding applies: material in the middle of a long context is attended to least, so the twelve
paragraphs you added may be the ones the model reads least carefully.

**And the body that assumes it was read selectively.** Writing *"see the section on escalation below"*
is fine; writing a skill whose first half only applies to one kind of ticket and whose second half
applies to another is writing two skills. The model receives both halves and has to work out which one
it is in, on every activation, forever. The one-idea test from this curriculum's own contract applies
to skills too.

## In production

**A skill's body is reviewed for length the way a function is reviewed for length.** The teams that keep
skills cheap have a habit rather than a rule: when the body passes a page, ask what could be a
reference. The answer is almost always the tables, the enumerations and the worked examples — the
things a model needs *sometimes*, not the steps it needs *always*.

**Write the body for the model, not for a reader.** They overlap more than you would expect, but they
differ in one way that matters: a human reader skims and finds the relevant heading, and a model
receives it all at once with no skimming. Anything you would have expected the reader to skip is
paying for itself in nobody's attention.

**What changes at scale** is that bodies drift toward being documentation. A skill is the only file
where the procedure is written down, so it accumulates the audience notes, the rationale, the history
of why the rule exists — all valuable, none of it needed by the model at the moment of acting. The
split that works is: **the body says what to do, `references/` says why and what the details are**, and
a link between them costs a dozen tokens.

**The failure that only appears with real traffic** is a long body making a conversation expensive
rather than making one request expensive. The activation is fine. The eighth turn of the conversation
is carrying the whole skill for the eighth time, and the cost lands on the requests that are already
the largest. Anything that adds fixed content to a conversation should be measured across the
conversation, not across a request.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"this table is in the body, so we load it every time
the skill fires, including the ninety percent of triages where the ticket obviously matches row one.
Move it to `references/` and link it."*

**The interview question:** *"what happens when a skill is activated?"* The answer that shows you have
measured it: *"the whole `SKILL.md` body goes into the context — not the relevant part, the whole file
— and it stays there for the rest of the conversation. So the body should be the steps and the edge
cases, and anything bulky that's only needed sometimes goes in `references/` behind a link. The spec
suggests under 500 lines and around 5,000 tokens for the body, and the real constraint is that you pay
for it on every turn after activation, not just once."*

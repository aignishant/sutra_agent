# 1.3 — What the listener actually got

## One-line answer

Both standups called all three tools, read the queue correctly and said only true things — and when
the listener spoke after two sentences, attention-first had delivered **3 of 3** sentences that
needed a person while queue order had delivered **0 of 2**.

## The story

You catch somebody on the stairs.

They are on their way out, jacket half on, and they have asked you how it went. What you have is
between here and the door, and you know it, so you say the thing they can do something about and then
whatever else fits.

The version of this that goes wrong is not rudeness or incompetence. It is starting at the beginning.
*"So we had twelve tickets in, mostly the usual sign-in stuff, there was one on the export thing that
turned out to be the same as last week—"* and they are at the door, and they are nodding, and the
refund that needed their approval was going to be the fourth thing you said.

You told the truth. You told it in order. They heard the part that needed nothing from them.

## The idea in plain language

Part [1.2](1.2-the-three-tools.md) established that the plumbing works: three tools, called in one
turn, each answered by the runtime before the next. This part is the first measurement of the thing
the plumbing was for, and it is deliberately unkind to the assumption that a working agent is a
finished one.

The setup is one line of the lab: **the listener says something after two sentences.** Not because
they are impatient — because that is what people on an open line do. Day 75 part 2.2 measured the
consequence: an interruption stops the answer, and the model stops producing. So a spoken standup is
not a document that gets delivered. It is a document that gets delivered **up to a point somebody
else chooses**.

Which means the question *"is this standup any good?"* has an operational form:

**Of the sentences that needed a person, how many were said before the listener stopped it?**

That is a strange question to ask about a report, and it is the right one, because it is the only one
whose answer changes when you reorder identical facts.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because this is the day the phase's four previous days become a product, and the temptation at that
moment is to check that it works and ship it. It works. Both arms work.

And because the gate cannot check this. The gate can assert that `lines(...)[0][1] is True` — that
the first sentence is flagged — and that is a proxy for what is measured here. What is measured here
needs a listener, an interruption and a session, which is why it is a script rather than a check, and
why part [2.1](../02-the-order-is-the-product/2.1-one-function-one-order.md) argues so hard for
putting the decision somewhere a test can reach.

## The mechanism

The listener's half is four lines of the driver, quoted at its own indentation from inside the
consuming loop — which is why the fence is tagged as text rather than as Python:

```text
            heard += 1
            if heard >= PATIENCE and not spoke:
                spoke = True
                queue.send_content(
                    types.Content(role="user", parts=[types.Part(text="yes, I saw that one")])
                )
```

**Line by line:**

- `heard` counts sentences that actually reached the listener, not events. An event with no text —
  and there are several in a turn that calls three tools — is not something anybody heard.
- `PATIENCE` is `2`, and it is the whole experimental design. It is not a claim about how patient
  people are; it is a fixed, small number applied identically to both arms, which is what makes the
  comparison mean something.
- `not spoke` makes the interruption happen exactly once. A listener who talks over every sentence is
  a different experiment.
- `"yes, I saw that one"` is what a person actually says when they want you to stop and move on. It
  is not a command, it is not an error, and on an open line it stops the standup — which is Day 75
  part 2.2's finding arriving as an ordinary morning.

Run it:

```bash
cd days/day-77-the-standup-agent/lab
uv run python live.py; echo "exit: $?"
```

**Line by line:**

- `live.py` opens a real `run_live` session, lets the model call all three tools, and starts speaking
  the standup one sentence at a time.
- `echo "exit: $?"` prints the exit code, because the claim is the exit code: `0` when the listener
  heard something that needed them.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
order: attention first
the listener spoke after 2 sentences

  tools the model called       ['queue_by_category', 'tickets_needing_a_person', 'last_night']
  sentences delivered          3
  > Two tickets need you: 4688, 4740.
  > 4688, refund over the limit - needs an approval.
  > 4740, waiting on a reply from us since the eleventh day.

  the standup was interrupted  yes
  the session ended            yes
  sentences needing a person   3 of 3 delivered

  The listener stopped the standup and had already heard what needed them. Every
  other fact is still available on request; that one was not going to be.
exit: 0
```

Now the same queue, the same tools, the same interruption, said in the order the tickets happen to sit
in:

```bash
uv run python live.py --flat; echo "exit: $?"
```

**Line by line:**

- `--flat` selects the other ordering and changes nothing else — not the state, not the tools, not the
  listener's behaviour, not the moment they speak.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
order: queue order (ablation)
the listener spoke after 2 sentences

  tools the model called       ['queue_by_category', 'tickets_needing_a_person', 'last_night']
  sentences delivered          3
    There are 12 tickets open.
    4610, sign-in, open 2 days.
    4633, billing, open 1 day.

  the standup was interrupted  yes
  the session ended            yes
  sentences needing a person   0 of 2 delivered

  Three tools were called, the queue was read correctly, and the listener walked away
  with the part that needed nothing from them. Nothing failed. The standup was
  accurate, complete in intent, and useless.
exit: 1
```

| | attention first | queue order |
| --- | --- | --- |
| tools called | 3 | 3 |
| sentences delivered | 3 | 3 |
| sentences needing a person, delivered | **3 of 3** | 0 of 2 |
| the standup was interrupted | yes | yes |
| anything false said | no | no |
| exit code | 0 | 1 |

**Read the first two rows and the fifth together.** Same tools. Same number of sentences delivered.
Nothing false in either. Every measurement people normally take of an agent is identical across the
two runs, and one of them told the listener something they could act on while the other did not.

**And read the failing run's three sentences.** *"There are 12 tickets open. 4610, sign-in, open 2
days. 4633, billing, open 1 day."* There is nothing wrong with any of that. It is accurate, it is
relevant, it is what a person would say if they started at the beginning — and the refund waiting on
an approval is sentence six.

## When it breaks

**`PATIENCE = 2` is a choice, and the finding depends on it in an obvious way and not in an
interesting one.** A listener who hears the whole standup gets everything in both orders; a listener
who stops after one sentence gets one useful sentence in one arm and none in the other. The number
moves the size of the gap and not its direction, and the honest statement is that this measures a
listener who stops early rather than a typical listener, because this lab has no typical listener.

**The interruption here is a message, not a person.** The lab sends text into the live queue at a
chosen moment. A real listener interrupts by making a noise, which has to be detected — Day 76
section 3's whole subject — and detection is imperfect in both directions. What is measured here is
what happens *after* an interruption is recognised.

**Nothing here says the flagged sentences are the right ones to flag.** The measurement rewards
putting flagged sentences first. If everything is flagged, the score is perfect and the standup is the
flat report with more confidence. That is not a hypothetical failure; it is the obvious way to make
this number look good, and part [2.2](../02-the-order-is-the-product/2.2-carry-the-flag.md) is about
where the flag should come from.

**And the two arms are not equally long, which muddies one thing.** Attention-first is five sentences
and queue order is fourteen, so the flat version was always going to be cut off further from its end.
Part [2.3](../02-the-order-is-the-product/2.3-words-before-it-matters.md) separates those two effects
by measuring words before the first flagged line — which is `0` against `25` and does not depend on
total length at all.

## In production

**What this changes about how a spoken agent is reviewed.** Reading the transcript is not review.
Both transcripts read well; one of them is a good standup and the other is a good list. The review
question is *what does a listener have after the first two sentences*, and answering it requires
somebody to imagine being interrupted, which nobody does unprompted.

**What a professional measures once this is real.** Where in the report the listener stopped —
because that is recorded for free on a voice session and is the closest thing to an attention signal
you will get. A rising "stopped at sentence two" across a week is a report that has grown a preamble.

**What changes at scale.** Reports accumulate sections. Somebody adds a line about deployments,
somebody adds a line about the on-call rota, and each addition is individually reasonable and lands
in front of the part that matters, because appending is easier than deciding. The number that degrades
is the position of the first flagged sentence, and nothing complains.

**What this shares with the rest of the phase.** Day 73 part 3.2 measured a written digest that named
zero of two failing evals while every number in it was true. This is the same finding with a listener
instead of a reader, and the interruption makes it sharper: a reader can scroll back, and a listener
cannot.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Both of these are correct and one of them is
useless, so let's stop reviewing the transcript. Put what needs a decision first, and add the test
that asserts the first line is the flagged one — the wording will change fifty times and that
assertion should survive all of them. And when this is live, log which sentence we were on when the
listener spoke. If that number drifts down over a month, somebody has added a preamble."*

**The interview question:** *"How would you tell whether a spoken report is any good?"* An honest
answer: *"Not by whether it's correct, because the bad version is also correct. On a voice channel the
listener interrupts, so the report is only delivered up to a point they choose — which means the
useful measurement is how much of the actionable part was said before they stopped it. I measured
exactly that: same queue, same three tool calls, same listener interrupting after two sentences.
Attention-first delivered all three sentences that needed a decision; the queue-order version
delivered none of the two, and every sentence it did say was true and relevant. Everything you'd
normally measure — tools called, sentences delivered, accuracy — was identical. The one caveat is that
the measurement rewards flagging things, so it only means something if the flag comes from the data
rather than from whoever wrote the report."*

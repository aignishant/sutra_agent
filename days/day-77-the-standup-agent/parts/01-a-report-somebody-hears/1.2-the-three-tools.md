# 1.2 — The three tools, called for real

## One-line answer

The standup agent is the first thing in this curriculum to call **three tools in one turn** — the
runtime answering each one and pushing the result back through the open connection before the next
call is emitted — and it worked the first time it was run, which is worth saying plainly, because
the ease of the plumbing is exactly what makes it tempting to think the agent is finished when the
tools work.

## The story

You go into the pharmacy and ask whether they have the thing your doctor wrote down.

Watch what she actually does. She turns and looks at the shelf behind her — that is one place. Then
she types something into the screen on the counter and reads it — that is a second place. Then she
glances at a note stuck to the till with tape, in somebody else's handwriting — that is a third.
None of that takes long and none of it is hard. She has done it a thousand times and she does not
have to think about any of the three.

Then she looks up and tells you something, and what she tells you is not in any of the three places.
She says: *we've got the smaller box now, or I can order yours in and it'll be here Thursday — but
if you want it tonight, the shop by the station had some yesterday.* The shelf did not say that. The
screen did not say that. The note certainly did not. She read three things and then made a decision
about what you needed to hear, in what order, and what to leave out — and the decision is the part
you came in for.

That is this whole part, and this whole day. The looking-up is routine. The three tools below are
the shelf, the screen and the note: real, really called, and completely uninteresting once they
work. What the agent says afterwards is the difficult thing, and nothing about having three working
tools makes it one bit easier.

## The idea in plain language

Three words, all of which you have met, and one that is new today.

A **tool** is an ordinary Python function the agent is allowed to ask for. A **turn** is one side's
go in the conversation, and on an open line the model has to declare when its turn is over, because
nothing else can tell — Day 75 part 3.2 was about who sets that flag.

A **function call** is the model producing a small structured message that says *run this tool*: a
name and a bag of arguments, nothing more. A **function response** is the answer coming back the
other way, carrying the name of the tool that ran and whatever it returned. Day 76 part 1.2 laid
that out as a four-step round trip in which the **runtime does the middle two**: the model asks and
does not finish its turn, the runtime looks the name up and runs the function, the runtime pushes
the result back into the connection, and only then does the model say something. Steps one and four
are the model's; steps two and three are not.

The new word is what happens when you do that three times without the model ever finishing its turn.
Call it a **multi-tool turn**: three complete round trips, one after another, inside a single go.
The model asks for the first tool and goes quiet. The result arrives. It wakes up, asks for the
second tool, and goes quiet again. The result arrives. Third, same again. Only after the third
result does it produce any words at all.

Two things are worth being precise about, because both are easy to get wrong from a diagram.

**This is one turn, not three.** Every one of those three requests is marked *I have not finished*.
If any of them had claimed the turn was over, the runtime would have treated the exchange as done
and the standup would never have been said — Day 76 part 1.2 walks through exactly that failure.

**The three calls are sequential, not simultaneous.** In this lab the second call is not emitted
until the first result has come back through the connection. That is the scripted model's behaviour,
not a rule of the protocol; a real model can ask for several tools in the same message. The
distinction matters when you read this day's measurements: what has been demonstrated here is three
round trips in a row, each completed before the next began.

Here are the three, and what the standup does with each:

| Tool | What it returns | What the standup does with it | What it would cost against a real desk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `queue_by_category` | a mapping of category to a count of open tickets | one sentence about the shape of the queue | one counting read against the ticket table, grouped by category — cheap, indexed, one round trip |
| `tickets_needing_a_person` | a mapping of ticket reference to the reason it is stuck | the sentences that need somebody, said first | one filtered read, but the *reason* text often lives elsewhere — an escalation note or a comments table — so plausibly two reads or a second service |
| `last_night` | the nightly job's stage outcomes and its failing cases | one sentence about the night | not the ticket database at all: a row or a file the nightly job wrote. Cheap to read, and it is the one that can be **absent**, which is part [3.2](../03-the-quiet-morning/3.2-saying-what-you-do-not-know.md) |

**The last column is honest guesswork.** In this lab all three tools read a frozen fixture — a small
Python module with the numbers typed into it — so all three are effectively free and instant. The
column is what those same three questions would plausibly cost against a real support desk, written
down so that the difference between *this lab* and *a desk* stays visible. Nothing in this part
measured it.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because the whole of Phase 11 has been building towards an agent that reads real state and says
something out loud, and this is the part where the reading works.

Day 76 measured **one** tool call inside a live session, and measured it carefully: which two of the
four steps belong to the runtime, what reaches the connection while the tool runs, and what happens
to the caller when the tool blocks. That was a day about the mechanism. Today's agent needs three
answers before it can open its mouth, and if getting three had been fragile — results arriving out
of order, a turn ending early, a second call never emitted — then today would have been a second day
about the mechanism and the actual subject would have slid to Day 78.

It was not fragile. Three calls, in order, each answered, first run.

That is the fact this part exists to establish, and it has a second job: it is the setup for the
trap. The hub's §7 lists *assuming the tools are the hard part* as trap two, and traps like that are
not stupidity — they are what happens when the visible difficulty and the real difficulty are in
different places. You can watch three tool calls resolve, see a queue read accurately, and
reasonably conclude that the hard bit is behind you. Part [1.3](1.3-what-the-listener-got.md) is the
measurement that says otherwise, and part [1.1](1.1-a-report-with-one-chance.md) already said why:
a spoken report arrives in one order and the listener does not skim.

## The mechanism

### The run

This is the day's live script, on its default arm, run on 2026-09-06:

```bash
cd days/day-77-the-standup-agent/lab
uv run python live.py
```

**Line by line:**

- `cd .../lab` is required rather than tidy. `live.py` does `import _live`, a plain module import
  that resolves against the directory the script lives in; run it from the repo root and you get
  `ModuleNotFoundError: No module named '_live'` before anything else happens.
- `uv run` executes it inside this project's environment, so the ADK doing the work is the version
  this repository pins — `google-adk==2.7.1`, the version the hub's §8 read the multi-tool turn off
  — rather than whatever Python happens to be first on your `PATH` (Principle 7).
- No flag. `live.py --flat` is the ablation — the same standup with the queue in its natural order —
  and it belongs to part [1.3](1.3-what-the-listener-got.md), which is where the two arms are compared.
- **No API key is needed and no provider is called.** The model's side is scripted. The runner, the
  live request queue, the tool lookup and the three tool functions are the real ones, which is why
  the request budget in the hub's §6 is zero to every provider and the tool calls are still real.

### What came back

This is the head of that run's output, pasted exactly as it printed:

```text
order: attention first
the listener spoke after 2 sentences

  tools the model called       ['queue_by_category', 'tickets_needing_a_person', 'last_night']
  sentences delivered          3
```

**Line by line:**

- `order: attention first` names the arm. The standup's wording was built by `standup.py`, which is
  section 2's subject; this part is not reading the words.
- `the listener spoke after 2 sentences` is the script's interruption, and it is here only because
  it printed. What it did, and what it cost, is part [1.3](1.3-what-the-listener-got.md)'s entire
  subject.
- `tools the model called` is **the line this part is about**. Three names, in the order their
  results came back — and the list is not a copy of the script. It is built at the connection, by
  appending `part.function_response.name` every time a result arrives through `send_content`. So
  what you are reading is not *what the model asked for*; it is *what the runtime actually ran and
  handed back*, recorded where the traffic is.
- `queue_by_category` is first because it was asked for first, and it is followed by
  `tickets_needing_a_person` because the second call was not emitted until the first result had
  landed. The list is therefore evidence of ordering as well as of completeness.
- `sentences delivered 3`, and everything printed underneath it, **belongs to part
  [1.3](1.3-what-the-listener-got.md)**. Reading it now spoils a measurement that has not been set up
  yet. The tool list is the whole of this part's evidence.

### The three tools

These are the three tool functions from `lab/_live.py`, quoted whole. They are module-level
functions, so nothing has been dedented or trimmed:

```python
async def queue_by_category() -> dict:
    """How many tickets are open in each category.

    Returns:
        A mapping of category to the number of open tickets.
    """
    return _state.by_category()


async def tickets_needing_a_person() -> dict:
    """The tickets that will not move without somebody deciding something.

    Returns:
        A mapping with one entry per blocked ticket: its reference and why it is stuck.
    """
    return {t.ref: t.why for t in _state.needs_a_person()}


async def last_night() -> dict:
    """What the nightly job did.

    Returns:
        The stage outcomes and the failing eval cases.
    """
    return dict(_state.LAST_NIGHT)
```

**Line by line:**

- All three are `async def`, and **none of them awaits anything**. That looks like a mistake and is
  not one: each reads a frozen fixture — `_state.py`, a module with twelve tickets and one nightly
  job typed into it — so there is nothing to wait for. Declaring them `async` matches the shape the
  rest of the day expects and costs nothing here.
- It would stop being free the moment one of them touched a database, and Day 76 part 1.3 is the
  measurement of exactly that: a tool declared `async def` whose body blocks anyway lets **zero**
  slices of the caller's audio reach the connection while it runs, against four when it awaits. The
  keyword is a promise about the body, not a property of it. These three keep the promise by having
  no body worth blocking on.
- **The docstrings are interface, not commentary.** The runtime turns each function into a tool
  declaration, and the summary line plus the `Returns:` block is what the model reads when it
  decides which tool it needs. Rewrite `tickets_needing_a_person`'s first line as "gets blocked
  stuff" and you have not made the code worse — you have made the model's choice worse. That is why
  they are written as sentences about the desk rather than about the code.
- `-> dict` on all three is the other half of that interface. A tool has to return something the
  runtime can put into a `function_response`, which in practice means something JSON-shaped: a dict,
  a list, a string, a number. A `Ticket` dataclass instance is not, which is why the middle function
  converts rather than returning `_state.needs_a_person()` directly.
- `tickets_needing_a_person` returns **a mapping of reference to reason**, and that choice is the
  most load-bearing line in the fragment. It would have been easier to return a count. But a standup
  that says *two need you* and cannot say *why* has saved nobody a single lookup — the listener now
  has to go and open two tickets to find out what you already knew. The reason travels with the
  reference or the tool has not done its job. Compare `queue_by_category`, which returns counts and
  is right to: nobody acts on "four billing tickets", so the count is the whole of the fact.
- `t.why` is a field on the ticket, not something derived from the text. That is a rule with a whole
  part behind it — part [2.2](../02-the-order-is-the-product/2.2-carry-the-flag.md) is a real bug
  from this lab caused by recovering importance from wording instead of carrying it from the data.
- `dict(_state.LAST_NIGHT)` copies rather than returning the module's own dictionary. The fixture is
  shared by every script on this day, and handing a caller a live reference to it is how a fixture
  quietly stops being fixed.

### The three calls, in a fixed order

This is the tuple that decides what gets asked for, at module level in `lab/_live.py`:

```python
CALLS = ("queue_by_category", "tickets_needing_a_person", "last_night")
```

**Line by line:**

- Three strings, and each one **must** match the name of a Python function passed to the agent in
  `tools=[...]`. The runtime resolves the name in the `function_call` against that list; a name that
  is not there raises rather than being skipped, which is exercise two below.
- A tuple rather than a list because it is a constant. Nothing in the run mutates it.
- The order in the tuple is the order the calls come out in, and therefore the order in the printed
  tool list. The two agreeing is a check worth making: if they ever disagree, something has
  reordered results, and results arriving out of order is a much more interesting bug than a
  standup with the wrong wording.

And this is the branch of `ScriptedConnection.receive` that emits them. It sits inside a class, so
the common leading indentation has been removed to bring it to the left margin; nothing else is
changed:

```python
while True:
    if await self.inbox.get() == self.HANGUP:
        return
    if self.step < len(CALLS):
        call = types.FunctionCall(name=CALLS[self.step], args={})
        self.step += 1
        yield LlmResponse(
            content=types.Content(role="model", parts=[types.Part(function_call=call)]),
            turn_complete=False,
        )
        continue
```

**Line by line:**

- `while True:` with `await self.inbox.get()` at the top is the scripted model **asleep**. It does
  nothing at all until something arrives. In this lab "something arrives" means `send_content` put
  an item in the queue, and `send_content` is called by the runtime when it has a result to deliver.
  In a real connection this wait is a socket read and the waking happens on somebody else's machine.
- `if ... == self.HANGUP: return` is the shutdown path. Ending the generator ends the model's side
  of the call, and Day 75 part 1.3 is the part about who is responsible for that happening at all.
- `if self.step < len(CALLS):` is the entire script. While there are unasked tools, ask for the next
  one; once there are none, fall through to speaking, which is below this fragment and is section
  2's material.
- `types.FunctionCall(name=CALLS[self.step], args={})` builds the request. Note `args={}` — none of
  these three tools takes an argument, so there is nothing for the model to get wrong. That is a
  simplification with a real edge: the moment a tool takes a date range or a category, choosing the
  argument becomes part of what you are trusting the model with, and this day is not testing that.
- `self.step += 1` advances **only when a call is emitted**. Nothing else in the class touches it.
  So the counter is not a clock and not a loop index — it is a record of how many tools have been
  asked for, and it is what makes the next pass through the loop ask for a different one.
- `turn_complete=False` is the load-bearing keyword, and it appears on **all three** calls. It is the
  model saying *I have asked, I am not finished*. Three requests, none of them ending the turn, is
  precisely what makes this one turn containing three round trips rather than three turns.
- `continue` goes back to the top of the loop and blocks on the inbox again. That is the model going
  quiet after asking. The next thing to arrive will be the runtime's function response for the tool
  it just requested — steps two and three of Day 76 part 1.2's sequence happen entirely inside that
  wait, and the connection records the result's name on the way past.
- **A real model would decide all of this**, and would not necessarily decide it this way. It might
  call one tool, or none, or the same tool twice, or one you did not think was relevant to a
  standup. The fixed order here is a simplification chosen so that the day's later measurements are
  comparable: the two arms of every measurement call exactly the same three tools in exactly the same
  order, so any difference between the arms cannot be blamed on the tools.

## When it breaks

**Nothing here says anything about latency, failure or staleness.** All three tools read a frozen
fixture and return instantly, so the run above has demonstrated that three calls resolve in order —
and nothing else. It has not shown what happens when a tool is slow, because none of these is; it
has not shown what happens when one raises, because none of these can; and it has not shown what
happens when the data is out of date, because a fixture is never out of date. Day 76 measured what a
slow tool does to a live session, with a caller on the line during the pause, and that measurement
stands unchanged. **Instant tools are a property of this lab, not of this design.**

**Three tools in a fixed order is a script, not a model.** Everything the "model" did here was typed
into `CALLS`. A real one chooses per turn, and the honest list of what it may do is longer than what
this file does: call all three, call one, call none and simply answer from the conversation, call
the same tool twice, or call one you did not expect a standup to need. Two consequences follow.
First, the tool list printed above is evidence about the *runtime*, not about a model's judgement,
which is not being exercised on this day at all. Second — and this is the one that reaches into
section 2 — **the standup's wording must not assume all three answers arrived**. A sentence built by
gluing three results together will produce nonsense the first morning one of them is missing, which
is the failure part [3.2](../03-the-quiet-morning/3.2-saying-what-you-do-not-know.md) is built
around.

**And the interesting failure is the one that is not on this page.** Every day in this curriculum
carries a part whose subject is a deliberate failure, and this part does not have one, because
nothing here broke. Three tool calls, first run, right order, correct data. Say that plainly and
then say the uncomfortable half: **none of it makes the day's actual problem any easier.** The
problem is what to say and in what order, and it is not helped by a fourth tool, a faster tool, a
retry policy or a cleverer schema. You can have perfect tools and a useless standup, and the next
two parts are the measurement that proves it. The tools working is not progress towards the answer;
it is the point at which you find out the answer was somewhere else.

## In production

**What a professional decides about a standup's tools, before writing any of them.** Three
decisions, and none of them is about the code:

| Decision | The answer for a standup | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| read or write? | **read-only, without exception** | a report is not an actor. A morning report that can also close a ticket is a morning report that can close a ticket by accident, at a moment when nobody is watching it and the person it is talking to is making tea. This is Day 68's least-privilege argument applied to the least dangerous-looking agent you will build |
| how expensive? | cheap enough to call **every single morning, for every listener** | a tool that is only affordable occasionally will get cached, and a cached standup is a standup that reports yesterday. The cost budget is set by the cadence, not the other way round |
| what happens when one fails? | **the standup says so, in words** | not silence, not a gap, not a cheerful summary of the two tools that did work — part [3.2](../03-the-quiet-morning/3.2-saying-what-you-do-not-know.md), and Principle 10 |

The third row is the one that gets deferred, and deferring it has a specific shape: the standup is
built and tested on a morning when all three tools answered, so the wording quietly assumes three
answers, and the code that would have said *I could not read last night's job* was never written
because the situation never came up in development. It comes up in production on a morning when
something is actually wrong, which is the worst possible morning to be missing a sentence.

**What changes at scale is that three becomes eight and the model starts choosing.** A real desk
does not have three questions; it has the queue, the escalations, last night's job, the on-call
rota, the incident channel, yesterday's deploys, the customers with a review this week, and the
thing your manager asked about. At three tools with no arguments, the model's choice is barely a
choice. At eight, three things change at once. The docstrings stop being documentation and become
the routing table, so two tools with overlapping summaries will be confused with each other and the
fix is a wording change rather than a code change. The turn gets longer, because every extra call is
another complete round trip with the listener waiting through it, so the question *does the standup
need this tool every morning* becomes a real question with a cost attached. And the standup's
wording has to survive **any subset** of the answers arriving, which is a much stronger requirement
than surviving all of them — and the only way to know it does is to test the subsets deliberately,
because the model will produce them for you eventually and will not warn you first.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"The tool path is fine and I have no comments on
it, which is my actual comment. Three things though. One: the docstrings are the model's interface,
so they go through review like any other public API — 'The tickets that will not move without
somebody deciding something' is doing real work and I do not want it 'tidied' into 'returns blocked
tickets' by someone shortening lines. Two: `args={}` everywhere means we have never once exercised
argument selection, so the first tool we add that takes a category or a date range is a new class of
bug, not a new tool — plan a test for it before you plan the tool. Three: I want the wording layer
tested against a missing answer now, while it is cheap. Delete `last_night` from `CALLS` in a test
and assert the standup still reads as a sentence somebody would say out loud. Right now the only
evidence we have is that it works when everything works, and that is the case that was never in
doubt."*

**The interview question:** *"You said your voice agent calls three tools in one turn. What actually
happens between the first call and the second?"* An answer that shows the work: *"Three complete
round trips, and the model is asleep for most of them. It emits a function call — a name and some
arguments, nothing else, because the model can't run anything itself — and it marks the turn as not
complete, which is the important bit: it's saying 'I've asked, I'm not finished'. Then the runtime
resolves that name against the tools the agent was given, runs the function, wraps what came back in
a function response and pushes it into the model's connection. That wakes the model up, and it asks
for the second tool. Same again for the third. So it's one turn containing three round trips, not
three turns, and the calls are sequential in my implementation — the second isn't emitted until the
first result has landed. The way I know the runtime actually did it rather than my script pretending
is that I record the tool names at the connection, off the function responses coming back, not off
the requests going out. What I'd want to say honestly is that this was the easy part. It worked
first time and it told me nothing about whether the agent was any good — the thing that decided
whether the standup was worth listening to was the order of the sentences afterwards, and I had to
measure that separately."*

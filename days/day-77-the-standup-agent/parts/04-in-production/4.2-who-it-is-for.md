# 4.2 — Who it is for

## One-line answer

"Needs a person" is not a property of a ticket — it is a property of a **ticket and a listener**, so
the same twelve tickets make three different correct standups for three different people, and the
ordering rule this whole day has been arguing for only means something once you have said who is
listening.

**This part measures nothing, and you should know that before you read it.** Every other part of this
day put two numbers side by side. This one cannot: the lab has one fixture, one ordering rule and one
imagined listener, and you cannot compare audiences when you only have one. What follows is an
argument, not a result. Where a reader would reasonably want evidence, this part hands you the exact
thing to go and try, marked `TODO(me)`, rather than a number it did not earn.

## The story

A woman in her seventies slips on the kitchen floor. She is bruised, nothing is broken, and by the
evening she has told three people about it.

She tells her son on the phone. What he wants is: are you hurt now, is anyone with you, do you need
me to come. The floor, the shoes, the lighting — none of that is what he is asking. He gets a short
account that is entirely about the next few things somebody has to do.

She tells the doctor at her appointment. He wants a different account of the same fall: which side
she landed on, whether she put a hand out, whether she hit her head, whether she has felt dizzy
before. He is not unkind about her son's worry. It is simply not his question. His question is about
one system, and he wants that system's details in full.

She tells the housing officer for the block, who is doing the rounds. He asks whether the floor was
wet, whether the light on the landing is still out, and — this is the one nobody else asked — whether
this has happened before, to her or to anyone else on that floor.

Three accounts. All three are true. Each one is complete for the person hearing it. And **none of
them is a shortened version of another**: you cannot get the doctor's account by trimming the son's,
and the housing officer's question does not appear anywhere in either of the other two. If you tried
to write one account for all three, you would not get a better account. You would get one that is too
long for her son, too vague for the doctor, and silent on the only thing the housing officer needed.

## The idea in plain language

Part [4.1](4.1-what-a-standup-costs.md) priced the standup in the listener's attention. This part
asks the question that price was quietly assuming an answer to: **whose attention?**

Look again at what this day has been claiming. *Put what needs a person first.* It sounds like a rule
about tickets. It is not. Unpack it and there is a second noun hiding in it: needs *a person* — which
person? The rule is really *put first the things the listener has to do something about*, and that
sentence has a listener in it. Take the listener out and the rule stops being decidable. There is no
fact about ticket 4688 that tells you whether it belongs at the top of a report; there is only a fact
about 4688 **and** whoever is hearing the report.

A **listener** here means the specific person the standup is spoken to, with the job they actually
hold. Not "the team". A team does not have attention; a team is a room of people with different
things they can act on, and a report addressed to all of them is addressed to none of them in
particular.

So the honest position is this. A single standup for everybody is a **compromise**. Compromises are
often correct — one report is cheaper to build, cheaper to keep true, and gives a group a shared
thing to talk about. The mistake is not making the compromise. **The mistake is making it without
noticing**, and then arguing about ordering rules as though the right order were a property of the
data.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because the lab has a listener and never says so. Read `standup.py` and you will find a report whose
first sentence is *"Two tickets need you"*. That "you" is a specific person — somebody who can
approve a refund and can chase a customer reply — and nothing in the file names them. The whole day's
design decision rests on an assumption that was never written down.

And because the last `TODO(me)` in the hub's [build brief](../../LESSON.md) asks you to write it
down: *decide who this standup is for*. That is the only item in the brief that is not a function or
a test. It is there because every other item becomes answerable the moment it is done, and stays
arguable forever while it is not.

## The mechanism

There is no algorithm in this section. The mechanism is a way of thinking, applied three times to one
queue, and then one small consequence for the code.

The queue is the fixture in `lab/_state.py`, and it is the same one in all three cases: twelve open
tickets, four each in sign-in, billing and export; ticket **4688**, billing, open nine days, blocked
on an approval for a refund over the limit; ticket **4740**, sign-in, open eleven days, waiting on a
reply from the desk; and last night's job, which reindexed fine, passed **3 of 5** evals with `sso`
and `webhooks` failing, and then died at the digest stage.

### The person working the queue today

They are on the desk. They will spend today moving tickets. What they need from a spoken report is
two things: **what changed since yesterday**, and **what is blocked on them**.

4688 is theirs. It is the one item in the whole state that will not move until this specific person
does something — the refund is over the limit, so it needs an approval, and no amount of the queue
progressing changes that. 4740 is half theirs: they can chase the reply, though whether an eleven-day
wait is acceptable is somebody else's call, which is a point we come back to below.

For them, the category counts are **noise**. "The queue is 4 sign-in, 4 billing, 4 export, 12 open in
total" is a true sentence that changes nothing they will do today. They are not going to work the
queue differently because export happens to have four. They will open 4688 because it is blocked.
And last night's job is background: interesting if it broke the tool they are about to use, invisible
otherwise.

Notice what has just happened. The single most defensible sentence in the current standup — the
category shape, which is a real aggregate over real data — is the first thing to cut for the person
this standup is most obviously written for.

### The engineer who owns the nightly job

Now hear the same state as the person who owns the job Day 73 built.

For them the twelve tickets are the background and the last sentence is the report. The digest stage
**died**. Two eval cases — `sso` and `webhooks` — are failing. That is their morning. Whether billing
has four open tickets is a fact about somebody else's work.

And here the current standup fails them in a way worth being exact about. It says *"Last night the
evals were 3 of 5 passed and the digest stage died."* The two failing case names are **in the state
and not said at all**. `_state.LAST_NIGHT` carries `"failing": ("sso", "webhooks")`, and `_night()`
in `standup.py` reads `evals` and `digest` and never touches `failing`. Part
[3.2](../03-the-quiet-morning/3.2-saying-what-you-do-not-know.md) named that gap as a problem about
honesty — a report that omits what it could not read makes a bad night sound like a good one. This
part adds the other half of the answer: **the omission has an owner**. Those two names are useless to
the person on the desk and they are the entire report for the engineer. "3 of 5" is the summary you
give someone who is not going to fix it. `sso` and `webhooks` is the summary you give someone who is.

So the gap is not simply a missing sentence. It is a missing sentence *for a listener the standup was
not written for*, which is why it survived review: read it as the desk person, and nothing looks
absent.

### The person accountable for the desk

The third listener is neither of those. They will not approve the refund and they will not fix the
digest. They are answerable for whether the desk is working.

What they need is the **shape** and the **trend**. Is 4740 the only ticket waiting on a reply from
us, or is it the only one we noticed? Is eleven days normal for this desk, bad, or unremarkable? Is
the queue growing or shrinking? Did the digest die last night, or has it been dying?

**The lab's fixture cannot answer a single one of those, and saying so is the honest move.**
`_state.QUEUE` is one morning. There is no yesterday in it, no history of the nightly job, no notion
of a target, no record of what the queue looked like last week. Every one of this listener's
questions is a question about *change over time*, and the state this day is built on has exactly one
point in it. You could invent numbers to fill the gap. You would be making them up.

That is worth sitting with, because it is a real finding about the design and not a limitation of the
teaching fixture. A report built from current state can serve the first two listeners well and
**cannot serve the third at all**, no matter how the sentences are ordered. Serving them is not a
wording problem. It is a data problem, and it is one part [4.3](4.3-what-a-real-one-adds.md) picks up
under the name *freshness*.

### The three, side by side

| Listener | Would put first | Would cut entirely | What the current standup gets wrong for them |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| On the desk today | 4688 — blocked on their approval | the category counts | Little, and that is the finding: this is the listener it was written for, without anyone saying so |
| Owns the nightly job | the digest stage died; `sso` and `webhooks` failing | all twelve tickets | Never says the two failing case names, which are in the state and are their whole report |
| Accountable for the desk | is eleven days on 4740 normal, and is the queue growing | individual ticket refs | Answers none of their questions, and the fixture holds nothing that could |

The last column is the useful one. Read down it and the three failures are of three different kinds:
for the first listener there is almost nothing to fix; for the second there is a sentence that exists
in the data and was not said; for the third there is no data at all. Those need three different
responses, and only the middle one is the kind of bug you can fix by editing `standup.py`.

### What that means for the flag

Now the consequence for the code, which is this part's real contribution.

Every part of this day has treated the second element of `(text, attention)` as though it were
objective — a fact about the sentence, carried from a fact about the ticket, exactly as part
[2.2](../02-the-order-is-the-product/2.2-carry-the-flag.md) insisted. That insistence was right and
nothing here weakens it. Flags must come from data, never from searching prose.

But look at where the flag comes from. Here is `needs_a_person`, quoted exactly as it stands in
`lab/_state.py` — it already sits at column 0, so nothing was dedented and nothing trimmed:

```python
def needs_a_person() -> tuple[Ticket, ...]:
    """The tickets that will not move without somebody deciding something."""
    return tuple(t for t in QUEUE if t.needs_person)
```

**Line by line:**

- The docstring says **"somebody"**, and that word is the entire subject of this part. Somebody
  *who*? A ticket blocked on a refund approval will not move without a person who can approve
  refunds. It will move perfectly well without the engineer who owns the nightly job, who could stare
  at it all morning and change nothing. "Somebody" reads like precision and is an unresolved
  pronoun.
- `def needs_a_person() -> ...` — **the function takes no argument.** There is nowhere to put a
  listener even if you had one in your hand. That absence is not an oversight to be patched with a
  comment; it is a design decision the lab made and never stated, and it is the reason the ordering
  rule felt objective for three whole sections.
- `t.needs_person` is a boolean on the record, so the answer is fixed at the moment the ticket is
  written, by whoever wrote it, for whoever they had in mind. It is the packer's sticker from part
  2.2 — and a sticker that says *fragile* is objective, while a sticker that says *urgent* is
  addressed to someone.
- `tuple(... for t in QUEUE if ...)` — a filter with a fixed predicate. The smallest honest fix is to
  make the predicate depend on the listener: `def needs_a_person(listener: str)`. That is **a change
  to a signature, not to an algorithm**, which is the cheapest kind of change there is and the reason
  it is worth doing before the codebase has ten call sites.

Follow that up one level and it reaches the day's central function. `lines(queue, night)` from part
[2.1](../02-the-order-is-the-product/2.1-one-function-one-order.md) grows an argument:
`lines(queue, night, listener)`. Every sentence's flag is then computed for that listener, and the
same twelve tickets produce three different orderings from one function — which is what you want,
because the ordering rule is unchanged and only its input got honest.

There are exactly two defensible positions, and it is worth naming both rather than pretending the
first is obviously right:

1. **The flag is computed per listener.** `lines` takes a listener, `needs_a_person` takes a
   listener, and the standup is genuinely different for different people.
2. **The standup is honestly one person's report.** The signature stays as it is, the file says at
   the top who this report is for, and **the others are not given it** — because a report that says
   "two tickets need you" to somebody who cannot act on either has taught them to stop listening,
   which part 4.1 priced.

What is not defensible is the third position, which is where most systems sit: one report, given to
everybody, with nobody having written down whose it is.

## When it breaks

**Three standups for three people is more work and more surface.** Three orderings to keep true,
three sets of sentences to review, three places for a fact to go stale, and three chances for two of
them to disagree in front of the people who heard both. The honest position is that **most teams
should start with one report and know whose it is** — a single standup with a named listener at the
top of the file is a better system than three unnamed ones, and it is one line of work.

**Personalising too early breaks something less obvious: the shared account.** Part of what a
standup does is give a group one description of the morning to argue with. Split it three ways on day
one and there is no longer any such thing as "what the standup said". Somebody raises 4688 and
somebody else did not hear it mentioned, and now the meeting is spent reconstructing which report
each person got. A report nobody can discuss is a real cost, and it does not appear in any measure of
the report's own quality.

**And there is a specific trap worth naming, because it is the one this curriculum's tools make
easy.** Having decided the flag depends on the listener, the tempting move is to hand the queue and
a description of the listener to a model and ask it what matters to them. It will produce something
plausible. It will produce something slightly different tomorrow on the same input. And you will
have taken an untestable judgement and put it somewhere it cannot be reviewed: there is no field to
inspect, no assertion to write, and no answer to "why did 4740 come second today and fourth
yesterday" beyond "the model thought so".

That is part [2.2](../02-the-order-is-the-product/2.2-carry-the-flag.md)'s argument arriving from a
new direction. There the flag was recovered from prose and the fix was to carry it from data. Here
the flag would be *generated* rather than searched, which feels more sophisticated and has the same
defect: the report's structure is a guess, made downstream of the place that knew. If a listener
should see 4688 first, some data says so — their role, their queue, the approval limit they hold —
and that data can be read, tested and argued with. A model's opinion about it cannot.

**The weakness in this part's own argument**, stated plainly: nothing here is measured. Three
listeners were reasoned about, not surveyed, and their needs were inferred from a fixture written by
one person. It is entirely possible that a real desk manager wants the ticket refs after all. The
argument is that the *question* is real and usually unasked; the specific answers are worked
examples, and the exercise below asks you to replace them with a listener you actually know.

## In production

**What a professional writes down before building the second standup.** Three things, on one page,
per listener:

| What to write down | Why it is the useful thing to ask |
| --- | --- |
| Who they are, by role and by name | "The team" has no attention and cannot be checked against |
| What they would *do* about each item | An item they would do nothing about is an item to cut, however true |
| What they would not want to hear | The hardest of the three, and the one that shortens the report |

The middle row is the one that does the work. Go through the twelve tickets asking "what would this
person do about that", and the report writes its own ordering: everything they would act on, first;
everything they would act on later, next; everything they would never act on, gone. The third row is
harder than it looks because cutting a true sentence feels like hiding something, and it is the only
way a spoken report gets short enough to be heard.

**"Who is this for" is the question that makes every other decision on this day answerable.** Order,
length, what to include, whether omitting the failing eval names is a bug or a choice, whether
"nothing needs you" is worth saying — every one of those has a clear answer once the listener is
fixed and no answer at all while they are not. Which is why it is striking that it is almost never
asked explicitly. In practice it gets answered **implicitly, by whoever happens to be in the room
when the thing is built**. The engineer who writes the standup writes the standup they would want,
and it goes to production as *the* standup, and it is correct, and it is quietly one person's report
wearing a general name.

**What changes at scale.** One report for one team survives being unlabelled. A reporting system for
twenty teams does not: the same aggregate is rendered for people who can act on it and people who
cannot, and the ones who cannot learn to ignore it, and then the ones who can start ignoring it too
because ignoring it has become the norm. The failure mode of an unaddressed report is not that it is
wrong. It is that it gets tuned out, and tuned-out reports are still true, still generated, and still
costing quota every morning.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Who is this for? I can guess from the first
sentence — it says 'need you', so somebody who can approve a refund — but the file doesn't say and
the function doesn't take a listener. Put a line at the top naming the reader, and if the answer is
'everyone', say that too and let's talk about it, because right now we're never telling the person
who owns the nightly job which eval cases failed and that's the only thing in here they'd act on."*

**The interview question:** *"How would you decide what goes in an automated daily report?"* An
honest answer: *"I'd start by refusing to answer until I knew who was hearing it, because I don't
think importance is a property of the data. On the last one I built, the state was a support queue
plus last night's batch job, and it made three completely different correct reports. The person on
the desk needed the one ticket blocked on their approval and nothing else — the category totals were
true and useless to them. The engineer who owned the batch job needed the two eval cases that failed,
by name, and didn't care about any of the tickets — and our report had those names in the state and
never said them, which nobody had caught because everyone reviewing it was reading as the desk
person. And the manager needed trend — is eleven days on this ticket normal, is the queue growing —
which our data physically couldn't answer, because we only had today. So my answer is: name the
listener first, write down what they'd actually do about each item, cut anything they'd do nothing
about, and if you're going to ship one report for everybody, do it deliberately and write down whose
it really is. The version I'd push back on is asking a model to decide what's important per person,
because then the ordering isn't reviewable and it changes on you."*

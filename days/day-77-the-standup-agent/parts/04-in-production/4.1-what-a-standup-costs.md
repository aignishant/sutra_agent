# 4.1 — What a standup costs

## One-line answer

A standup is spent from **a person's attention** long before it is spent from anybody's quota, and
the two budgets behave nothing alike: quota is per morning, shared across the whole product and
written down; attention is per listener, renews slowly, is spent whether or not the report turned out
to be worth hearing — and nothing anywhere records it.

## The story

There is a meeting in your calendar that repeats. It was already there when you arrived. Nobody
currently in it can tell you who set it up, because that person has moved on.

Most weeks it is fine. Somebody says a thing, somebody else says another thing, and about one week in
ten something genuinely useful comes out of it — a clash spotted before it became a mess, a piece of
work two people were both about to start. On that week everybody remembers, and the meeting is
obviously worth having.

The other weeks you stop what you were doing to be there, half-listen with a laptop open, and then
have to find your way back into whatever you were in the middle of. None of that is a disaster. No
single instance of it is expensive enough to complain about.

Now ask who is going to cancel it. Cancelling means somebody has to decide it is not worth it, say so
out loud in front of everyone, and then be the person who was wrong if the very next week is one of
the useful ones. Attending requires no decision at all. You just go.

So it stays. Not because anyone defends it — nobody has ever been asked to — but because attending is
cheaper than deciding, every single week, for everyone. And there is no line anywhere in any system
that adds up what it has cost: the number of people, multiplied by the number of weeks, multiplied by
the getting-back-into-it. That number exists. It is simply not written down anywhere, so it is never
compared against the one useful week in ten.

A daily standup that a machine delivers is that meeting, with the cost of attending pushed down to
almost nothing and the cost of cancelling left exactly where it was.

## The idea in plain language

Everything this curriculum has budgeted so far has been budgeted in things the provider counts. Day
70 built a router around **RPM** and **RPD** — requests per minute and requests per day, the two
allowances a free tier is usually expressed in — because those are the numbers that run out and stop
your product working. Day 75 part 3.1 added a second unit for voice: an open line is priced by **how
long it was open**, not by what was said into it.

This part adds a third, and it is the one with no meter on it.

**Attention** is what a listener spends by listening. It is spent per person and per morning, it does
not come back when the report turns out to have been boring, and — this is the part that makes it
different from the other two — **it is spent before the listener can possibly know whether it was
worth spending.** You cannot decide whether to listen to a sentence until you have listened to it.
That is why part [1.3](../01-a-report-somebody-hears/1.3-what-the-listener-got.md) measured what it
measured: on an open line the listener said something after two sentences in both arms, so the first
sentence is the only part of a standup you can be sure of actually delivering. Everything after that
is at the listener's discretion, and they are exercising that discretion with information they got by
paying.

Put the three budgets in the same sentence and the ranking is uncomfortable. A standup that runs
every morning and is worth hearing one morning in ten has spent nine mornings of somebody's attention
to deliver one morning of value. The quota it spent over those nine mornings is negligible. Nobody
will ever see a bill for the attention.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because the desk is about to have a feature that runs on a schedule and talks to a person, and that
is a new category of thing in this project. Everything before it was pull: somebody asked, the desk
answered, and if nobody asked, nothing happened and nothing was spent. A standup is **push**. It
happens because the morning happened.

And because the honest accounting is not flattering to the day. The hub's §6 records this day's
provider cost as zero requests to every provider, and it names the gap in the same breath: this day
measures a standup in **sentences and words** rather than in tokens, because the wording here was
written by hand rather than generated by a model. That is a real limitation and section 4 is where it
gets said plainly rather than left in a footnote.

Part [4.2](4.2-who-it-is-for.md) argues that the same queue makes three different standups for three
different people. That argument only bites if attention is scarce — if it were free, you would send
all three reports to all three people and let them sort it out. This part is what makes 4.2 a design
problem instead of a preference.

## The mechanism

Three budgets, in order of how scarce they actually are.

### 1. Attention — the scarce one

Spent per listener, per morning, by the act of listening. There is no way to spend a little of it and
then decide: the listener's only instrument is stopping you, and stopping you costs them the sentence
they stopped you in.

This is why the ordering argument in section 2 is a cost argument and not a style argument. Part
[2.3](../02-the-order-is-the-product/2.3-words-before-it-matters.md) measured, on identical facts,
attention-first at 5 sentences and 53 words against queue order at 14 sentences and 103 words, with
**0 words against 25** before the first sentence that needed a person. Read those as prices. The
queue-order standup charges the listener 25 words before it gives them anything they could act on.
The attention-first one charges nothing. Both charge the full amount if the listener sits through the
whole thing, and neither refunds anything on a morning when there was nothing worth saying.

The morning when there was nothing worth saying is the expensive one, and part
[3.1](../03-the-quiet-morning/3.1-nothing-needs-you.md) is why it is also a morning you cannot simply
skip: "nothing needs you" and silence are different reports, and only one of them distinguishes a
calm queue from a job that never ran.

### 2. Quota — small, and say so

If the wording is generated rather than written, a daily standup costs **one request per morning**,
plus whatever the tools it calls cost. Part [1.2](../01-a-report-somebody-hears/1.2-the-three-tools.md)
showed three tool calls inside that one turn, and those tools read the desk's own state — they are
not provider requests.

That is genuinely small, and the honest thing is to say so rather than dress it up. The hub's §6
budget table records Groq at **30 RPM / 250 RPD, observed 2026-09-05**. One request every morning
against a per-day allowance of that order is not the thing that will exhaust a free tier; a single
afternoon of a developer re-running an eval loop will spend more of it than a month of standups.
Pretending otherwise to make the section feel weightier would be inventing a problem, which is
Principle 10 pointed at your own writing.

Two things do move it, and both are about size rather than count: how much of the queue you hand the
model, and whether the standup is generated fresh each morning or the same wording is reused. A
twelve-ticket queue is small. A queue of every open ticket in a support desk, pasted into a prompt
every morning, is not, and it grows without anybody deciding that it should.

### 3. Connection time — only if it is spoken

If the standup is delivered over an open line rather than written down, a third meter starts. Day 75
part 3.1 measured an open microphone at **64,000 bytes for two seconds of sound** against a **20-byte**
typed question — and was careful about which half of that was measured: the byte counts were counted
on the far end of a real connection, while turning bytes into seconds relied on a **stated assumption**
of 32,000 bytes per second of sound, written in the file as an assumption and not as a measurement of
any provider's wire format.

The consequence for a standup is direct. On an open line, a longer report costs more **in a way a
written one does not**. A written standup that grew from 5 sentences to 14 costs the reader some
scrolling and costs the storage nothing worth counting. A spoken one that grew the same way holds the
line open for longer, and the line is priced by duration whether the extra sentences mattered or not.
Length stops being purely a courtesy and becomes a line item.

### What the lab actually counts

One line of `lab/standup.py` produces the day's length number. It sits inside `main()` in the file,
so it is indented there; it is shown here dedented to column 0 and is otherwise character for
character what is on disk:

```python
total = sum(len(text.split()) for text, _ in lines)
```

**Line by line:**

- `text.split()` with no argument splits on any run of whitespace, so a hyphen standing on its own
  counts as a word. That is **crude, and adequate**, and saying which of those two it is matters more
  than the number does. This is a **proxy** for how long a listener is held, not a measurement of it —
  nothing in this lab has ever been said out loud, and no speaking rate has been measured here. The
  proxy is applied identically to both orderings, which is what makes the comparison in part
  [2.3](../02-the-order-is-the-product/2.3-words-before-it-matters.md) fair even though the absolute
  figure is soft.
- `for text, _ in lines` unpacks each `(text, attention)` pair and throws the flag away. The
  underscore is deliberate: **total** length is the one number on this whole day that does not care
  whether a sentence needed a person. Every other measurement here is about position and importance;
  this one is about bulk, and bulk is what a connection is billed for.
- `sum(...)` over a generator expression rather than a list — nothing is built up in memory, which is
  irrelevant at fourteen sentences and is simply the idiom.
- And the number this line does **not** produce is **tokens**. Tokens are the unit the rest of this
  curriculum budgets in, and they are not words: a token is a chunk of text a model's tokenizer
  produces, and one word can be several of them. The two are related by a ratio that depends on the
  model and the wording, and **nobody in this lab has measured it**. So `total` may be quoted as a
  word count and must not be quietly re-labelled as a token count.

Here are the three budgets side by side. The last column is the finding.

| Budget | Spent from | Per what | Does it renew? | Recorded anywhere today? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Attention | one listener's morning | per listener, per delivery | slowly, and only for that person | **no** |
| Quota | the product's shared free-tier allowance | per request, shared across every feature | yes — on the provider's RPM and RPD cycle | yes — the hub's §6 table |
| Connection time | seconds the line is held open | per second, per session | no — each session is spent afresh | yes, in principle — Day 75 part 3.1 counted the bytes |

**Read the last column downwards.** The two budgets with meters on them are the two that are small
and shared. The budget that is scarce, per-person and unrecoverable is the one with **no** in it. That
is not an oversight in this lab specifically; it is the normal state of every scheduled report in
every organisation, and it is exactly the situation the standing meeting in the story is in.

## When it breaks

**Words are a proxy for attention, and a poor one.** A dense sentence full of ticket references costs
a listener more than a longer, easier one — "4688, refund over the limit, needs an approval" asks more
of somebody than a sentence twice its length about the weather. The day's paper is about precisely
this: the limit is on units the listener has to hold, not on how much text went past them. Counting
words treats every word as equal, and they are not. Nothing on this page measured a listener. The only
listener-shaped measurement anywhere on this day is part 1.3's interruption after two sentences, and
that was a fixed number chosen by the lab, not a person's behaviour.

**The token cost is unmeasured here, and the reason is honest but it is still unmeasured.** The
sentences on this day were written, not generated, so there is no prompt, no completion and no
provider-reported token count to quote. A real standup hands the queue to a model, and its cost scales
with **how much of the queue you hand over** — the whole ticket list, or a summary, or just the two
that need somebody. That is a real number and this day does not have it. Do not let the day's zero
provider requests be read as "a standup is free"; it means "this day did not buy one".

**And the failure this whole framing predicts is optimising the cheap budget.** Shortening a prompt is
easy, measurable, satisfying and reviewable. Deciding that the report is not worth giving every
morning is a judgement call with somebody's name on it, cannot be demonstrated in a pull request, and
will look wrong on the first morning something is missed. So teams do the first one. They will trim
the prompt, cache the tool results, switch to a smaller model and put a graph of it on a dashboard —
and the report will still go out every morning to eleven people who have stopped listening to it, and
that will not appear on any graph at all. This is the standing meeting again, in a system: the cheap
option is the one that requires no decision, and it is the one that keeps costing.

## In production

**There are two honest answers to the attention budget, and they are not the same answer.**

The first is to make the report **conditional** — say it when there is something to say, and stay
quiet otherwise. This is the one that actually reduces what is spent, because it stops charging people
on the mornings with nothing in them. It also runs straight into part
[3.1](../03-the-quiet-morning/3.1-nothing-needs-you.md), which is the strongest argument against it in
this whole day: a report that goes quiet is indistinguishable from a report that failed to run, and
the value of "nothing needs you" is precisely that it tells you the queue was looked at. Suppress the
quiet mornings and you have saved attention by deleting the only evidence that the system is alive.
The workable middle is that the *quiet* report and the *loud* report are different objects with
different costs: a single short sentence confirming the queue was read is not the same charge as a
full standup, and it is the full standup that should be conditional.

The second is to make the report **shorter at the top** — keep it daily, keep it unconditional, and
put everything that needs a decision in the first sentence. This is what part
[2.3](../02-the-order-is-the-product/2.3-words-before-it-matters.md) measured, and its advantage is
that it survives contact with reality: it needs no judgement about whether today is interesting, it
cannot fail closed, and it degrades gracefully when the listener drifts. Its disadvantage is that it
does not actually reduce the spend. Everybody still stops what they were doing, every morning. It
lowers the price of the transaction rather than skipping it.

Which one is right depends on something neither of them can tell you: how bad a missed morning is. If
a missed escalation is expensive, the unconditional short report wins, because it never asks the
system to be right about whether today mattered. If nobody is on fire, the conditional report wins,
because nine mornings in ten are being charged for nothing. What is not defensible is choosing neither
and letting the report grow, which is the default.

**And the thing to say out loud in the design review: a daily report nobody can turn off is a
subscription somebody was signed up for without being asked.** It renews every morning, it charges
everyone on the list, and the cancellation path — if there is one — is usually harder than the
attending. Anything that pushes at somebody on a schedule should be able to be turned off by the
person it is pushed at, and somebody should be watching whether people are turning it off, because
that is the closest thing to a meter this budget will ever have.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Two things. First, your cost section only prices
the request, and the request is the cheapest thing here — one call a morning against our free tier is
noise, and I'd rather you said that plainly than padded it. The number I want is how many people this
goes to and how far in they stop listening; on a voice session, where they interrupted is recorded for
free, so log it. Second, before you tune anything, tell me what happens on a morning with nothing in
the queue. If the answer is that it still says all of it, we are charging eleven people for a report
that had nothing in it, and no dashboard we own will ever show that. Make the full report conditional
and keep one short line for the quiet mornings so we can still tell the difference between a calm
queue and a dead job."*

**The interview question:** *"What does a scheduled agent that talks to people cost?"* An honest
answer: *"Less than you'd think in quota and more than anybody measures in attention. On the standup I
built, the provider cost was one request a morning plus tool calls that never left our own system —
against a free tier of a couple of hundred requests a day, that's noise, and I'd say so rather than
inflate it. The cost that mattered was the listener's. I measured the same twelve tickets said two
ways: attention-first was five sentences with the thing that needed a decision at word zero, queue
order was fourteen sentences with it twenty-five words in, and on an open line the listener spoke
after two sentences in both. So the second version spent the same attention and delivered nothing. And
if it's spoken there's a third meter — an open line is priced by duration, so a longer report costs
more in a way a written one doesn't. The honest gaps are that I counted words rather than tokens,
because I wrote the sentences instead of generating them, and that words are a rough proxy for
attention anyway. The general point is that a daily report is a subscription: it renews every morning,
it charges everyone on the list whether or not it was worth it, and it's the only one of the three
budgets nothing records — which is why teams shorten the prompt instead of asking whether it should go
out every day."*

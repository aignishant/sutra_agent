# 2.2 — Carry the flag, do not search for it

## One-line answer

The first version of this lab worked out which sentences mattered by searching them for the phrase
`"needs you"` — and the good standup says *"Two tickets **need** you"*, so the search missed it and
the measurement reported **53 words before the first important sentence when the answer was 0**,
ranking the ablation as the better of the two.

## The story

A box goes on a van with a sticker on the side that says **fragile**.

Nobody has to look at the box and decide. The person who packed it knew what was in it, and the
sticker is that knowledge travelling with the item — so a handler at the depot who has never seen
inside it, and who has forty seconds and a trolley, does the right thing anyway.

The alternative is a handler who works it out. Light box, probably nothing. Taped a lot, probably
fine. That works most of the time, which is the problem, because the times it does not work are not
announced either. The box arrives, it looks like every other box, and the thing inside it is in
pieces.

The sticker is not a hint. It is the packer's judgement, made once, where the information was, and
carried to the place where the decision has to be taken by somebody who cannot see in.

## The idea in plain language

Part [2.1](2.1-one-function-one-order.md) established that the standup is a list of sentences in the
order they will be said. This part is about the second half of each sentence — the flag that says
*this is one somebody has to act on* — and about where that flag comes from.

There are only two answers.

**Carry it.** The ticket knows whether it needs a person; `_state.Ticket` has a `needs_person` field
put there by whoever knows. The sentence is built from that ticket, so the flag comes along with it,
and nothing downstream has to look at the words.

**Recover it.** Build the sentences, then read them back and work out which ones sound important.

The second is what this lab did first, and it is worth being precise about why it is tempting rather
than merely wrong. The sentences are right there. The important ones do say *needs you*. Writing
`if "needs you" in text` takes four seconds, requires no change to any data structure, and works —
against the first standup you test it on.

What it actually is, is a **guess at a decision that has already been made somewhere else**. And a
guess that reads English text is a guess with a very large surface: a plural, a synonym, a
translation, a rewording by somebody improving the tone. Every one of those is a normal, welcome
change to a report, and every one of them silently breaks a measurement that nobody thought was
downstream of the wording.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because the build brief asks for `(text, attention)` pairs and the gate checks their shape, and both
of those look like fussy over-specification until you have watched the alternative fail.

And because of what the failure *looked like*, which is the part worth carrying away. It did not
crash. It did not produce an obviously silly number. It produced a plausible number that ranked the
two orderings **the wrong way round** — and this day's entire argument is a comparison between those
two orderings.

## The mechanism

Here is the original, restored and re-run so this is a measurement rather than a memory:

```python
def before_attention(lines: list[Line]) -> tuple[int, int]:
    """(words, sentences) a listener hears before the first sentence that needs them."""
    words = 0
    for index, (text, _attention) in enumerate(lines):
        if "needs you" in text:
            return words, index
        words += len(text.split())
    return words, len(lines)
```

**Line by line:**

- `(text, _attention)` — the flag is unpacked and then deliberately ignored, and the underscore is the
  bug made visible. Everything needed to get this right is already in the tuple.
- `if "needs you" in text` is the guess. It is a substring test against prose, which means the
  measurement now depends on the exact wording of a sentence somebody wrote for a listener.
- `return words, index` on a match, and the running total otherwise: the arithmetic is correct. The
  function does exactly what it says. It is the *question* it asks that is wrong.
- The final `return words, len(lines)` is the fall-through for a report with nothing important in it —
  which is the correct answer for a quiet morning, part
  [3.1](../03-the-quiet-morning/3.1-nothing-needs-you.md)'s case, and is also what a report with a
  *missed* important line looks like. Those two cases became indistinguishable, and that is precisely
  how the bug hid.

Put that version back and run both orderings. Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
  sentences in the standup             5
  words in total                       53
  sentences before the first > line    5
  words before the first > line        53
```

```text
  sentences in the standup             14
  words in total                       103
  sentences before the first > line    5
  words before the first > line        25
```

The first block is **attention-first**, whose opening sentence is the one that needs a person. It
reports 53 words before the first important sentence — the entire standup — because the sentence says
*"Two tickets need you"* and the search was for *"needs you"*.

The second block is the ablation, which puts the important tickets at positions six and eleven. It
reports 25.

**So the measurement said the ablation was better.** Not by a little. It said the good ordering buries
the important thing twice as deep as the bad one, and every number on the screen was internally
consistent.

Now the version that ships:

```python
def before_attention(lines: list[Line]) -> tuple[int, int]:
    """(words, sentences) a listener hears before the first sentence that needs them."""
    words = 0
    for index, (text, attention) in enumerate(lines):
        if attention:
            return words, index
        words += len(text.split())
    return words, len(lines)
```

**Line by line:**

- One token changed in the unpacking and one in the test. No new data structure, no new argument,
  nothing anywhere else in the lab.
- `if attention` reads a boolean that was set by the code that knew. `_state.Ticket.needs_person` is
  the packer's sticker; this is the handler reading it.
- The function is now independent of the wording. Rewrite every sentence in the standup, translate it,
  make it politer, and this measurement is unaffected — which is the property you actually wanted and
  did not know you were choosing against.

And the comment that now sits above the type alias in `standup.py`, because the reason has to survive
the person who found it:

```python
# A sentence of the standup, and whether it is one of the ones somebody has to act on. The flag is
# carried rather than detected: a report that has to be searched for its own important lines is a
# report whose structure is a guess.
Line = tuple[str, bool]
```

**Line by line:**

- `tuple[str, bool]` is the whole fix, stated as a type. The pairing is what makes it impossible to
  build a sentence without answering the question.
- The comment explains **why** rather than what, which is the only kind of comment that survives —
  and it is here because a future reader looking at `tuple[str, bool]` would reasonably wonder whether
  the boolean is worth the trouble.

## When it breaks

**The correct version has a real weakness and it is worth naming plainly:** the flag is only as good
as `needs_person`, and nothing checks *that*. A ticket that is quietly blocked and not marked will be
said in the middle of the report and no measurement on this day will notice, because every number here
is computed from the flag. The guessing version had the same weakness plus another one; fixing the
second does not fix the first.

**And this failure has a general shape** worth recognising, because it is the third time this
curriculum has met it. Day 72 part 2.3 found `retry_after or default` collapsing "the server said
zero" into "the server said nothing". Day 73 part 3.2 found a digest reporting only successes because
nobody asked it for failures. All three are the same move: **a downstream piece of code re-deriving
something an upstream piece already knew**, and being wrong in a way that produces a plausible value
rather than an error.

**The specific trap here is that the wrong number was in the right range.** 53 is the length of the
standup. 25 is a plausible prefix. Neither is `-1`, `None`, or a crash. A validation that checked for
absurd values would have passed both. The only thing that would have caught it is knowing what the
answer should be for one case — and the attention-first case has an answer you can state without
running anything: **zero**, because the first sentence is the flagged one.

**That is the test the build brief asks for**, and it is the one that looks pointless: assert the flag
survives a rewording. Change *"need you"* to *"require your attention"* and the assertion must still
pass. Under the guessing version it would not have.

## In production

**Where this bites hardest is in things that are re-worded on purpose.** Report text is edited: for
tone, for length, for a customer, for a language. Every one of those edits is somebody's job and none
of them is expected to touch behaviour. A system that reads its own prose has made the copywriter into
a programmer without telling either of them.

**What a professional does when they find a string search in a data path.** Traces it upstream and
asks who already knew. There is almost always somebody: the record has a field, the API returned a
status, the event had a type. Recovering it from rendered text means the fact travelled through a
lossy channel — prose — and is being reconstructed on the far side.

**What changes at scale.** With one report and one phrase, the guess is a bug waiting. With a dozen
reports, three languages and a template system, it is a class of bug that produces wrong numbers on
dashboards nobody can debug, because the failure is in the gap between two things that both look
right.

**The one honest exception.** Sometimes the upstream genuinely does not know, and the text is all you
have — a supplier's email, a customer's message, a log line from a system you do not own. Then parsing
prose is the job rather than a shortcut, and the difference is that you know you are doing it: it gets
tests, a failure mode, and a plan for the day the wording changes. What makes this part's bug a bug is
that the information was **right there**, in the same tuple, and was unpacked and thrown away.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"This works out which lines matter by looking for a
phrase in them. Take the flag from the ticket instead — it's already on the record, and right now a
copy edit changes our metrics. Add the test that rewords the sentence and asserts the flag survives;
it'll look like a pointless test until the day somebody makes the report politer."*

**The interview question:** *"Tell me about a bug you shipped."* An honest answer: *"I was measuring
how far into a spoken report the first important sentence appeared, and I worked out which sentences
were important by searching them for a phrase — 'needs you'. The good version of the report said
'two tickets **need** you', plural verb, so the search never matched, and it reported the whole report
length as the answer. The number was plausible, nothing raised, and because the bad version happened
to use the singular it matched there — so the measurement ranked the deliberately bad ordering as
better than the good one, which was the exact comparison the whole thing existed to make. The fix was
to carry the flag from the data instead of recovering it from the text: the ticket already had a field
saying it needed a person. What I took from it is that when a downstream piece of code re-derives
something an upstream piece already knew, the failure isn't a crash — it's a plausible number, and the
only defence is knowing what the answer should be for at least one case."*

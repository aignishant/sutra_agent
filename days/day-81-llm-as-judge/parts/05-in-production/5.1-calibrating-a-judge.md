# 5.1 — Calibrating a judge against people

## One-line answer

A judge is a measuring instrument, and the only calibration available is a set of answers people have
labelled — which costs an afternoon, has to be re-taken whenever the judge model changes, and is the
item Days 79, 80 and 81 have each parked.

## The story

The moderation meeting, the week after the exams.

Six people have marked the same paper. Before any of them touch the real scripts, they all mark the
same five, sit in a room, and compare.

It is a tedious morning and it is the only part of the process that makes the rest of it mean
anything. One marker turns out to be giving a mark for working that the others give for the answer.
Two of them disagree about a question where the wording was ambiguous, and after twenty minutes they
agree on a rule. One person is a full grade harsher than everybody else, across the board, and now
knows it.

Nobody's marking was wrong before the meeting. What was missing was any way to know how each person's
marking related to anybody else's — and without that, six markers produce six scales with the same
numbers written on them.

They do it again next year with the new markers. It does not carry over.

## The idea in plain language

Everything in this day has compared a judge against **human labels** — the `human` field on every
answer in `_cases.py`. Part [3.1](../03-honest-baselines/3.1-a-number-needs-a-baseline.md)'s baselines,
part [1.2](../01-the-judge-is-a-system/1.2-the-judge-disagreed-with-itself.md)'s 20-of-24, part
[2.3](../02-the-judge-has-biases/2.3-self-enhancement.md)'s two house styles: none of those
measurements exists without somebody having read the answers and written down a verdict.

That set is the **calibration set**, and building one is the only way to answer the question that
decides whether any judged number is worth having: *how well does this judge agree with us?*

Four things about it that are easy to get wrong.

**It has to be labelled before the judge sees it.** Labels written after looking at the judge's output
are not labels, they are agreement. The order matters and it is not enforceable by anything except
discipline.

**It has to be balanced, not representative.** This is the counter-intuitive one, and Day 80's paper
part is why: on a set that is 83% valid, chance agreement is high and every coefficient is
uninterpretable. A calibration set wants roughly equal numbers of good and bad answers — which means
deliberately over-sampling the failures, which means it is **not** a sample of your traffic and must
never be used to estimate a rate.

**It has a ceiling, and the ceiling is not 100%.** Two people labelling the same answers disagree.
Day 80's paper measured what that looks like and the paper behind today reported LLM judges reaching
*"over 80% agreement, the same level of agreement between humans"* — which is the sentence to
remember, because it makes 80% a **good** result rather than a mediocre one. A judge measured against
an assumed ceiling of 100% will always look worse than it is.

**It expires.** The judge model changes and the calibration is void. Not degraded — void, because the
instrument is a different instrument. That is the property that makes this expensive: it is not a
one-off cost, it is a cost per judge version, forever.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because this day's numbers all rest on twenty-four labels in a fixture file, and that fixture is
twenty-four *invented* answers labelled by the person who invented them. It is enough to demonstrate
every mechanism in this day and it is not a calibration set for the real desk.

And because the item has now been parked three times. Day 79 part 5.3 item 9 parked human labelling.
Day 80 part 5.2 item 8 parked a second judge and a human panel. This part is the third, and the
pattern is worth naming rather than repeating: it keeps being parked because it costs somebody's
attention rather than somebody's money, and attention has no line in a budget.

## The mechanism

The measurement, once the labels exist, is three numbers over two label lists. From `lab/baseline.py`:

```python
def agreement(a: list[str], b: list[str]) -> float:
    return sum(1 for x, y in zip(a, b, strict=True) if x == y) / len(a)


def chance(a: list[str], b: list[str]) -> float:
    n = len(a)
    ca, cb = Counter(a), Counter(b)
    return sum((ca[label] / n) * (cb[label] / n) for label in set(a) | set(b))


def kappa(a: list[str], b: list[str]) -> float:
    """Day 80's correction: the agreement chance does not explain."""
    p_o, p_e = agreement(a, b), chance(a, b)
    return 0.0 if p_e == 1.0 else (p_o - p_e) / (1 - p_e)
```

**Line by line:**

- These are the same three functions as
  [Day 80's paper demo](../../../day-80-rubrics-and-trajectories/papers/01-agreement-above-chance.md),
  reimplemented here rather than imported across day folders. The duplication is deliberate: a lab is
  a self-contained thing, and a cross-day import would break the moment either folder is renamed.
- `zip(a, b, strict=True)` raises on a length mismatch. Two label lists that do not correspond
  item-for-item are not comparable, and a silent truncation produces a plausible number from a bug.
- `kappa` takes **two raters** and is symmetric, so `kappa(HUMAN, judge)` and `kappa(judge, HUMAN)` are
  the same number. That matters conceptually: it measures agreement between two raters, not the
  accuracy of one against a truth. Calling the human labels "truth" is a convention, and a useful one,
  and it is a convention.
- The chance correction uses each rater's own marginals, so a judge that says valid 90% of the time is
  compared against a baseline built from *its* habits and the human's — not from an assumed prior.

And the three numbers the calibration produces, from the run:

```text
                             agreement   kappa  over baseline
  the judge (reads it)           95.8%    0.83         +12.5%
```

**Line by line:**

- `agreement` is what people ask for and what part
  [3.3](../03-honest-baselines/3.3-the-eighty-seven-that-was-eighty-three.md) showed is not reportable
  alone.
- `kappa` at 0.83 is agreement chance does not explain. Compare with the coin judge's 0.00 in the same
  table — same column, and the two numbers are 83 points apart where the agreement figures are 12.5.
- `over baseline` is the margin against the majority class, which is the number that survives the
  class balance moving.

Run the calibration this day has:

```bash

```

**Line by line:**

- The `HUMAN` list in `baseline.py` is the calibration set: twenty-four answers, labelled in
  `_cases.py` before any judge existed.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
24 answers, human labels: {'valid': 20, 'invalid': 4}
majority class 'valid' is 83.3% of the suite

                             agreement   kappa  over baseline
  the judge (3-2 coin)           83.3%    0.00          +0.0%
  the judge (reads it)           95.8%    0.83         +12.5%
```

**And now the honest criticism of this very set.** It is 20 valid to 4 invalid — 83% one class — which
is exactly the skew that makes kappa hard to interpret and that
[Day 80 part 4.2](../../../day-80-rubrics-and-trajectories/parts/04-when-raters-disagree/4.2-agreement-is-not-agreement.md)
measured producing a coefficient of −0.05 at 90% raw agreement.

A proper calibration set for this desk would be roughly twelve valid and twelve invalid: **deliberately
half wrong answers**, which means going and finding twelve genuinely wrong answers, which is the work.
The set in this lab is shaped for demonstrating the mechanisms of section 1 and section 3, and it says
so here rather than being quietly presented as fit for a purpose it is not.

## When it breaks

Calibration breaks by **expiring silently**, and there is no error at the moment it happens.

The sequence:

```text
1. fifty answers are labelled; the judge agrees 92% of the time; kappa 0.78
2. that number goes into a document and a dashboard
3. four months later the judge model is upgraded - or its provider updates it
4. the score on the nightly suite moves by three points
5. somebody investigates the desk
```

Step 3 is the one nobody records, and it may not even be your action — a hosted model can change
underneath a stable model string. Step 5 is where the week goes.

The only thing that makes step 3 visible is recording the judge's model and version **on every
score**, which is what part [1.1](../01-the-judge-is-a-system/1.1-a-judge-is-a-model-with-a-job.md)'s
review comment asked for and what part
[2.3](../02-the-judge-has-biases/2.3-self-enhancement.md) asked for again. Three parts of this day
converge on the same one-line fix, which is usually a sign that it is the cheapest thing on the list.

There is a second, slower break: **the labels drift from what the team means.** The person who
labelled the set in March had one idea of what counts as a valid answer; the team's standard has moved
since, nobody re-labelled, and the judge is being calibrated against an old opinion. The mitigation is
to re-label a handful of the set each quarter and check that the original labeller would still agree —
which is measuring the humans, and which is exactly what the moderation meeting is for.

## In production

**What a professional builds.** A small, balanced, versioned calibration set — fifty to a hundred
items is usually plenty — labelled by two people independently, with the human-human agreement
computed and recorded as the ceiling. That last number is the one that makes every judged result
interpretable afterwards, and it costs one extra person's afternoon on the day the set is built and
nothing ever again.

**What degrades at scale.** The set stops representing the system. It was built when the desk answered
three kinds of question and the desk now answers nine, so the calibration covers a third of what is
being judged. Re-balancing means labelling more, which means the afternoon again — and the practical
compromise most teams reach is a small permanent core plus a rolling sample, re-labelled on a cadence
somebody owns.

**The failure that only shows with real traffic.** Labels are cheap to gather badly. The fastest way to
get a hundred labels is to have one person do it in an hour, which produces a hundred labels with one
person's blind spots and no way to detect them — no second labeller, so no agreement figure, so no
ceiling, so a judge measured at 92% against a standard nobody has checked. Two labellers on a subset
is the minimum that produces a number about the labels themselves.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Before we quote a judge agreement figure anywhere,
two things. Get a second person to label thirty of them so we have a ceiling — 92% against a human
ceiling of 94% is a different story from 92% against an assumed 100%. And record the judge model
string on every score; when this number moves I want to be able to rule out the rater in five minutes
instead of five days."*

**The interview question:** *"How do you know your LLM judge is any good?"* The answer that shows
experience describes a held-out, human-labelled, deliberately balanced set, and reports agreement
against **both** a baseline and the human-human ceiling. The tell is whether they mention that the
calibration is void when the judge model changes, because that is the part that turns a one-off task
into an ongoing cost and it is the part people discover late.

# 5.2 — What a real judged suite adds

## One-line answer

The lab runs ADK's real judged metric at zero cost and has been shown to fail four separate ways, and
the distance between that and a judged suite a team would act on is nine things — three of which cost
nothing at all, and one of which has now been parked on four consecutive days.

## The story

The examination board's procedures document, and the appeals that wrote it.

Nobody sat down and designed it. It grew. There is a clause about candidates who arrive late because
of a year when a train was cancelled. There is a rule about which colour pen, because of a year when a
script was photographed and the pencil did not show. There is an entire section on scripts that go
missing between the centre and the marker, which reads like paranoia until you learn that it happened.

Every year the board hears appeals, and most of them fail, and one or two reveal something the
procedures could not have anticipated. Those become clauses.

A new examiner reads the document and finds it absurdly long for the job of marking papers. Ten years
later they are the one arguing to add a clause, because something happened that nobody had thought
of, and they do not want it to happen twice.

## The idea in plain language

The lab does four things properly. It runs ADK's real `final_response_match_v2` — its prompt template,
its label parser, its majority vote, its threshold — at zero requests, through the `LLMRegistry` seam.
It prices itself honestly against a measured allowance. It compares against baselines rather than
reporting a bare accuracy. And it has been **shown to fail**: a coin judge at 83.3%, four wrong answers
passing on padding, two identical facts graded oppositely on style, and twelve pairwise verdicts that
were entirely the ordering.

Here is the rest of the procedures document.

**1. The judge model and version on every score.**
Three parts of this day asked for it independently —
[1.1](../01-the-judge-is-a-system/1.1-a-judge-is-a-model-with-a-job.md),
[2.3](../02-the-judge-has-biases/2.3-self-enhancement.md),
[5.1](5.1-calibrating-a-judge.md) — which is usually the sign that something is both cheap and
load-bearing. A judged score is only comparable with another judged score if both were produced by the
same rater, and a hosted model can change under a stable model string. One string per row.

**2. The baseline in the same table as the score.**
Part [3.3](../03-honest-baselines/3.3-the-eighty-seven-that-was-eighty-three.md) measured the removal:
the same run reports *"both judges agree with us most of the time; ship it"*. `baseline.py` already
exits 1 when a candidate fails to beat its baseline; putting that in CI is one line.

**3. The vote margin per case.**
Part [1.2](../01-the-judge-is-a-system/1.2-the-judge-disagreed-with-itself.md) measured a 3–2 split on
every case producing clean 1.0 and 0.0 scores with no trace. The samples are already paid for. One
flag per case — *this was not unanimous* — is enough, and it is what separates "the desk changed" from
"the rater wobbled" on the morning Day 82's comparison goes red.

**4. The swap control on every pairwise comparison, and both agreement figures.**
Part [2.1](../02-the-judge-has-biases/2.1-position.md) measured 12 of 12 verdicts becoming 0. And the
paper's demo shows the reporting rule that goes with it: **100% of the pairs with a verdict** and
**68.8% of all pairs** are both true, and quoting only the first is the standard way a pairwise result
is overstated.

**5. Answer length recorded next to every judged score.**
Part [2.2](../02-the-judge-has-biases/2.2-verbosity.md) turned four wrong answers into passes with one
polite sentence. You cannot neutralise verbosity bias, and you can notice when the mean answer length
and the judged score are climbing together. One integer per case.

**6. Deterministic checks for deterministic claims.**
Part [4.2](../04-what-it-buys/4.2-when-a-judge-is-the-wrong-tool.md): a large fraction of this day's
twenty-four cases assert that a number appears in a sentence, at ten requests each. Moving those to
fact checks makes them free, exact, unbiasable, and runnable on every commit instead of nightly.

**7. A balanced, human-labelled calibration set, with a second labeller.**
Part [5.1](5.1-calibrating-a-judge.md). Fifty to a hundred items, roughly half of them wrong answers,
labelled before the judge sees them, with human-human agreement recorded as the ceiling. This is the
one that has been parked on days 79, 80 and 81, and it is parked because it costs attention rather
than money.

**8. 🅿️ Parked — a second judge from a different family.**
The only cheap control for self-enhancement, and there is not one — part
[2.3](../02-the-judge-has-biases/2.3-self-enhancement.md). It needs a second provider, a second key
and a second allowance, and Day 78's criterion 3 could not determine the allowance on the first one.

**9. 🅿️ Parked — the judge's rationale, kept.**
`_parse_critique` reads a `"rationale"` field out of the judge's answer and the score keeps none of
it. Storing the rationale for the cases that failed turns an eval result into something a person can
argue with rather than only act on. It is parked because it is a storage and privacy decision — the
rationales quote customer text — and Day 69's boundaries apply.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because Day 82 turns this into a nightly comparison and Day 83's gate reads the result. Items 1, 3 and
5 are precisely the three that let somebody answer *"why did this number move?"* on a morning when it
did, and none of them costs a request.

And because item 7 is now blocking in two directions. It is the calibration every number in this day
rests on, and it is the prerequisite for item 8, which is the only control for the one bias Sutra is
most exposed to.

## The mechanism

The list, with what each costs, so it can be argued with rather than admired:

| # | Item | Where it lands | Roughly |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | judge model on every score | the results writer | fifteen minutes |
| 2 | baseline in the same table | `sutra/judge.py` + one line in CI | half an hour |
| 3 | vote margin per case | a subclass or a wrapper | half a day |
| 4 | swap control + both agreement figures | the pairwise harness | half a day, and 2x the requests |
| 5 | answer length per score | the results writer | fifteen minutes |
| 6 | deterministic claims moved out | `tests/`, one per claim | a day, and it *saves* requests |
| 7 | balanced human-labelled set | somebody's afternoon, twice | a day, repeatedly |
| 8 | 🅿️ second judge, different family | not built | a second measured allowance |
| 9 | 🅿️ rationales kept | not built | a storage and privacy decision |

**Items 1, 2 and 5 together are about an hour**, and they are the three that make every other number
on the page interpretable. Item 6 is the only entry that reduces the bill. Item 7 is the only entry
that decides whether the judge is any good at all, and it is the one with no technical content
whatsoever.

The three no-cost items, as one row in a results file:

```python
row = {
    "case_id": answer.case_id,
    "score": result.overall_score,
    "judge_model": criterion.judge_model_options.judge_model,
    "unanimous": margin in (0, num_samples),
    "answer_chars": len(answer.said),
}
```

**Line by line:**

- `judge_model` is read back off the criterion rather than from a constant, so the row records what
  the run actually used rather than what somebody believed it used.
- `unanimous` is a **boolean, not the margin**. Part
  [1.2](../01-the-judge-is-a-system/1.2-the-judge-disagreed-with-itself.md) wanted one bit; a full
  margin per case is a column nobody reads and a boolean is one somebody sorts by.
- `margin in (0, num_samples)` — unanimous means *all* samples agreed, in either direction. Writing it
  as `margin == num_samples` would call a unanimous "invalid" non-unanimous.
- `answer_chars` rather than a token count, because it needs no tokenizer and moves with the thing
  being watched. It is a tripwire, not an accounting figure.
- Nothing here costs a request. All four values exist at the moment the score does.

## When it breaks

The list breaks the way every such list breaks — it reads as improvements, so none of it is done — and
the composite failure has been measured piece by piece in this day:

```text
1. deterministic claims are judged, so the suite is expensive          (item 6, part 4.2)
2. the cost forces num_samples down, so verdicts are single draws      (item 3, part 1.2)
3. the score is reported without a baseline                            (item 2, part 3.3)
4. it reads 83.3%, which is the base rate                              (part 1.2)
5. the judge model is upgraded and nothing records it                  (item 1, part 5.1)
6. the number moves; a week goes on investigating the desk             (item 7 would have said)
```

Six steps, no error at any of them, and the number at step 4 is one somebody will describe as good.

## In production

**What a professional does instead of building all nine.** Items 1, 2 and 5 this week — an hour, and
they are what make the next argument possible. Then item 7, because without a calibration set every
other number is uncalibrated, and the afternoon it costs is the cheapest afternoon on this list.
Item 6 pays for itself.

**What degrades at scale.** Judged suites get slower and more expensive faster than any other kind, and
the pressure lands on the controls rather than on the case count — because cutting `num_samples` is a
config change and cutting cases is a conversation. Every item on this list except 6 makes the suite
more expensive, and the only durable defence is that the *interpretability* of the number is what is
being bought.

**The failure that only shows with real traffic.** Every measurement in this day used a scripted
judge. A real one is inconsistent in ways no script reproduces, is influenced by things nobody
anticipated, and changes without notice. The lab proves the machinery around the judge; it proves
nothing about any judge. That is the honest boundary of this day and item 7 is the only thing that
crosses it.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Do 1, 2 and 5 before you touch anything else —
about an hour, and they are what let us answer 'why did this move' without guessing. Then get fifty
answers labelled by two people. I know it has been parked three times; it is the difference between
having a judge and having a number, and everything on this list above it is scaffolding around a
measurement we have never checked."*

**The interview question:** *"What would you add to an LLM-as-judge setup before trusting its
numbers?"* The answer that shows experience starts with the baseline and the calibration set — the two
things that say whether the number means anything — rather than with prompt engineering or a better
model, because a better judge measured the same careless way produces a better-looking number and the
same amount of knowledge.

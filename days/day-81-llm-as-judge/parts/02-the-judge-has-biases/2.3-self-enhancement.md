# 2.3 — Self-enhancement

## One-line answer

💥 Two answers that both say *eleven days* and that a person marks equally correct — one in the
judge's own house phrasing, one in a terse style — come back **valid** and **invalid**, and nothing in
the result says the difference was style.

## The story

The contractor sends somebody to inspect the work, and the somebody is from the contractor's own firm.

He is not dishonest. He genuinely knows what he is looking at, arguably better than an outsider would.
He walks the site, checks the joints, looks at the finish, and signs it off.

What he is also doing, without deciding to, is comparing everything against how *his* firm does it.
The joint is finished the way he was trained to finish joints, so it looks right. The other firm's way
— equally sound, different — would have looked odd to him, and he would have written a note, and the
note would have been sincere.

The problem is not that he passed the work. The work may well be fine. The problem is that his
approval carries less information than it appears to, because part of what he approved was the
familiarity, and there is no line on the form for that.

## The idea in plain language

**Self-enhancement bias** is a judge's tendency to prefer output that resembles its own. The paper
behind this day names it alongside position and verbosity, and it is the one with the least obvious
mechanism and the most awkward consequences.

Two ways it shows up, and they are worth keeping apart:

- **The literal case.** A model is asked to judge answers, one of which it produced. It rates its own
  higher.
- **The family case, which is the one that will happen to you.** The judge and the agent are the same
  model, or the same family, or trained on overlapping data — so the agent's phrasing, structure and
  habits are the ones the judge finds natural, without either of them having any awareness of the
  other.

The second is the practical problem because it is the **default configuration**. The obvious judge for
a Gemini-backed desk is a Gemini model, for reasons that have nothing to do with bias: it is the
provider you have a key for, the model whose free tier you understand, and the one Addendum 02 already
tells you to use for eval volume. Every incentive points at judging your own family.

And unlike position — which a swap detects — and verbosity — which a length column exposes — self
enhancement has **no cheap control**. You cannot swap style. Detecting it needs a second judge from a
different family, or human labels, and both are things Sutra does not currently have. Part
[5.1](../05-in-production/5.1-calibrating-a-judge.md) is about getting them.

The consequence is specific and it compounds: a judged score that quietly rewards house style will,
under any optimisation pressure, push the agent **towards** the judge's family. The system converges
on sounding like its own marker, and the metric improves the whole way.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because Sutra's desk runs on Gemini — Addendum 02 makes that the primary lane — and the judge
Addendum 02 recommends for eval volume is `gemini-2.5-flash-lite`. Same provider, same family. That
is the right call for cost and it means the one bias with no cheap control is the one Sutra is most
exposed to.

It also affects something Day 79 already put in place. The reference answers in the fixture were
written by a person, in that person's style, and part
[1.3](../01-the-judge-is-a-system/1.3-what-the-judge-is-shown.md) showed the judge is asked whether
the agent's answer is equivalent to that reference. So there are two styles in play — the judge's and
the reference author's — and an answer that matches neither is being marked by a rater with two
reasons to find it unfamiliar.

## The paper behind it

> **Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena**
> `arXiv:2306.05685` · 2023 · <https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685>

It named **self-enhancement** among the biases of LLM judges, alongside position and verbosity, while
reporting that such judges reach over 80% agreement with human preferences.

Taught in this day's paper part:
[Judging the judge — agreement, and the three biases](../../papers/01-judging-the-judge.md).

## The mechanism

Two answers, same fact, different house. From `lab/bias.py`:

```python
# The same twelve facts in two house styles. A person marks every pair equally correct.
HOUSE_A = "Ticket 4740 has been open for eleven days."
HOUSE_B = "4740: open 11d."
```

**Line by line:**

- Both say eleven days about ticket 4740. There is no fact in one that is not in the other, which is
  what makes this an ablation of **style** rather than of content.
- `HOUSE_A` is a full sentence with a subject and a verb — the shape a chatty assistant produces, and
  the shape the reference answers in `_cases.py` are written in.
- `HOUSE_B` is the shape an operations tool produces: an identifier, a colon, an abbreviation. It is
  denser, it is correct, and it is what a person on a support desk would actually type to a colleague.
- Two constants rather than a generated pair, so the experiment is one comparison a reader can hold in
  their head.

And the judge:

```python
def by_house_style(prompt: str, _call: int) -> str:
    """A judge that calls an answer valid when it is written the way the judge writes."""
    return "valid" if "has been open for" in prompt else "invalid"
```

**Line by line:**

- The test is a **phrasing** substring, `"has been open for"`, not a fact. This rater is not checking
  whether the answer is right; it is checking whether the answer sounds like it.
- It ignores the reference and the question, exactly as part
  [2.2](2.2-verbosity.md)'s length judge does. Both are caricatures of one effect, isolated so that
  the effect and nothing else drives the output.
- Searching the whole `prompt` rather than the extracted answer is safe here **only because** the
  reference is `"Eleven days."`, which does not contain the phrase. Part
  [2.2](2.2-verbosity.md)'s `response_in` exists because that shortcut does not generalise, and this
  arm is a case where it happens to hold.
- `_call` is unused: this rater is perfectly consistent. Inconsistency was part
  [1.2](../01-the-judge-is-a-system/1.2-the-judge-disagreed-with-itself.md)'s subject and mixing the
  two would make neither legible.

Run it:

```bash
cd days/day-81-llm-as-judge/lab
uv run python bias.py --self; echo "exit: $?"
```

**Line by line:**

- `--self` grades the two answers against the same reference, `"Eleven days."`, with ADK's real
  evaluator and one sample each.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
metric: final_response_match_v2 (real), num_samples 1
judge: says valid when the answer uses its own house phrasing

  house style     valid  the answer
  A (judge's)       yes  'Ticket 4740 has been open for eleven days.'
  B                  no  '4740: open 11d.'

  both answers say eleven days; a person marks both correct
  the judge marked one of them wrong for its phrasing
exit: 1
```

**Same fact, opposite verdicts.** A person reading those two lines marks both correct without
hesitating — one of them is how you would say it to a customer and the other is how you would say it
to a colleague, and eleven days is eleven days.

The metric reports `overall_score` of 1.0 and 0.0. There is no field in the result for *why*, and
nothing about the second verdict looks different from a genuine failure. If `HOUSE_B` were the desk's
real style, this metric would report it as broken, indefinitely, and every investigation would look at
the desk.

## When it breaks

The break is slow and it runs in one direction, and there is no error at any point.

```text
1. the judge and the desk are the same model family (the default)
2. the judged score rewards answers that sound like the judge
3. somebody tunes the desk's prompt against the score
4. the desk's style converges towards the judge's
5. the score improves, and so does every future score
```

Nothing in that sequence is a mistake. Step 1 is a cost decision Addendum 02 recommends. Step 3 is the
job. And the end state is a metric that has become a measure of stylistic similarity between two
models from the same family, reported as correctness.

The tell, and there is only one available without a second rater: **the score improves without any
change that should have improved it.** A prompt edit about tone should not move a correctness metric.
When it does, that is the signal.

The two real defences are both expensive, and it is worth being straight about that rather than
offering a cheap one that does not work:

- **A second judge from a different family.** Grade a sample with both and look at the cases they
  disagree on. Costs a second provider, a second key, a second allowance — and Day 78's criterion 3
  could not determine the allowance on the *first* one.
- **Human labels on a small sample.** The only ground truth there is. Costs somebody's afternoon,
  repeatedly, which is part [5.1](../05-in-production/5.1-calibrating-a-judge.md)'s subject and is the
  item Day 79 and Day 80 both parked.

What does **not** work is instructing the judge to ignore style. It is the same category of fix as
telling somebody not to be influenced by an accent, and it fails for the same reason: the influence
is not available to the instruction.

## In production

**What a professional does.** Uses a judge from a different family from the agent where the budget
allows, and records the judge's model and version on every score so that the pairing is visible. If
the judge must be from the same family — which it usually must — then the mitigation is not technical:
it is to treat the judged score as **one** signal among several, and never as the thing to optimise.

**What degrades at scale.** Optimisation pressure. A judged metric that nobody optimises against is
merely biased; one that a team tunes towards is a metric being actively climbed, and self-enhancement
is the direction with the smoothest gradient. Any process that automatically selects prompts or model
versions on a judged score will find this, faster than a person would.

**The failure that only shows with real traffic.** Style is correlated with the customer. A desk
serving engineers writes tersely and a desk serving the public writes in sentences, so a
self-enhancing judge scores one product surface higher than the other for no reason connected to
quality — and the team concludes that one surface is working better and moves effort towards it.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"We are judging Gemini with Gemini, which is the
right cost call and means we cannot detect the one bias that has no cheap control. Two things: record
the judge model on every score so that a future upgrade is visible in the data, and do not let anybody
tune the desk's prompt against this number. It is a signal, not an objective."*

**The interview question:** *"Would you use the same model to generate and to evaluate?"* The answer
that shows experience says it is usually what you end up doing and names the specific cost — the judge
prefers its own family's phrasing, and unlike position or verbosity there is no cheap control — and
then says what they would do instead of pretending to fix it: record the pairing, sample against a
different family or against people, and refuse to optimise against the score.

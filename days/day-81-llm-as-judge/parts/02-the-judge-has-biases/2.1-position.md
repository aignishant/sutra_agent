# 2.1 — Position

## One-line answer

A judge asked to choose between two answers can be choosing between two **positions** — measured
here as the first answer winning **12 of 12** pairs — and the only way to find out is to ask again
with the two swapped, which leaves **0 of 12** with a verdict.

## The story

A blind tasting at a tea stall. Two cups, set down left and right, and you are asked which is better.

You taste the left one. Then the right one. You say the left.

You are not being careless. You genuinely preferred it. But think about what actually happened: the
first sip landed on a palate that had nothing to compare it to, and by the second sip you had a
standard — the left one — and you were asking *is this better than that*, which is a different
question from the one you would have asked if they had come the other way round.

If somebody had swapped the cups before you arrived, there is a very good chance you would have said
the left one again.

The tasting was real. Your preference was real. What was also real, and invisible from inside, is
that part of the answer was the order.

## The idea in plain language

A **pairwise** judge is shown two answers and asked which is better. It is the most useful form of
LLM-as-judge for questions with no reference answer — *which of these two replies is more helpful* —
and it is the form the paper behind this day studied.

**Position bias** is the tendency to prefer an answer because of where it appeared, not what it said.
It is not a small effect and it is not a defect of any one model; it is a property of the setup, and
it shows up as a preference that is confident, well-argued and reversed by swapping the inputs.

The control is one sentence: **ask twice, once each way round, and keep only the pairs where both
orderings agree.** Three things fall out of it, and the third is the one people resist:

- a pair where the two orderings agree has a verdict you can use;
- a pair where they disagree has **no verdict** — not a tie, not half a point, nothing;
- and the number of pairs you can say anything about is now smaller than the number you judged.

That last point is why the control gets skipped. It makes your dataset shrink, it makes your headline
agreement figure harder to compute, and it costs exactly twice as many requests. Part
[4.1](../04-what-it-buys/4.1-priced-against-the-allowance.md) prices the doubling.

One clarification before the measurement, because it matters for what this lab can honestly claim.
ADK's `final_response_match_v2` is **not** pairwise — it grades one answer against one reference, so
it has no ordering to be biased by. Position bias belongs to comparison, so the harness in this part
is Sutra's own thirty lines rather than the framework's, and the lab's output says so on its first
line.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because the moment there is no reference answer, pairwise is the shape you reach for — and Sutra will
reach for it. *"Is this morning's standup better than yesterday's wording?"* has no golden answer, and
Day 77 spent a whole day arguing about orderings that a reference could not express.

It also decides what the phrase *"the judge agrees with people"* can mean. If a preference flips when
you swap the inputs, that preference was never evidence about the answers, and an agreement figure
computed over such preferences is counting coin flips that happened to land right. The paper's demo
measures exactly that.

## The paper behind it

> **Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena**
> `arXiv:2306.05685` · 2023 · <https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685>

It reported that strong LLM judges reach *"over 80% agreement, the same level of agreement between
humans"* with human preferences, and named the failure modes that come with them — **position,
verbosity, and self-enhancement biases, as well as limited reasoning ability**.

Taught in this day's paper part:
[Judging the judge — agreement, and the three biases](../../papers/01-judging-the-judge.md).

## The mechanism

The comparison, and the control, in two functions. From `lab/papers/mtbench/arena.py`:

```python
def ask(judge, first: str, second: str, first_name: str, second_name: str) -> str:
    """Show the judge two answers in a given order; it returns 'a' or 'b'."""
    winner = judge(first, second)
    return first_name if winner == "first" else second_name


def verdict(judge, pair: Pair, *, swap_control: bool) -> str | None:
    """The judge's preference for a pair, or None when the two orderings disagree."""
    forward = ask(judge, pair.a, pair.b, "a", "b")
    if not swap_control:
        return forward
    backward = ask(judge, pair.b, pair.a, "b", "a")
    return forward if forward == backward else None
```

**Line by line:**

- `ask` separates **where an answer sat** from **which answer it was**. The judge returns `"first"` or
  `"second"` — a position — and `ask` translates that back into `"a"` or `"b"` using the names it was
  given. Without that translation you cannot express the bug, because the judge's answer would already
  be about the answers rather than about the slots.
- `verdict` calls `ask` twice with `pair.a` and `pair.b` **exchanged**, and exchanges the names to
  match. Getting the names wrong here inverts the second reading and makes every pair look consistent,
  which is a bug that produces a reassuring result.
- `-> str | None` and `return ... if forward == backward else None`. A disagreement is `None`, not a
  tie and not a default. That is the same three-valued discipline Day 78 part 4.4 argued for: *cannot
  say* is a distinct answer, and collapsing it into either of the others is where the damage happens.
- `*, swap_control: bool` is keyword-only, so no call site can pass `True` or `False` positionally and
  leave a reader guessing what the third argument was.
- Two calls per pair, not one. That is the entire cost of the control and it is the whole of part
  [4.1](../04-what-it-buys/4.1-priced-against-the-allowance.md)'s doubling term.

And the lab's own smaller version, which uses the same twelve answers twice to isolate the effect. From
`lab/bias.py`:

```python
    for left, right in pairs:
        # The judge is shown (left, right) and prefers the first one it sees.
        forward = left
        if not controlled:
            decided += 1
            first_wins += forward == left
            continue
        # Controlled: show the pair both ways round and keep the result only if it agrees.
        backward = right
        if forward == left and backward == left:
            decided += 1
            first_wins += 1
        elif forward == right and backward == right:
            decided += 1
```

**Line by line:**

- `forward = left` is the judge, written out: whatever is shown first wins. It is a caricature and it
  is deliberately one, because the point of this arm is to show what the control does, not to estimate
  how biased a real model is.
- `decided += 1` happens on **every** pair in the uncontrolled arm — every pair gets a verdict,
  because a single ordering always produces one.
- In the controlled arm, `decided` is only incremented when both orderings name the same answer. With
  a purely position-driven judge, `forward` names the left one and `backward` names the right one, so
  neither branch fires and nothing is decided.
- The two branches are written out rather than compared with `==` because the names matter: the first
  is "both orderings chose a", the second is "both chose b", and only the first counts as the first
  answer winning.

Run it without the control:

```bash
cd days/day-81-llm-as-judge/lab
uv run python bias.py --position; echo "exit: $?"
```

**Line by line:**

- `--position` runs twelve pairs through a single ordering, which is what a pairwise judge does unless
  somebody deliberately adds the swap.

```text
judge: always prefers whichever answer it is shown first

```

Now ask each pair both ways round:

```bash
uv run python bias.py --position-controlled; echo "exit: $?"
```

**Line by line:**

- `--position-controlled` runs the same twelve pairs, the same judge, and keeps only the pairs whose
  two orderings agree.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
swap control: on

  pairs judged:            12
  pairs with a verdict:    0
  the first answer won:    0

  no pair survived being shown both ways round
exit: 0
```

**Twelve verdicts become zero.** Read the first run's output as a report somebody would circulate:
*twelve pairs judged, twelve verdicts, a clear preference in every case.* Every column in it is true.
There is nothing in it — no confidence figure, no flag, no note — that hints the entire result is the
order the answers were passed in.

The second run's `0` is not a failure of the judge. It is the honest count of how much this judge
knows about these answers, which is nothing, reported for the first time.

## When it breaks

The control has a cost that is not the requests, and it is the reason teams abandon it: **it takes
your sample away.**

A real judge is not the caricature above. It reads most pairs and falls back to position on the ones
it cannot tell apart — and those are the close pairs, which are the interesting pairs. So the control
drops, disproportionately, the comparisons you most wanted answered, and the surviving sample is
biased towards the easy ones.

The paper's demo measures exactly this shape:

```text
  pairs judged:          16
  pairs with a verdict:  11
  agreed with the human: 11
  agreement:             100.0% of the pairs with a verdict
  agreement:             68.8% of all pairs
```

Two agreement figures from one run, and both are true. `100%` is the honest answer to *"when this
judge commits, is it right?"*. `68.8%` is the honest answer to *"can this judge answer my question?"*.
Reporting only the first is the most common way an LLM-as-judge result is overstated, and it is not
dishonesty — it is picking the denominator that the control produced.

There is no error text for any of this. The smallest fix is to report **both numbers, always**, and to
report the number of dropped pairs beside them. A pairwise result with one percentage in it is
incomplete.

## In production

**What a professional does with a pairwise judge.** Randomises the order *and* swaps, records which
ordering was used on each call, and reports the inconsistency rate as a first-class metric — because
that rate is a health measure of the judge, independent of what it is judging. A rising inconsistency
rate after a model upgrade is the earliest warning available that the rater has changed.

**What degrades at scale.** Position effects are strongest on close comparisons, and close comparisons
are what you have once the obvious improvements are done. Early in a project the judge is deciding
between a good answer and a bad one and the bias barely shows; a year later it is deciding between two
good answers and the drop rate climbs — so the metric gets less usable exactly as the questions get
more interesting.

**The failure that only shows with real traffic.** Ordering is rarely random in practice. The
"current" system goes first and the "candidate" second, or vice versa, consistently, because that is
how the loop was written — so position bias becomes a **systematic** advantage for one side of every
comparison you run, in the same direction, forever. That is worse than noise, and it is invisible
without the swap.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Never report a pairwise win rate without the
consistency rate next to it. And randomise which side the candidate goes on — right now the candidate
is always second, so if this judge has any position preference at all we have been giving the
incumbent a systematic edge on every comparison since we started."*

**The interview question:** *"You use an LLM to pick the better of two responses. How do you know the
preference is about the responses?"* The answer that shows experience swaps the order and requires
agreement — and then names the cost honestly, which is that the sample shrinks and the pairs you lose
are the close ones you cared about.

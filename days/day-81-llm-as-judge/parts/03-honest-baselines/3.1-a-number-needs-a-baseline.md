# 3.1 — A number needs something to be better than

## One-line answer

An accuracy figure is not a measurement until you know what a procedure that does nothing scores on
the same data — and on Sutra's fixture that number is **83.3%**, which is exactly what a judge that
never read anything achieved.

## The story

The board outside the shop says **90% of our customers are satisfied.**

It is a real survey. They really asked people. Nobody made the number up.

Stand there for a moment and try to work out what you have learned. Is ninety good? Compared with the
shop next door? Compared with shops in general? Compared with what people say when a person with a
clipboard asks them a question in a shop they are standing inside?

You cannot answer any of those, because the number arrived alone. And ninety per cent *sounds* like a
lot — it is near a hundred, it is an A — so it does a job on you before you notice you have not been
told anything.

The version of the sign that would be worth reading has two numbers on it. Ninety per cent, and: the
average for this kind of shop is eighty-eight.

That second number is smaller, less flattering, and the only reason the first one means anything.

## The idea in plain language

Part [1.2](../01-the-judge-is-a-system/1.2-the-judge-disagreed-with-itself.md) measured a judge that
never read a single answer and agreed with the human labels **20 of 24 times**. That is not a story
about a broken judge. It is a story about a **number reported alone**.

A **baseline** is what a deliberately trivial procedure scores on the same data. Not a bad system — a
non-system: one that ignores the input entirely and still produces answers.

Three things a baseline gives you, and the first is the only one people expect:

**A floor.** Your number minus the baseline is the part that came from your work. 83.3% against a
baseline of 83.3% is zero work.

**A unit.** *"Ten points better than always-valid"* is a sentence that survives the class balance
changing; *"87.5% accurate"* is not, because it moves when the population moves even if the judge does
not.

**A sanity check on the metric itself.** If nothing you build ever beats the baseline, the problem is
usually not the model. It is that the metric is measuring something the baseline already has — which
on skewed data is almost always the base rate.

The **base rate** is the term for the fraction of the data that falls in the most common class. It is
the number that makes a majority-class baseline strong, and it is high in almost every situation
worth building for: most answers are fine, most tickets are routine, most nights are quiet, most
transactions are honest. **The rarer the thing you care about, the higher the score a system that
ignores it can achieve.**

That inversion is the whole of this section. Accuracy is at its most misleading exactly where the
stakes are highest.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because the sentence *"the judge agrees with human labels 83% of the time"* is the first output every
LLM-as-judge project produces, and on this fixture it is true of a rater that flips a biased coin.
Reporting it without a baseline is not a small imprecision; it is the difference between having a judge
and having a majority-class predictor with a large bill.

It matters again for the desk itself, immediately. Day 82's regression discipline compares tonight's
numbers with last night's, and a metric with no baseline attached cannot distinguish "the desk got
better" from "the mix of tickets changed". Part
[3.3](3.3-the-eighty-seven-that-was-eighty-three.md) measures a case of exactly that.

And it is the honest reading of Day 80's kappa. That day's paper is the same idea with arithmetic
attached: *observed agreement minus chance agreement*. A baseline is chance agreement, chosen by hand
rather than computed, and part [3.2](3.2-three-baselines-you-can-always-build.md) uses both.

## The mechanism

The baselines are computed from the human labels themselves. From `lab/baseline.py`:

```python
HUMAN = [a.human for a in ANSWERS]
...
    majority_label = Counter(HUMAN).most_common(1)[0][0]
    base_rate = HUMAN.count(majority_label) / n
```

**Line by line:**

- `HUMAN` is the list of labels a person gave, in fixture order. Everything in this script is measured
  against it and nothing modifies it.
- `Counter(HUMAN).most_common(1)[0][0]` finds the **majority class** rather than hard-coding
  `"valid"`. That matters: on a suite where most answers were wrong, the strong baseline would be
  `always invalid`, and a script that assumed otherwise would report a flattering comparison against a
  weak baseline.
- `base_rate` is that class's share. It is the score `always <majority>` achieves, by definition, and
  it is printed at the top of the output so the reader sees it before any judge's number.
- Both are computed **from the data**, not configured. A baseline you have to remember to update is a
  baseline that will be out of date the first time the fixture grows.

And the comparison, which puts every candidate on the same footing:

```python
    candidates = [
        ("the judge (3-2 coin)", COIN_JUDGE),
        ("the judge (reads it)", GOOD_JUDGE),
        (f"baseline: always {majority_label}", [majority_label] * n),
        ("baseline: always invalid", ["invalid"] * n),
        ("baseline: alternating", ["valid" if i % 2 else "invalid" for i in range(n)]),
    ]
```

**Line by line:**

- Judges and baselines are in **one list**, scored by the same code and printed in the same table.
  Putting the baselines in a separate section invites reading the judges first and the baselines as a
  footnote, which is the failure this part is about.
- `[majority_label] * n` is the majority-class baseline: one label, repeated, ignoring every input.
- `always invalid` is the minority-class baseline. It scores terribly here and it is kept in the table
  on purpose — it shows the reader the range, so `83.3%` can be located between `16.7%` and `100%`
  rather than floating alone.
- The alternating baseline ignores the input and produces both labels, so it lands near 50% however
  skewed the data is. It is the one that separates *"my judge beats a constant"* from *"my judge beats
  a coin"*, which are different claims.
- Every entry is a **list of labels**, not a callable. That is what lets a real judge's output be
  dropped into the same table without any adapter — it is just another label list.

Run it:

```bash
cd days/day-81-llm-as-judge/lab
uv run python baseline.py; echo "exit: $?"
```

**Line by line:**

- No flag: every candidate against the human labels, with the base rate printed first.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
24 answers, human labels: {'valid': 20, 'invalid': 4}
majority class 'valid' is 83.3% of the suite

                             agreement   kappa  over baseline
  the judge (3-2 coin)           83.3%    0.00          +0.0%
  the judge (reads it)           95.8%    0.83         +12.5%
  baseline: always valid         83.3%    0.00          +0.0%
  baseline: always invalid       16.7%    0.00         -66.7%
  baseline: alternating          50.0%    0.00         -33.3%

  the coin judge beats the majority-class baseline by +0.0%
  the reading judge beats it by +12.5%

  one of these two judges is not reading the answers, and its accuracy hides it
exit: 1
```

**Read the first two rows against the third.** The coin judge and `always valid` produce the **same
number to the decimal place**, because they are the same procedure wearing different clothes: a rater
whose majority is always valid and a constant that is always valid agree with the humans on exactly
the twenty valid cases.

And read the `over baseline` column, which is the one that should be on any slide. `+0.0%` and
`+12.5%`. Those two numbers separate the judges immediately, and the `agreement` column — 83.3% and
95.8% — makes them look like a good judge and a slightly better one.

The `kappa` column is Day 80's correction and it agrees: 0.00 for the coin, 0.83 for the rater that
reads. A coefficient of exactly zero is what "no better than chance" looks like arithmetically, and
it is worth noticing that all three baselines score 0.00 as well. That is not a coincidence — it is
what kappa is for.

## When it breaks

The baseline is right and the comparison is against the wrong one. Three ways, all of which produce a
flattering number:

**Comparing against random when the data is skewed.** Random is 50%, so 83.3% looks like a
thirty-point win. The right comparison is the strongest trivial procedure, which here is the majority
class, and it is a zero-point win.

**A baseline computed on different data.** The baseline has to come from the same set, in the same
proportions. A base rate from last quarter compared against this quarter's judge is comparing two
populations.

**A baseline nobody recomputes.** The class balance moves — the desk improves, so a smaller fraction
of answers are wrong — and the base rate moves with it. A hard-coded 83.3% in a report is a number
that ages silently, and it ages in the direction that makes your system look better.

There is no error message for any of these. The failure looks like this, which is the ablation:

```bash
uv run python baseline.py --raw; echo "exit: $?"
```

**Line by line:**

- `--raw` prints the two judges' agreement figures and nothing else — the report a project produces in
  its first month.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
24 answers, human labels: {'valid': 20, 'invalid': 4}
majority class 'valid' is 83.3% of the suite

                            agreement
  the judge (3-2 coin)          83.3%
  the judge (reads it)          95.8%

  both judges agree with us most of the time; ship it
exit: 0
```

Two judges, both apparently good, one of which is a coin. Every number is correct. The class balance
is even printed at the top and does not help, because a reader who is not looking for it does not
connect `83.3% of the suite` in line two with `83.3%` in line five.

The smallest fix is a formatting rule rather than a code change: **never print an accuracy without the
baseline in the same table**, adjacent, in the same units. Not in a footnote and not in an appendix —
the next column.

## In production

**What a professional reports.** Three numbers per evaluation: the score, the baseline, and the
difference — plus the base rate and the sample size. Five numbers, one line, and every question a
reader can reasonably ask is answered before they ask it. Any one of the five removed makes the other
four less interpretable.

**What degrades at scale.** Base rates drift, always towards the majority, because systems improve.
A desk that was wrong 17% of the time is wrong 5% of the time a year later — which pushes the
majority-class baseline from 83% to 95% and makes every judge look worse against it, while the judge
has not changed at all. The margin over baseline is the honest quantity, and it shrinks as the system
gets better, which is uncomfortable and correct.

**The failure that only shows with real traffic.** The labelled sample is not the traffic. Cases get
labelled because somebody found them interesting, so a hand-built evaluation set is systematically
richer in failures than production is — which means the base rate on your set is *lower* than the real
one, and your judge looks better than it will in service. The correction is to sample for labelling
at random from real traffic, which is slower and much less interesting.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Put the majority-class number in the same table, in
the next column, every time. And recompute it from the current set rather than pasting it — the day
our desk gets better, that baseline moves, and I would rather find that out from the table than from
somebody noticing our judge has been flat for two quarters."*

**The interview question:** *"Your classifier is 94% accurate. Is that good?"* The answer that shows
experience asks for the class balance before saying anything, because 94% on a set that is 94% one
class is what a constant achieves — and then asks what a person scores on the same set, because that
is the ceiling and it is rarely 100%.

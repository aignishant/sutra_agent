# 3.3 — The 83.3% that was free

## One-line answer

💥 Remove one column from the report and a judge that never read an answer and a judge that reads
correctly become *"83.3% and 95.8% — both agree with us most of the time; ship it"*, with every number
in that sentence true.

## The story

A test for a disease that one person in a thousand has, and the leaflet says it is **99% accurate**.

You take it. It says positive. What are the chances you have the disease?

Almost everybody's first answer is ninety-nine per cent, and the real answer is about nine per cent.
In a thousand people, one has it and the test finds them; of the nine hundred and ninety-nine who do
not, the one per cent error rate produces about ten false positives. Eleven positive results, one of
them real.

The leaflet is not lying. Ninety-nine per cent accurate is a fair description of that test. And a test
that simply printed **negative** on every slip, without touching the sample, would be **99.9%**
accurate on the same population — better than the real one, by the number on the leaflet, while
detecting nothing.

The number was never wrong. It was answering a different question from the one you were asking, and
nothing on the leaflet says which.

## The idea in plain language

Part [3.1](3.1-a-number-needs-a-baseline.md) established the rule and part
[3.2](3.2-three-baselines-you-can-always-build.md) gave the three baselines. This part runs the
ablation: **take the baseline column out and see what the same run says.**

It is not a hypothetical. It is the default. Every eval harness in the world prints the score, because
that is what a harness is for; printing the baseline beside it is a decision somebody has to make, and
it is not the decision that gets made in the first month.

Three things happen when the column is missing, and they compound:

- **The two judges become comparable to each other rather than to anything real.** 83.3% and 95.8%
  read as "good and better", which is a true statement about their ordering and tells you nothing
  about whether either is doing anything.
- **The bigger number becomes the story.** 95.8% is the one that goes on the slide, and the coin judge
  quietly becomes the fallback option for when the good one is too expensive.
- **The base rate is present and ignored.** It is printed at the top of the output — `majority class
  'valid' is 83.3% of the suite` — three lines above a judge scoring exactly 83.3%, and a reader who
  is not looking for the coincidence does not see it.

That third point is the one worth sitting with, because it means the fix is not "provide the
information". The information was there. The fix is **adjacency**: the baseline has to be in the same
row, in the same units, in the reader's eye at the same moment as the score.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because this is what Day 82 will put on a dashboard and Day 83's gate will read. *"Full evalset
green"* has to mean something, and a judged score reported without a baseline can be green for a
reason that has nothing to do with the desk.

And because the coin judge in this fixture is not a strawman — it is what a real judge looks like when
it is cheap, under-sampled or reading a prompt that does not contain what the question needs. Part
[1.3](../01-the-judge-is-a-system/1.3-what-the-judge-is-shown.md) measured that a rater grading Sutra's
desk is briefed for an agent that calls APIs and writes Python. A rater that cannot locate the question
in what it was shown falls back on the majority, and the majority is 83.3%.

## The mechanism

The ablation is one branch. From `lab/baseline.py`:

```python
    if raw:
        print(f"  {'':<26}agreement")
        for name, labels in candidates[:2]:
            print(f"  {name:<26}{agreement(HUMAN, labels):>9.1%}")
        print("\n  both judges agree with us most of the time; ship it")
        return 0
```

**Line by line:**

- `candidates[:2]` takes the two judges and **drops the three baselines**. Not by deleting them —
  they are still computed a few lines later in the honest arm — but by not printing them, which is
  exactly how a report loses them.
- One column: `agreement`. No kappa, no margin. Two numbers and a sentence.
- The closing sentence is written in the register a summary is actually written in: *"both judges
  agree with us most of the time; ship it."* Not a lie, not a hedge, and a completely reasonable thing
  to write from those two rows.
- `return 0`. The ablation **passes**, which is the point — the run that hides the finding is the run
  that exits green. If the honest arm did not exit 1, this pair of commands would not be an eval that
  can go red.

And the honest arm, which differs by three rows and two columns:

```python
    print(f"  {'':<26}{'agreement':>10}{'kappa':>8}{'over baseline':>15}")
    for name, labels in candidates:
        a = agreement(HUMAN, labels)
        over = a - base_rate
        print(f"  {name:<26}{a:>10.1%}{kappa(HUMAN, labels):>8.2f}{over:>+15.1%}")
```

**Line by line:**

- `for name, labels in candidates` — the full list this time, so the baselines are printed *between*
  and *below* the judges rather than in a separate block. A reader scanning the column meets 83.3%
  twice.
- Three columns rather than one. `agreement` is the number people ask for, `kappa` is Day 80's
  chance-corrected version, and `over baseline` is the one that answers the question.
- `over` is computed against `base_rate` — the strongest baseline — for the reason part
  [3.2](3.2-three-baselines-you-can-always-build.md) gave: comparing against the weak ones is
  choosing the flattering denominator.

And the decision the script makes at the end:

```python
    if coin_over < WORTH_HAVING <= good_over:
        print("\n  one of these two judges is not reading the answers, and its accuracy hides it")
        return 1
```

**Line by line:**

- `WORTH_HAVING = 0.05` is a threshold on the **margin**, not on the accuracy — five points over
  baseline. It is a convention and the constant is named so it reads as one.
- The comparison is a chained one: the coin judge is below the margin **and** the reading judge is
  above it. That is the specific finding worth failing on — two judges whose accuracies look similar
  and whose margins do not.
- `return 1`. The honest arm goes red and the ablation goes green, which is the shape every ablation in
  this curriculum has.

Run the honest arm:

```bash
cd days/day-81-llm-as-judge/lab
uv run python baseline.py; echo "exit: $?"
```

**Line by line:**

- No flag: five rows, three columns, and the base rate printed above them.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
  the judge (reads it)           95.8%    0.83         +12.5%

```

Now take the column away:

```bash
uv run python baseline.py --raw; echo "exit: $?"
```

**Line by line:**

- `--raw` prints the two judges and their agreement. Same data, same computation, fewer columns.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
24 answers, human labels: {'valid': 20, 'invalid': 4}
majority class 'valid' is 83.3% of the suite

                            agreement
  the judge (3-2 coin)          83.3%
  the judge (reads it)          95.8%

  both judges agree with us most of the time; ship it
exit: 0
```

**Eighty-three and ninety-six, and ship it.** Both numbers are correct. The class balance is printed
two lines above. The word "baseline" does not appear, and neither does anything false.

And notice which run exits 0. The report that hides the finding is the one that passes; the report
that surfaces it fails. That is the shape a metric takes when it is measuring the wrong thing, and it
is why part [5.2](../05-in-production/5.2-what-a-real-judged-suite-adds.md) puts this command in CI
rather than in a runbook.

## When it breaks

There is no error at any point, and the run that produces the misleading report is a **successful**
run.

The way it reaches a decision is worth writing out, because every step is somebody being reasonable:

```text
1. the good judge costs 240 requests per evaluation (part 4.1)
2. somebody proposes a cheaper judge, or fewer samples
3. the cheaper configuration scores 83.3%, four points below 95.8%
4. four points to save five times the cost is an easy trade
5. the cheaper judge is a coin, and the four points were all of the signal
```

Step 4 is the one that looks like good engineering judgement. Four points is a small number; it is
also, on this fixture, **the entire distance between reading the answers and not reading them**. The
margin column says +12.5% against +0.0% and makes step 4 impossible to argue for; the accuracy column
says 95.8% against 83.3% and makes it obvious.

The smallest fix is a rule about reports rather than a code change: **an accuracy figure and its
baseline appear in the same row, or neither appears.** If the baseline is not available for some
reason, the accuracy is not reportable either — which sounds severe and is the only version of the
rule that survives contact with a slide deck.

## In production

**What a professional puts on the dashboard.** The margin, as the headline, with the raw accuracy as a
secondary column. Inverting the usual order is deliberate: whatever is largest and topmost is what
people react to, and the margin is the quantity that behaves sensibly when the population moves. A
dashboard whose headline number rises because the desk got better at easy tickets is a dashboard that
will be trusted for about a year.

**What degrades at scale.** Reports get shorter as they get more senior. The engineering run has five
columns, the team summary has two, and the number that reaches a decision has one — and the column
that gets dropped at each stage is the one that needs explaining, which is the baseline. The defence is
not to fight it but to make the top-line number *already* the margin, so that the surviving column is
the honest one.

**The failure that only shows with real traffic.** The base rate is not stable, so the margin is not
either. A desk that improves pushes the majority class up, which shrinks every judge's margin without
any judge changing — so the honest metric gets worse-looking as the system gets better. That is
correct and it is deeply unintuitive, and a team that has not been told will read a shrinking margin
as a regression.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Make the margin the first column and accuracy the
second. And put `baseline.py` in CI with the exit code — right now the only thing standing between us
and the cheap judge is that somebody remembered to look at a column, and that is not a control."*

**The interview question:** *"Two candidate evaluators score 83% and 96%. The cheaper one is the 83%.
What do you need to know?"* The answer that shows experience asks for the base rate first, and then
says what it would mean if the base rate were 83% — that the cheaper option is not a slightly worse
judge, it is not a judge. The tell is whether they ask before they trade.

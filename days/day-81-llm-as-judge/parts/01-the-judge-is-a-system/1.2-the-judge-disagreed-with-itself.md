# 1.2 — The judge disagreed with itself

## One-line answer

💥 A judge that answers the same question five times and splits three-two on **every single case**
agrees with the human labels on **20 of 24** — because twenty of the twenty-four are valid and the
majority vote always lands on valid.

## The story

You ask a shopkeeper the price of something and he says two hundred.

You come back an hour later, having thought about it, and ask again. Two hundred and twenty. You
mention that he said two hundred earlier and he says yes, two hundred, that is right.

Now: what is the price?

He is not lying to you. He is not running a scheme. He has three hundred things in the shop, he
prices from memory and from what the last person paid, and there is a genuine range in his head that
he draws from each time you ask. If you asked five times you would get two hundred three times and
two twenty twice, and he would mean all of them.

The thing to notice is what happens if you only ask once. You get a number, said with complete
confidence, and you have no way at all to know whether that number was the one he always says or one
end of a range he is drawing from. The confidence is identical either way.

## The idea in plain language

Part [1.1](1.1-a-judge-is-a-model-with-a-job.md) said `num_samples` exists because raters are
unreliable, and that the majority vote resolves it. This part measures what the vote hides.

Two facts about `FinalResponseMatchV2Evaluator`'s aggregation on `google-adk==2.7.1`, both from its
own source and its own docstring:

- **The vote is a majority over the successfully-graded samples**, and — quoting the docstring —
  *"In the case of a tie, consider the result to be invalid."* That is a policy, not a law.
- **The result carries no margin.** `EvaluationResult` has `overall_score`, `overall_eval_status`,
  `per_invocation_results` and `overall_rubric_scores`. There is no field for how close the vote was.

So a 5–0 and a 3–2 produce identical output. Day 80 part 4.1 found the same for the rubric metric;
here the consequence is sharper, because the aggregation is happening on top of a **skewed** set of
cases.

That is the finding. Take a judge that is a pure coin — valid, invalid, valid, valid, invalid, every
time, regardless of what it is reading — and its majority is *always* `valid`. Now score it against a
suite where twenty of twenty-four answers really are valid, and it agrees with the human on twenty of
twenty-four.

**83.3% agreement, from a rater that never read anything.**

Two things follow, and they are the two halves of the day's title.

**The reported score is a function of the vote, not of the samples.** So a judge whose per-sample
behaviour is nearly random can produce a per-case verdict that is perfectly stable and completely
uninformative. The vote does not merely hide the disagreement; on a skewed suite it *converts*
disagreement into the majority class.

**An agreement percentage is meaningless without its baseline.** 83.3% is exactly what you get from
`always valid`, which is section 3's subject. The vote and the base rate conspire, and the number
that comes out looks like a measurement.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because this is the number a team will report. *"Our judge agrees with human labels 83% of the
time"* is the sentence every LLM-as-judge project produces in its first month, and on Sutra's own
fixture that sentence is true of a rater that flips a biased coin.

And because Day 82 is about to compare this number
against yesterday's, every night. A metric built on a wobbling judge does not merely give you a wrong
number — it gives you a number that moves for reasons that have nothing to do with the desk.

## The mechanism

The wobbling judge is one list. From `lab/judge.py`:

```python
SAMPLES = 5

# A judge that changes its mind: valid, invalid, valid, valid, invalid - a 3-2 split every time.
WOBBLE = ["valid", "invalid", "valid", "valid", "invalid"]
```

**Line by line:**

- Five labels for five samples, with three valid and two invalid, so **every** case is a 3–2 split.
  Not a random judge — a deterministically inconsistent one, which is what makes the measurement
  reproducible rather than a simulation you have to run many times.
- The list starts with `valid`, which matters for the tie rule: with `num_samples=4` this would be
  2–2 and the docstring's *"a tie is invalid"* would flip every case. The lab uses 5 because that is
  ADK's default and because an odd count makes the vote unambiguous.
- It is a module constant rather than something generated, so a reader can see the exact behaviour
  being attributed to the judge.

And the behaviour that drives it, from `lab/_judge.py`:

```python
def wobbling(labels: list[str]) -> Behaviour:
    """A judge that cycles through a fixed list of labels, one per call."""
    return lambda _prompt, call: labels[call % len(labels)]
```

**Line by line:**

- It ignores `_prompt` entirely. That is the whole point: this rater does not look at the answer, the
  reference or the question. Naming the parameter with a leading underscore says so at the call site.
- `call % len(labels)` cycles, so sample *n* of every case gets the same label. With five samples and
  a five-element list, each case sees the identical 3–2 pattern.
- `Behaviour` is `Callable[[str, int], str]` — the prompt and the call number in, a label out. A
  script that took no call number could not express inconsistency at all, which is why part
  [2.1](../02-the-judge-has-biases/2.1-position.md)'s biases and this one share a signature.

Run the control first — a judge that reads correctly:

```bash
cd days/day-81-llm-as-judge/lab
uv run python judge.py; echo "exit: $?"
```

**Line by line:**

- No flag: the judge gives the human's verdict on every case, so this is the ceiling.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
judge: gives the human verdict
cases: 24

  judge agreed with the human on 24 of 24 cases
exit: 0
```

Now the coin:

```bash
uv run python judge.py --wobble; echo "exit: $?"
```

**Line by line:**

- `--wobble` installs the 3–2 cycle. Same evaluator, same threshold, same five samples, same
  twenty-four cases, same reference answers. The only change is a rater that is not reading.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
metric: final_response_match_v2, threshold 1.0, num_samples 5
judge: 3-2 split on every case
cases: 24

  judge agreed with the human on 20 of 24 cases
    x01: human said invalid, judge said valid
    x02: human said invalid, judge said valid
    x03: human said invalid, judge said valid
    x04: human said invalid, judge said valid

  the judge was 3-2 on every case, 5 samples each
  every reported score is 1.0 or 0.0; none of them says 3-2
exit: 1
```

**Twenty of twenty-four.** Read the four disagreements: they are `x01` through `x04`, which are
*every* invalid case in the fixture and nothing else. This judge got every wrong answer wrong, and
every right answer right, and scored 83.3%.

It is worth being precise about why that is not bad luck. The suite is 20 valid and 4 invalid. A
rater whose majority is always `valid` is correct on exactly the valid ones. So its accuracy is the
**base rate**, and it will be the base rate on any suite, forever, without ever improving or
degrading.

And the last line of the output is the second half of the finding: `none of them says 3-2`. Compare
the two runs' result objects and there is nothing to distinguish a rater that was certain from one
that was a coin. The four disagreements are visible only because the lab has human labels to compare
against — which is exactly what a project without labels does not have.

## When it breaks

There is no error. Both runs complete, both produce well-formed results, and one of them is a metric
that has stopped measuring.

The way it reaches production is through the cost conversation. Part
[4.1](../04-what-it-buys/4.1-priced-against-the-allowance.md) prices this suite at 240 requests
against a measured allowance of 20 a day, and the first lever anybody reaches for is `num_samples`.
Cut it from 5 to 1 and the cost drops by a factor of five, and the metric becomes a **single draw**:

- a judge that is genuinely 3–2 uncertain now returns valid or invalid depending on which sample you
  happened to get;
- the same case, re-run, can flip;
- and Day 82's nightly comparison reports it as a regression.

There is a second, quieter version that has no cost pressure behind it: a judge that is 5–0 today and
3–2 in six months, because the model behind it was updated. The score does not move at all — the
majority is still the majority — and the rater has become substantially less sure. Nothing in the
result says so, and nothing in a dashboard built on the score can.

The smallest fix is to keep what you already paid for:

```python
margins = [sum(1 for s in samples if s.score == 1.0) for samples in per_case_samples]
```

**Line by line:**

- The samples exist inside `LlmAsJudge.evaluate_invocations` before the aggregator runs; capturing
  them needs a subclass or a wrapper, not extra requests.
- One count per case — how many samples voted valid — is enough. From that, "was this unanimous?" is
  a comparison against 0 or `num_samples`, and that single flag catches every case in this part.
- Reported as a **rate over the suite**, not per case: *"3 of 24 cases were not unanimous"* is a line
  somebody reads; twenty-four margins are a table nobody does.

## In production

**What a professional records.** The margin, and the judge's model version, on every case. Neither
costs a request. Together they answer the question that arrives the first time a nightly run
disagrees with the previous one: *did the desk change, or did the rater?*

**What degrades at scale.** Judge uncertainty is not spread evenly. It concentrates on the answers
that are genuinely borderline — partially correct, correct but oddly phrased, correct with an extra
claim — which are exactly the cases a team is trying to make decisions about. So the flickering cases
are the interesting cases, and a suite tuned until it stops flickering has usually been tuned until it
stops asking.

**The failure that only shows with real traffic.** Class balance drifts. This fixture is 83% valid
because the desk is mostly right; after a regression it might be 60% valid, and the coin judge's
"accuracy" would drop to 60% — which looks like the judge got worse. Any accuracy figure computed
against a skewed, moving population is measuring the population as much as the rater, and section 3
is the correction.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Before we quote an agreement figure anywhere,
compute what `always valid` scores on the same set. If the two numbers are within a couple of points
we do not have a judge, we have a majority-class predictor with a large bill. And keep the vote
margins — we are paying for five opinions and reporting one bit."*

**The interview question:** *"Your LLM judge agrees with human labels 83% of the time. Good?"* The
answer that shows experience asks for the class balance before answering, because 83% on a set that is
83% one class is exactly what a constant predictor achieves. The tell is whether they ask *before*
they say anything about the judge.

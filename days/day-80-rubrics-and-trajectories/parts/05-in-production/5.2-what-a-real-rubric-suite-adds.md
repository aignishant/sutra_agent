# 5.2 — What a real rubric suite adds

## One-line answer

The lab grades whole conversations against named criteria with ADK's real evaluator at zero cost, and
the distance between that and a rubric suite a team would act on is nine specific things — one of
which is a `noqa` away from being done today, and two of which are parked.

## The story

The calibration log in a workshop that measures things for a living.

The instruments get checked. Not because anybody expects them to be wrong — they are good
instruments — but because an instrument that has drifted reads confidently and there is nothing on
its face to say so. So there is a routine: check against a reference block, write the date, write the
reading, sign it.

The routine did not arrive complete. Somebody added the reference block after a batch went out
undersized. Somebody added "record the temperature" after a summer week when everything read long.
Somebody added "two operators, independently" after two people got different numbers from the same
gauge and there was an argument nobody could settle.

Read as a list it is bureaucracy. Read line by line it is a history of every way this workshop has
been fooled by a confident number.

## The idea in plain language

The lab does four things properly. It grades a whole conversation as one unit rather than turn by
turn. It runs ADK's own evaluator — parser, vote, mean, threshold — at zero requests. It has been
**shown to fail**, three separate ways. And it prices itself against a measured allowance.

That is the mechanism. Here is the calibration log.

**1. Gate lines checked separately, before the threshold.**
Part [3.1](../03-escalated-before-any-write/3.1-a-safety-rule-as-a-rubric-line.md) measured the
problem: a conversation failing only `escalated_before_write` and one failing only
`told_the_customer` both score 0.75, and no threshold separates them. Read `overall_rubric_scores`,
check the named gate lines first, and treat a `None` on a gate line as a failure rather than a skip.
This is first because it is the difference between a metric and a control.

**2. An assertion on the assembled prompt.**
Part [2.2](../02-the-evaluator/2.2-what-the-judge-is-shown.md) measured 4 tool calls rendered as 0
when `intermediate_data` is `IntermediateData` rather than `InvocationEvents`. One test — assert the
dialogue history contains `(tool call)` as many times as the conversation has calls — turns a silent
loss into a red build. Everything else on this list assumes the judge can see what it is grading.

**3. Tool calls carried as `InvocationEvents`.**
The conversion is `wired.py`'s `as_events`, about twenty lines. Without it, three of this day's four
rubric lines are answered from the desk's own sentences about itself.

**4. The kept-bad conversation, in CI.**
Part [3.2](../03-escalated-before-any-write/3.2-the-rubric-that-cannot-fail.md) measured a rubric
rewording that took the spread from 0.75 to 0.00 while every conversation scored 1.00. `grade.py`
already exits 1 on a zero spread; putting it in front of the suite means a rubric that has stopped
discriminating fails the build rather than reassuring somebody.

**5. Ungraded counted and reported.**
Part [3.3](../03-escalated-before-any-write/3.3-the-case-nobody-graded.md) measured a case coming back
`overall_score: None`, `NOT_EVALUATED`. Three numbers on the dashboard — passed, failed, ungraded —
always with the total, so a rising ungraded rate is visible next to a rising pass rate rather than
inside it.

**6. Per-sample verdicts kept.**
Part [4.1](../04-when-raters-disagree/4.1-three-samples-three-answers.md) measured a 1–2 split and a
3–0 producing byte-identical results. The samples are already paid for; keeping one flag per case —
*at least one line was not unanimous* — is the cheapest possible version and catches every flicker.

**7. Agreement measured on a balanced sample.**
Part [4.2](../04-when-raters-disagree/4.2-agreement-is-not-agreement.md) measured kappa −0.05 and 0.80
at the same 90% raw agreement. Rubric lines about rare failures need a deliberately balanced set —
equal numbers of conversations that pass and fail each line — kept for measuring the raters rather
than the desk.

**8. 🅿️ Parked — a second judge model, and a human panel.**
Everything in item 7 needs two raters. Two runs of one model is the cheap version and measures
consistency rather than correctness; a second model or a person measures something better. Both cost
requests this repository has not measured an allowance for, and human labelling costs somebody's
afternoon repeatedly. Day 81 is where the question becomes urgent.

**9. 🅿️ Parked — a rubric that is versioned like code.**
A rubric line is a claim, and claims change. A real suite records which version of the rubric produced
each score, so a score from March and one from September are comparable — or are known not to be.
It is a small piece of engineering and it is parked here because Sutra has one rubric and no history
yet, which is exactly when it is cheap and exactly when nobody does it.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because Day 81 and Day 82 consume this list directly. Day 81 is item 8 — LLM-as-judge and honest
baselines — and it inherits item 7's measurement. Day 82 is items 4 and 5, evals in CI with the
checks that keep a green result meaningful. Day 83's gate — *"Full evalset green; rubric trajectories
pass"* — cannot say what "pass" means without item 1.

And item 2 has a deadline of its own: every rubric line about tool use that anybody writes before it
exists is a line being answered from text.

## The mechanism

The list, with what each costs, so it can be argued with rather than admired:

| # | Item | Where it lands | Roughly |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | gate lines checked before the threshold | `sutra/rubrics.py` | an hour, plus the decision |
| 2 | assertion on the assembled prompt | `tests/test_rubrics.py` | half an hour |
| 3 | tool calls as `InvocationEvents` | wherever cases are built | half a day |
| 4 | kept-bad conversation in CI | one line in the build script | ten minutes |
| 5 | ungraded counted and reported | the results reader | an hour |
| 6 | per-sample verdicts kept | a subclass or a wrapper | half a day |
| 7 | agreement on a balanced sample | a second small evalset | a day, mostly labelling |
| 8 | 🅿️ second judge or human panel | not built | needs a measured allowance |
| 9 | 🅿️ rubric versioning | not built | cheap now, expensive later |

**Items 2 and 4 together are forty minutes**, and they are the two that stop the other seven from
being built on a metric that is not looking at what you think it is. Item 3 is the largest piece of
plumbing on the list and the one that decides whether item 2's assertion can pass at all.

Item 1's decision — which lines are gates — is the only entry that is not code. It is one set:

```python
GATE_LINES = {"escalated_before_write"}
```

**Line by line:**

- One line, in a file, that a reviewer can disagree with. That is the entire deliverable of item 1 and
  it is why the hub's build brief asks for the set before it asks for the function that uses it.
- A `set` rather than a list, because membership is the only question ever asked of it and duplicates
  are meaningless.
- One member, deliberately. Part [3.1](../03-escalated-before-any-write/3.1-a-safety-rule-as-a-rubric-line.md)'s
  **In production** section is why the number stays small: gate lines multiply, and a suite that is
  red every day on one gate or another teaches people to reclassify gates as contributors.

## When it breaks

The list breaks the way every such list breaks — it reads as improvements, so none of it is done —
and the sequence that follows is specific and has been measured in this day, step by step:

```text
1. cases are built with IntermediateData, the default              (item 3, part 2.2)
2. the judge sees 0 of 4 tool calls, and nothing says so           (item 2, part 2.2)
3. escalated_before_write is answered from the desk's own text
4. it comes back yes, because the desk's sentences are polite
5. the mean is 1.00, and the gate line is inside the mean          (item 1, part 3.1)
6. the nightly reports: 3 of 3 conversations passed                (item 5, part 3.3)
```

Six steps, no error at any of them, and the last line is the most reassuring output in this whole
curriculum. It is not one gap; it is three composing, and each one individually looks like a detail.

## In production

**What a professional does instead of building all nine.** Items 2 and 4, this week, because they are
forty minutes and they are the ones that make every other number trustworthy. Then item 1, because it
is the difference between a quality score and a control. The rest is genuinely optional and genuinely
ordered by what your last incident was.

**What degrades at scale.** Rubric lines accumulate and the mean flattens — part
[1.1](../01-a-rubric-is-a-decomposition/1.1-one-judgement-or-several.md)'s **In production** section
measured the shape: at four lines one failure moves the score 0.25, at twenty it moves 0.05, which is
inside the noise a real rater produces. So the metric becomes less able to see a single regression at
exactly the point the rubric becomes detailed enough to describe the system. Item 1's per-line checks
are what survive that; the mean does not.

**The failure that only shows with real traffic.** Every measurement in this day used a scripted
rater. A real one is inconsistent between samples, sensitive to the order things appear in, and
inclined to reward length — none of which a script reproduces. Item 7 is the measurement that would
tell you how much that matters, item 8 is what item 7 needs, and Day 81 is where both stop being
parked.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Do 2 and 4 before anything else — I do not want to
have a conversation about rubric wording while we are not sure the judge can see the tool calls. Then
1, and write the gate set down where a reviewer sees it. Put 7 on the calendar rather than the
backlog; without it we have no idea whether any of these lines is being read consistently, and that
is the number Day 81 is going to be built on."*

**The interview question:** *"What would you add to an LLM-graded rubric evaluation before trusting
it?"* The answer that shows experience starts with the prompt — can the judge see the thing the
criterion is about — rather than with more criteria or a better model, because a rater answering the
wrong question answers it fluently and every downstream improvement is built on that answer.

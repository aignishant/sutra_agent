# 5.1 — What a rubric costs

## One-line answer

A trajectory rubric is priced **per conversation**, not per rubric line — four lines and twelve lines
cost the same 30 requests on three conversations — so the only lever that reduces the bill is the one
that reduces coverage, and the cheapest thing you can do to this metric is make the rubric better.

## The story

Paying a marker to go through a stack of scripts.

The rate is per script. He picks one up, reads it, marks it, puts it down. A hundred scripts is a
hundred units of that, and everybody understands the arithmetic.

Now somebody says the marking scheme should have six criteria instead of three. Content, structure,
spelling, evidence, referencing, presentation.

What does that cost? Nothing, per script. He still picks it up once, reads it once and puts it down
once. He is looking for more things while he reads, and the reading was the expensive part. The rate
per script does not move.

Which means there is a thing that is nearly free and a thing that is not, and they are easy to
confuse: **asking more questions about each script is nearly free; marking more scripts is not.**

## The idea in plain language

Day 79 part 4.1 priced judged metrics as `cases × num_samples × num_runs`. A trajectory rubric fits
that formula with one substitution and one thing that is not in it at all.

- **cases** is the number of **conversations**, not turns. Part
  [1.3](../01-a-rubric-is-a-decomposition/1.3-the-whole-conversation-is-the-unit.md) measured it: a
  two-turn conversation with `num_samples=3` produced three judge calls, not six. The whole
  conversation is assembled into one prompt and graded once.
- **num_samples** is the same multiplier as before, defaulting to 5, and part
  [4.1](../04-when-raters-disagree/4.1-three-samples-three-answers.md) is why cutting it is the wrong
  lever.
- **num_runs** is `AgentEvaluator.evaluate`'s default of 2.
- **rubric lines do not appear.** Four lines or twelve, it is one prompt and one call. More lines
  means a longer prompt and a longer answer, which costs tokens, and it does not cost **requests** —
  which is the currency Addendum 02 denominates budgets in.

That last point flips the intuition people arrive with. A rubric feels expensive because it looks
like several checks. It is one check that asks several questions, and its cost is dominated by how
many conversations you grade and how many times you ask.

There is a second consequence, and it is the useful one. Because rubric lines are nearly free in
requests, **the cheapest improvement available to this metric is a better rubric**. Rewriting four
vague lines into four specific ones took part
[3.2](../03-escalated-before-any-write/3.2-the-rubric-that-cannot-fail.md)'s spread from 0.00 to 0.75
and cost exactly zero extra requests. Adding a fifth line costs zero extra requests. Grading a fourth
conversation costs ten.

The three levers, and what each actually does:

| Lever | Effect on requests | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| fewer conversations | linear reduction | less coverage — the thing you were buying |
| fewer samples | linear reduction | a noisier verdict (part 4.1) |
| fewer rubric lines | **none** | less information, for no saving at all |

**The third row is the one to remember.** Trimming a rubric to save money saves nothing.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because the numbers are uncomfortable and they need to be in front of somebody before Day 82 puts
this on a schedule. Three conversations, four lines, ADK's defaults: **30 requests**, against the 20
per day Day 78 measured for `gemini-3.7-flash` off a live 429.

Two conversations' worth of eval is more than a day's whole allowance on the only lane this
repository has ever observed. And Day 78's criterion 3 could not decide the Flash-Lite lane, so the
sentence *"run it on Flash-Lite instead"* is a plan resting on a number nobody has measured — the
same finding Day 79 part 4.2 recorded, now blocking a second thing.

## The mechanism

The arithmetic, made explicit rather than asserted. From `lab/cost.py`:

```python
NUM_SAMPLES = 5  # JudgeModelOptions.num_samples default on google-adk==2.7.1
NUM_RUNS = 2  # AgentEvaluator.evaluate's default on the same version
```

**Line by line:**

- Both constants carry the source of the number and the version it was read from, because a default
  copied from a library is still a number that needs a provenance — Principle 7 applied to
  arithmetic, as Day 78 part 1.1 argued for job costs.
- They are module constants rather than arguments, so the script cannot silently be run with
  different assumptions than the ones it prints.

```python
    total = cases * NUM_SAMPLES * NUM_RUNS
    print(f"\n  the rubric lines multiply the prompt, not the calls: {lines} lines, still 1 call")
```

**Line by line:**

- `cases * NUM_SAMPLES * NUM_RUNS` and **no `lines` term**. That absence is the whole part: the
  formula from Day 79 carries over unchanged, and the thing you would expect to be in it is not.
- `lines` is printed rather than multiplied, so a reader who expects it in the product sees it
  accounted for and excluded.
- `cases` is the number of conversations. On a per-turn metric this term would be turns, and a
  ten-turn conversation would cost five times a two-turn one; here it does not.

Run it:

```bash

```

**Line by line:**

Measured on 2026-09-06:

  the three levers, and what each does:

```bash

```

**Line by line:**

- `--grow` takes it to sixty conversations and twelve rubric lines — Addendum 02's "full 60+ only at
  phase gates" figure, with a rubric three times as detailed.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
  requests for one evaluation:        600
  a day's observed flash allowance:   20
  flash-lite allowance:               unmeasured

  that is 30x a whole day on the measured lane
```

**600 requests, and tripling the rubric contributed none of them.** Sixty conversations at five
samples at two runs. The twelve rubric lines are twelve extra questions in one prompt, twelve extra
blocks in one answer, and zero extra calls.

That is the number Day 83's phase gate has to live inside, and it is thirty times a day.

## When it breaks

The request count is right and the **token** count is where a rubric can still surprise you.

Part [2.2](../02-the-evaluator/2.2-what-the-judge-is-shown.md) measured the prompt for a two-turn
conversation at **8,909 characters**, and almost all of it is the fixed instruction block — the
mission, the five-step procedure, two worked examples, the output format. The conversation itself was
eight lines.

So the prompt for a forty-turn conversation is that same block plus forty turns of dialogue, and the
answer is one block per rubric line. Requests stay flat; tokens grow with both dimensions. On a
per-request free tier that is invisible, which is why this day counts requests — and on a paid tier
or a token-per-minute limit it is the whole bill.

There is a second thing that breaks and it has no error either: **the prompt has a limit and
conversations do not**. When the assembled dialogue exceeds what the judge accepts, something
truncates — and what is dropped is the beginning, which is where the escalation happened. The metric
keeps returning scores.

The smallest fix is to record two numbers per run rather than one:

```python
print(f"  prompt characters: {len(prompt)}, turns: {len(actual_invocations)}")
```

**Line by line:**

- Printed per case, not per suite, so the distribution is visible rather than an average — the long
  conversations are the ones at risk and an average hides them.
- Characters rather than tokens because it needs no tokenizer and moves with the thing you care
  about. A tokenizer is more accurate and is one more dependency for a number you are using as a
  tripwire.

## In production

**What a professional decides before writing the config.** Which conversations to grade, not which
rubric lines to write. Rubric lines are free and coverage is not, so the design conversation is about
sampling: grade every conversation that hit an approval gate, a stratified sample of the rest, and
nothing that was abandoned. That decision moves the bill by an order of magnitude; nothing about the
rubric does.

**What degrades at scale.** Conversation length. A support desk's median conversation gets longer as
the product gets more capable, and a rubric metric's cost per conversation is flat in requests and
rising in tokens — so the budget looks stable in the currency you are watching and moves in the one
you are not. On a free tier that is a slow surprise; on a paid one it is a fast one.

**The failure that only shows with real traffic.** Retries. A rate limit in the middle of a
`num_samples=5` loop means some samples land and some do not, and the aggregator votes over whatever
arrived — so a quota squeeze silently reduces the effective sample count on exactly the runs where
the system was busiest. The score gets noisier and nothing says why.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Stop trying to trim the rubric. It costs nothing.
What costs is sixty conversations at five samples — so tell me which conversations we actually need
graded, and let us run the full rubric on a third of them rather than a thin rubric on all of them.
And log the prompt size per case; the day one of these gets truncated I want it visible."*

**The interview question:** *"Your LLM-judged rubric evaluation is too expensive. What do you cut?"*
The answer that shows experience says the rubric lines are not where the money is — they multiply the
prompt, not the calls — and goes to the number of items graded and the sampling. The tell is whether
they then protect `num_samples`, because that is the one everybody cuts first and it is the one that
buys reliability.

# 4.2 — Agreement is not agreement

## One-line answer

Two raters agreeing 90% of the time on a rubric line the desk almost always passes is **worse than
chance** — measured at kappa −0.05 — while 90% agreement on a line the desk is genuinely inconsistent
about is kappa 0.80, and the raw percentage is identical in both.

## The story

Two people in a dry city are asked, every morning for a year, whether it will rain today.

Both say no, nearly always, because it nearly always does not rain. At the end of the year somebody
checks how often they gave the same answer: three hundred and fifty-five days out of three hundred
and sixty-five. Ninety-seven per cent.

You could write that number on a slide. *Our forecasters agree 97% of the time.*

It means nothing. Two people who had never met, who spoke no common language, who simply said "no"
every morning without looking outside, would score the same. The agreement is not evidence that they
are seeing the same sky; it is evidence that the sky is usually the same.

The days that would tell you anything are the ten it rained. And on those ten they might have agreed
twice.

## The idea in plain language

Part [4.1](4.1-three-samples-three-answers.md) established that raters disagree and that ADK resolves
it by voting. This part is about the question that comes next: **how do you know whether a rubric line
is being answered consistently at all?**

The obvious measurement is **raw agreement** — take two raters, or two runs of the same rater, and
count the fraction of items they gave the same verdict on. It is easy, it is intuitive, and it is
almost useless on its own for one reason: **it does not subtract the agreement you would get by
accident.**

If a rubric line is answered "yes" ninety-five per cent of the time — which most rubric lines are,
because most conversations are fine — then two raters who each say yes ninety-five per cent of the
time will agree about ninety per cent of the time **even if neither is looking at the conversation**.
Chance agreement is high precisely when the thing you are measuring is rare, and rubric lines about
safety failures are measuring something rare by design.

The fix is one subtraction, and it has a name and a 1960 paper behind it:

```text
kappa = (observed agreement - chance agreement) / (1 - chance agreement)
```

- **observed** is the raw fraction they matched on;
- **chance** is what two raters with those answering habits would match on if they answered
  independently;
- the denominator rescales so that 1.0 is perfect and 0.0 is "no better than chance".

Kappa can be **negative**, which is worth knowing because it looks like a bug the first time you see
one. Negative means the two raters agreed *less* than two people with their habits would have agreed
by accident.

Three things this buys, in the order they matter for a rubric:

**It tells you which lines are worth keeping.** A line whose two raters produce kappa near zero is a
line that is not being *read* — either it is vague (part
[3.2](../03-escalated-before-any-write/3.2-the-rubric-that-cannot-fail.md)) or the transcript does not
contain what it asks about (part [2.2](../02-the-evaluator/2.2-what-the-judge-is-shown.md)).

**It works between any two raters.** Two people. Two runs of the same model. A model and a person —
which is Day 81's subject, and the number that decides whether a judge can be trusted at all.

**It is the honest form of the sentence "the judge agrees with us".** A raw agreement figure quoted
without its chance baseline is the dry-city forecast.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because `escalated_before_write` is a rare-failure line. The desk is *supposed* to escalate, and
almost always does, so any two raters will say yes to it nearly every time — and the raw agreement on
this line will be the highest of the four while being the least informative of the four.

That is exactly backwards from what a team needs, and it will be read the other way round: the line
with the highest agreement will look like the most reliable one. The subtraction is what stops the
safety line's rarity from being mistaken for the safety line's clarity.

And it is what Day 81 needs immediately. *"The judge agrees with our human labels 92% of the time"* is
the sentence every LLM-as-judge project produces, and without a chance baseline it is not a
measurement.

## The paper behind it

> **A Coefficient of Agreement for Nominal Scales**
> Educational and Psychological Measurement 20(1), 1960, pp. 37–46 · `doi:10.1177/001316446002000104`
> <https://doi.org/10.1177/001316446002000104>

It showed that the fraction of times two raters agree is inflated by however often they would have
agreed by chance, and gave the correction that removes it.

[Agreement above chance — the 1960 coefficient](../../papers/01-agreement-above-chance.md).

## The mechanism

Three functions, and the middle one is the whole idea. From `lab/papers/kappa/kappa.py`:

```python
def observed_agreement(a: Verdicts, b: Verdicts) -> float:
    """The fraction of items the two raters gave the same verdict on."""
    return sum(1 for x, y in zip(a, b, strict=True) if x == y) / len(a)


def chance_agreement(a: Verdicts, b: Verdicts) -> float:
    """How often two raters with these habits would agree if they answered independently."""
    n = len(a)
    counts_a, counts_b = Counter(a), Counter(b)
    return sum((counts_a[label] / n) * (counts_b[label] / n) for label in set(a) | set(b))
```

**Line by line:**

- `zip(a, b, strict=True)` raises on mismatched lengths rather than silently truncating to the
  shorter. Two verdict lists that do not line up item-for-item are not comparable, and a quiet
  truncation would produce a plausible number from a bug.
- `chance_agreement` multiplies each rater's **marginal rate** for a label — how often *this* rater
  says yes, times how often *that* rater says yes — and sums over labels. That product is the
  probability they both land on the same label independently.
- The marginals come from the raters' own answers, not from an assumed prior. So chance agreement is
  computed from the habits the raters actually displayed on this data, which is what makes it a
  correction rather than an assumption.
- `set(a) | set(b)` covers labels one rater used and the other did not; those contribute zero, which
  is correct and which a naive iteration over one rater's labels would get wrong.

```python
def kappa(a: Verdicts, b: Verdicts) -> float:
    """(observed - chance) / (1 - chance): the agreement that chance does not explain."""
    p_o = observed_agreement(a, b)
    p_e = chance_agreement(a, b)
    if p_e == 1.0:
        return 0.0  # both raters always said the same thing; there is nothing to measure
    return (p_o - p_e) / (1 - p_e)
```

**Line by line:**

- The guard on `p_e == 1.0` is not defensive padding. If both raters said "yes" to every single item,
  chance agreement is 1.0, the denominator is zero, and kappa is genuinely undefined — there is no
  variation to explain. Returning 0.0 encodes "this measured nothing", which is the truthful reading.
- `(p_o - p_e)` can be negative, and the function does not clamp it. A negative kappa is information,
  and clamping to zero would hide the case where two raters are anti-correlated.
- No rounding anywhere. The demo formats to two places for display; the function returns the number.

```text
two raters, 20 conversations, two rubric lines
chance correction: on

  escalated_before_write
    raw agreement      0.90  (18 of 20 items)
    chance agreement   0.90
    kappa              -0.05
    verdict            THIS LINE MEASURES ALMOST NOTHING

  told_the_customer

    chance agreement   0.50
    kappa              0.80
    verdict            keep the line

  one of the two lines is agreement that chance already explains

```

**Read the two `raw agreement` rows: both 0.90, both 18 of 20.** A report that stopped at that column
would say the two lines are equally well understood by the raters.

The `chance agreement` row is where they separate. On `escalated_before_write` the desk almost always
escalates, so both raters say yes nineteen times out of twenty and would have matched 0.90 of the time
by accident — leaving **nothing** for the correction to credit them with, and a hair less than nothing
because their two disagreements fell on different items. On `told_the_customer` the desk is genuinely
inconsistent, the marginals are near even, chance agreement is 0.50, and the same 0.90 raw figure is
now carrying real information.

Same percentage. One line worth keeping, one line that is measuring the base rate.

## When it breaks

Kappa has a well-known behaviour that looks like a bug and is not, and anybody using it will meet it
in the first week: **high agreement with a low kappa, on data that is genuinely skewed.**

That is exactly the first row above, and there are two different situations that produce it:

- the raters are not really reading the line — the case this part is about;
- the raters are reading it perfectly and the failure is genuinely rare, so there is almost no
  variation for kappa to work with.

Kappa cannot tell those apart, and treating a low kappa as automatic evidence of a bad line will make
you delete rubric lines about rare failures — which are the ones you most want. The honest reading is
that a low kappa on a skewed line means **you cannot conclude anything from this data**, and the fix
is more of the rare cases rather than a different statistic.

There is a second limit worth naming. Kappa is between two raters. With three or more you need a
different coefficient, and quoting a pairwise kappa averaged over pairs is a common and slightly wrong
thing to do.

The smallest practical fix for the skew problem is to **stratify**: compute agreement on the failing
cases separately. If two raters agree on eighteen of twenty overall but on one of the two conversations
where the desk actually did not escalate, that is the number to put in front of people, and it needs no
coefficient at all.

## In production

**What a professional reports.** Kappa **and** the raw agreement **and** the base rate — all three,
because each answers a question the others cannot. Raw agreement says how often they matched; the base
rate says how surprising that is; kappa combines them. Reporting only the third is nearly as
misleading as reporting only the first, because a reader who does not know the base rate cannot tell a
genuinely rare event from a badly written line.

**What degrades at scale.** Rubric lines get added for rare failures, which is right, and every one of
them arrives with a base rate near 1.0 and therefore an uninterpretable kappa. Over time the suite
accumulates lines whose agreement statistics say nothing, and the temptation is to drop the statistic
rather than to collect the cases. The working answer is a small, deliberately **balanced** sample —
equal numbers of conversations that pass and fail each line — kept separately for measuring the raters
rather than the desk.

**The failure that only shows with real traffic.** Base rates move. A line whose failure rate was 5%
when it was written drifts to 30% after a regression, and its kappa jumps — not because the raters
improved but because the data got more interesting. An agreement number quoted without the date and
the base rate it was computed on is not comparable with itself six months later.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Do not put raw agreement on the slide without the
base rate next to it. And when you report kappa for the escalation line, say how many of the twenty
conversations actually failed it — if the answer is one, then no coefficient is going to rescue that
sample and what we need is more of those conversations, not more statistics."*

**The interview question:** *"Two annotators agree 95% of the time. Is your labelling reliable?"* The
answer that shows experience asks for the class balance first, and then names the correction —
because 95% agreement on a task where 95% of items are one class is exactly what you would get from two
people who never looked. A candidate who says "yes, that's high" has not labelled anything rare.

# 1.3 — Levels are a routing decision

## One-line answer

A level is not an opinion about how bad something is — it is the answer to *who should find out*, and
at the default `INFO` threshold five of six happenings are written and the sixth is silently
discarded.

## The story

The hotel has three ways of reaching you and staff are trained on which is which.

A note under the door is for the dry cleaning being ready. The phone in the room is for a delivery at
reception. Knocking is for the fire alarm.

None of those is a judgement about how *important* the message is — the dry cleaning might matter more
to you than the delivery. They are judgements about **how much of your attention it is allowed to
take**, and the training is what stops somebody knocking at 3am about laundry.

## The idea in plain language

Python gives you five levels, and the temptation is to read them as a severity scale — mild, annoying,
bad, very bad, catastrophic — and then to argue about where a particular happening sits. That argument
never converges, because it is the wrong question.

The right question is: **who reads this, and what do they do about it?**

| Level | Who reads it | What it is for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `DEBUG` | you, today, with a debugger open | the whole prompt, the raw payload, the intermediate value |
| `INFO` | a dashboard, and an incident review next week | the things that happened: runs, tool calls |
| `WARNING` | a dashboard, and an alert if the *rate* moves | something degraded but the user got an answer |
| `ERROR` | an alert, now | nobody got an answer |
| `CRITICAL` | a person, on a phone, now | the system is down |

Read that table and the boundaries stop being arguable. *Did the user get an answer?* separates
`WARNING` from `ERROR`. *Would you want this on a dashboard forever?* separates `INFO` from `DEBUG`.
*Would you get out of bed?* separates `ERROR` from `CRITICAL`.

Two consequences follow, and the second is the one people miss.

**A threshold is a filter, and the filtering is the point.** `logger.setLevel(logging.INFO)` means
`DEBUG` records are created and then dropped. That is not waste — it is how you can leave the
expensive `DEBUG` line in the code permanently and pay for it only when you turn it on.

**And the level does not change what happened.** A `tool_error` logged at `DEBUG` is the same failure
as one logged at `ERROR`. What changed is whether anyone will ever see it. Choosing a level too low is
not a stylistic choice; it is a decision to hide something, made in a place that does not look like a
decision.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because Day 21 gave Sutra three distinct outcomes and they must not share a level.

A *substituted* failure — the knowledge base timed out and the user got a degraded answer — is a
`WARNING`. The user was served; the dependency is unwell; the number to watch is the rate. An
*escalated* failure is an `ERROR`, because nobody got an answer. And quota exhaustion is `CRITICAL`,
because Sutra is down until midnight and no amount of retrying changes that
([21.3.1](../../../day-21-errors-surface-not-swallow/parts/03-policy/3.1-which-429-is-it.md)).

Collapse those three into one level and you have thrown away the routing that Day 21's whole policy
existed to produce. Day 24's budget alarms and Day 84's tracing both key on that distinction.

There is also a `DEBUG` line Sutra genuinely needs and must not ship on: the assembled prompt.
[Day 19](../../../day-19-context-engineering-selection/LESSON.md) measured requests at 6kB and more,
and having the exact prompt is the difference between debugging
[20.4.2](../../../day-20-context-engineering-compaction/parts/04-failure-lab/4.2-the-fact-that-was-true-and-is-now-gone.md)
in minutes and guessing. At `DEBUG`, that line costs nothing until you need it.

## The mechanism

Six real happenings, one threshold, and a count of what survives it.

```python
# days/day-22-structured-logging/lab/levels.py
"""A level is not an opinion about how bad something is. It is a routing decision."""

import logging
import os

THRESHOLD = os.environ.get("THRESHOLD", "INFO")

HAPPENINGS = [
    (logging.DEBUG, "prompt_assembled", "the whole request, 6kB of it"),
    (logging.INFO, "run_start", "one per invocation"),
    (logging.INFO, "tool_call", "one per tool call"),
    (logging.WARNING, "tool_error", "a dependency failed; we substituted"),
    (logging.ERROR, "run_failed", "nobody got an answer"),
    (logging.CRITICAL, "quota_exhausted", "Sutra is down until midnight"),
]

WHO_READS = {
    logging.DEBUG: "you, today, with a debugger open",
    logging.INFO: "a dashboard, and an incident review next week",
    logging.WARNING: "a dashboard, and an alert if the rate moves",
    logging.ERROR: "an alert, now",
    logging.CRITICAL: "a person, on a phone, now",
}

level = logging.getLevelNamesMapping()[THRESHOLD]
print(f"THRESHOLD={THRESHOLD} (numeric {level})\n")
print(f"  {'level':<9} {'event':<18} {'kept?':<7} {'who reads it'}")
for lvl, event, _why in HAPPENINGS:
    kept = "yes" if lvl >= level else "no"
    print(f"  {logging.getLevelName(lvl):<9} {event:<18} {kept:<7} {WHO_READS[lvl]}")

kept = sum(1 for lvl, _, _ in HAPPENINGS if lvl >= level)
print(f"\n  {kept} of {len(HAPPENINGS)} happenings written at this threshold.")
print("  the level did not change what happened. it changed who can find out.")
```

**Line by line:**

- `HAPPENINGS` is Sutra's actual set, not an abstract example. Every one of the six is a line the
  system will emit by the end of this day, and the two `INFO` entries are deliberately adjacent to
  show that a level is per-*kind*, not per-line.
- `(logging.DEBUG, "prompt_assembled", "the whole request, 6kB of it")` is the entry that justifies
  the whole idea. It is the most useful line in the system when you need it and the most expensive
  when you do not, which is precisely what `DEBUG` is for.
- `WHO_READS` maps level → **audience**, not level → severity word. That mapping is the part worth
  copying into your own project; the argument about whether something is "a warning" evaporates once
  the question is "does this page someone".
- `logging.getLevelNamesMapping()` is the 3.11+ way to turn `"INFO"` into `20`. The older
  `logging.getLevelName("INFO")` also works but is documented as returning either direction depending
  on input, which is the kind of API you do not want in a config path.
- `lvl >= level` is the entire filter, and it is worth seeing written out: levels are integers, the
  threshold is an integer, and "keep it" is one comparison. `DEBUG` is 10 and `CRITICAL` is 50.
- `_why` is captured and unused, prefixed with an underscore to say so. It documents each entry for a
  reader of the file, which is a legitimate reason for a variable to exist.
- The final line is the claim, and it is deliberately about what the level does *not* do.

```bash
cd days/day-22-structured-logging/lab
THRESHOLD=INFO uv run python levels.py
THRESHOLD=DEBUG uv run python levels.py
```

**Line by line:**

- Two runs, one environment variable — the ablation is the threshold itself.
- Pure Python: no ADK, no key, no quota, and nothing is actually written.

Measured on 2026-09-03:

```text
THRESHOLD=INFO (numeric 20)

  level     event              kept?   who reads it
  DEBUG     prompt_assembled   no      you, today, with a debugger open
  INFO      run_start          yes     a dashboard, and an incident review next week
  INFO      tool_call          yes     a dashboard, and an incident review next week
  WARNING   tool_error         yes     a dashboard, and an alert if the rate moves
  ERROR     run_failed         yes     an alert, now
  CRITICAL  quota_exhausted    yes     a person, on a phone, now

  5 of 6 happenings written at this threshold.
  the level did not change what happened. it changed who can find out.
```

**Five of six**, and the one dropped is the 6kB prompt. That is the design working: the expensive line
is in the code, permanently, costing nothing, and one environment variable brings it back when an
incident needs it.

At `THRESHOLD=DEBUG` the count is **6 of 6** — same code, same happenings, one more line per run and
about 6kB more per invocation. Nothing about the system changed except who can find out.

**And the `who reads it` column is the useful half of the table.** Two of the six rows go to a
dashboard, one to an alert, one to a phone, and one to a person with a debugger. Five happenings,
four different audiences — which is why collapsing them all to `INFO`, the most common mistake, throws
away four routing decisions at once.

## When it breaks

A level set in the wrong place produces no error, no output, and a **debugging check that lies**. The
trap is that a level lives in *two* places — on the logger and on each handler — and a record has to
clear both. Measured on 2026-09-03:

```text
logger level : DEBUG
handler level: ERROR
lines written: 1
contents     : ERROR run_failed

log.isEnabledFor(WARNING): True
```

The logger was set to `DEBUG`, so it kept everything. The handler was set to `ERROR`, so it wrote one
line of the two. `log.warning("tool_error")` vanished.

**And `log.isEnabledFor(logging.WARNING)` returned `True`.** That is the line to remember. It is the
obvious thing to check when a log line is missing, it consults only the **logger**, and it will
cheerfully tell you that warnings are enabled while every warning is being discarded one layer
further on. The check that would have answered the question is
`handler.level`, and nothing prompts you to look there.

The version that reaches production is a `handler.setLevel(...)` copied from a snippet, or a
`logger.setLevel(logging.WARNING)` added during a noisy afternoon and never reverted. Every `INFO`
line in the system stops being written, the dashboard goes quiet, and quiet reads as healthy.

## In production

**The threshold belongs in configuration, not in code.** One environment variable —
`SUTRA_LOG_LEVEL=DEBUG` — read once at startup. That is what makes `DEBUG` lines worth writing: they
are one restart away rather than one deploy away.

**What changes at scale** is that `DEBUG` becomes unaffordable everywhere and essential somewhere. The
mature version is per-logger levels: `DEBUG` for `sutra.context` while you are debugging prompts,
`INFO` for everything else. The logger hierarchy is what makes that possible, and it is the same
hierarchy [2.3](../02-wiring-the-run/2.3-adk-logs-too.md) uses to turn ADK's own logging down.

**The failure that only appears with real traffic** is the `WARNING` that fires constantly. A
substituted failure is a `WARNING` because the *rate* is the signal — and if a dependency is
permanently unwell, the level is correct and the alert is useless. That is not a level problem, it is
[4.1](../04-in-production/4.1-what-to-log-and-what-to-count.md)'s: you needed a counter and a
threshold on its derivative, not a line.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"this logs the substituted-failure case at `INFO`.
The user got a degraded answer — that's a `WARNING`, because we want the rate on a dashboard. `INFO`
is for things that are working."*

**The interview question:** *"how do you decide what level to log something at?"* The answer that
shows you have owned a pager: *"by who reads it, not by how bad it feels. `ERROR` means nobody got an
answer and somebody should be alerted. `WARNING` means it degraded but we served the user, so what
matters is the rate. `INFO` is what happened, for a dashboard and for incident reviews, and `DEBUG` is
the expensive stuff you leave in the code and turn on when you need it."*

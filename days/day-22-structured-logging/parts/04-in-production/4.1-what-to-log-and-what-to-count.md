# 4.1 — What to log and what to count

## One-line answer

A log answers *what happened to this one* and a counter answers *how often* — three of five real
questions need a specific line and two need a number, and at five million runs a day the lines are
**6.15 GB** while the counters are the same size they were at twenty runs.

## The story

The bus depot keeps two completely different records and nobody confuses them.

There is a logbook per vehicle. Bus 41 went in on the ninth with a rattle from the offside rear, the
fitter replaced a bracket, here is the part number. You read it when something is wrong with bus 41.

And there is a board in the office with numbers on it: buses out this morning, cancellations this
week, average age of the fleet. Nobody reads a logbook to fill in that board, and nobody consults the
board to find out what was wrong with bus 41.

The board is small and it stays small when the fleet doubles. The logbooks are a shelf, and the shelf
grows.

## The idea in plain language

Everything today has been about logs, and this part is the boundary: **there is a class of question a
log is the wrong tool for**, and the wrong tool in this case is also the expensive one.

| | a log line | a counter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| answers | *what happened to **this** request* | *how many / how often, across all of them* |
| one per | happening | metric name (plus labels) |
| size at 20 runs/day | 120 lines | 4 numbers |
| size at 5,000,000 runs/day | 30,000,000 lines | 4 numbers |
| retained for | days to weeks | months to years, cheaply |
| good at | *why did ticket 4521 get closed?* | *is the error rate rising?* |

The asymmetry in rows three and four is the whole argument. A counter is a fixed cost: `tool_errors`
is one number whether it was incremented ten times or ten million. A log line is a **per-event** cost,
so its total grows with traffic exactly as fast as your traffic does.

That does not make logs bad. It makes them the right answer to a specific kind of question — the
**particular** one — and the wrong answer to aggregates. Reaching for a log to answer *"is the error
rate rising?"* means scanning thirty million lines to produce a number you could have had for free,
and the reason people do it anyway is that the log is already there.

The practical rule, and it is the one to take away: **log the particular, count the aggregate.** If
the question has the word *"which"* or *"why"* in it, it wants a line. If it has *"how many"*, *"how
often"* or *"is it rising"*, it wants a number.

There is a related trap worth naming now because Day 84 will meet it properly. A counter can carry
**labels** — `tool_errors{tool="lookup_article", kind="TimeoutError"}` — and labels are what make a
counter useful. But every distinct combination of label values is a separate series to store, so a
label with unbounded values (a user id, an invocation id, a ticket number) turns a fixed-cost counter
into a per-event cost with extra steps. Ids belong on log lines and never in labels.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because Day 24 is token accounting and it is the first thing Sutra needs a counter for.

*"How many requests have we made to `gemini-3.7-flash` today?"* is an aggregate over a day, checked
before every call, against a limit of twenty. Answering it by reading and parsing a log file on every
model call would be absurd, and answering it from a number that is incremented once per call is
trivial. Day 70's quota router does the same at a larger scale, per provider, per window.

And this part decides Sutra's log volume from now on.
[2.1](../02-wiring-the-run/2.1-one-line-per-happening.md) put six lines per run into the file, and
five of the six come from `on_event_callback`. That was the right call for a day about records and it
is the number that would dominate the arithmetic below at any real scale — which is why the honest
production answer is events at `DEBUG`, run boundaries at `INFO`, and counters for everything you plan
to graph.

## The mechanism

Sutra's own measured line count per run, projected across four scales, and five real questions sorted
by which tool answers them.

```python
# days/day-22-structured-logging/lab/what_to_count.py
"""Logs answer 'what happened to this one'. Counters answer 'how often'. Sizing decides which."""

LINES_PER_RUN = 6  # measured from log_the_run.py
BYTES_PER_LINE = 205  # measured: 1235 bytes / 6 lines, rounded

SCALES = (
    ("Sutra today, free tier", 20),
    ("a small internal desk", 2_000),
    ("one support team", 50_000),
    ("a real product", 5_000_000),
)

QUESTIONS = (
    ("why did ticket 4521 get closed?", "log"),
    ("how many tool errors this hour?", "counter"),
    ("which conversation did priya have at 14:22?", "log"),
    ("is the kb error rate rising?", "counter"),
    ("what exactly did we send the model?", "log"),
)


def volume(runs: int) -> tuple[int, float]:
    """Log lines and gigabytes per day at this many runs."""
    lines = runs * LINES_PER_RUN
    return lines, lines * BYTES_PER_LINE / 1_000_000_000


print(f"{'scale':<26} {'runs/day':>10} {'log lines/day':>14} {'GB/day':>9}")
for label, runs in SCALES:
    lines, gb = volume(runs)
    print(f"{label:<26} {runs:>10,} {lines:>14,} {gb:>9.2f}")

print(f"\n{'question':<45} {'answer from'}")
for question, source in QUESTIONS:
    print(f"{question:<45} {source}")

logs = sum(1 for _, source in QUESTIONS if source == "log")
print(f"\n{logs} of {len(QUESTIONS)} questions need a specific line; the rest need a number.")
print("a counter costs the same at every scale in the table. a log line does not.")
```

**Line by line:**

- `LINES_PER_RUN = 6` and `BYTES_PER_LINE = 205` are **measured, not assumed**: six lines and 1,235
  bytes came out of [2.1](../02-wiring-the-run/2.1-one-line-per-happening.md)'s run. Anchoring a
  projection to a real observation is the difference between arithmetic and hand-waving.
- `SCALES` starts at Sutra's actual twenty runs a day. Including the smallest case matters, because it
  is where the honest answer is *"none of this is a problem yet"* — and knowing that is as useful as
  knowing where it becomes one.
- `("a real product", 5_000_000)` is the top of the range rather than a billion, because five million
  requests a day is a large but ordinary internal system and the number should be one a reader can
  believe.
- `QUESTIONS` pairs each question with the tool that answers it, and the wording is chosen so the rule
  is visible: *why*, *which*, *what exactly* → log. *How many*, *is it rising* → counter.
- `"what exactly did we send the model?"` is the one that needs a `DEBUG` line
  ([1.3](../01-a-line-you-can-query/1.3-levels-are-routing.md)) and never a counter. There is no number
  that summarises a prompt.
- `lines * BYTES_PER_LINE / 1_000_000_000` uses decimal gigabytes rather than binary, because that is
  what a storage bill is quoted in.
- The final `print` states the asymmetry rather than the total, because the total is not the point —
  the *slope* is.

**Sutra today: 120 lines and 0.00 GB.** The correct engineering response to that row is *do nothing*.
Six lines per run at twenty runs a day is free, and optimising it would be the purest possible waste
of an afternoon. This is worth saying out loud in a curriculum that is about to spend a page on
volume: the arithmetic exists to tell you *when* to care, and the answer for Sutra is not yet.

**One support team: 300,000 lines and 60 megabytes a day.** Still nothing, and this is the scale most
internal tools live at forever. A `RotatingFileHandler`
([4.2](4.2-a-log-file-only-grows.md)) and no further thought.

**A real product: 30 million lines and 6.15 GB a day.** Now it is a decision. That is roughly two
terabytes a year, on a log platform that charges by ingest, for a file whose main purpose is to answer
questions about individual requests — most of which are never asked. At this scale the events go to
`DEBUG`, the run boundaries stay at `INFO`, and everything you wanted to graph is a counter.

**And three of five questions still need the line.** That is the row that stops this being an argument
for logging less. The majority of the questions an operator actually asks are *particular*, and no
number answers them. The two that are aggregates are the two that would have cost you thirty million
lines to answer badly.

## When it breaks

The failure is a dashboard built on log queries, and it breaks in a way that is hard to attribute:
the *"is the error rate rising?"* panel starts timing out on the day traffic doubles, on a log
platform that charges by bytes scanned. The exact wording depends on the platform — a scan limit, a
query timeout, a bill — and this document has not run one, so no message is quoted here rather than
inventing one. Nothing is wrong with the log. The question was always an aggregate, and it
was being answered by scanning particulars — which worked at 60 MB a day and stopped working at 6 GB.

The version that costs money rather than time is the same query on a schedule. A panel that refreshes
every minute, scanning a day's logs, is 1,440 full scans a day of a growing file, and it appears on the
bill as observability spend rather than as a design mistake.

And the cardinality trap has its own signature, on the metrics side rather than the logs side: a
counter labelled with `invocation_id` creates one series per request, which is a log line wearing a
metric's costume and is the most expensive way anyone has yet found to store one integer.

## In production

**Emit both, from the same place.** The hook that writes the `tool_error` line is the hook that
increments the `tool_errors` counter — two statements, one place, no chance of the count and the
record disagreeing. Sutra will not have a metrics backend until Day 84, so today a counter is a
dictionary in the plugin and a line at `run_end`; the shape is what matters, not the storage.

**What changes at scale** is the level of the event lines. `on_event_callback` fires several times per
invocation and is therefore the multiplier. Moving it to `DEBUG` takes six lines per run down to two
and keeps the boundaries — and if you need per-event detail during an incident, that is one
environment variable ([1.3](../01-a-line-you-can-query/1.3-levels-are-routing.md)).

**The failure that only appears with real traffic** is the log-based alert. An alert defined as a log
query is an alert whose latency and cost grow with your traffic, and which fails during exactly the
incidents that produce the most log volume. Alerts belong on counters, and the log is what you read
once the alert has fired.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"this panel counts `event=tool_error` by scanning the
log. Increment a counter in the same hook that writes the line and graph that instead — the query is
going to get slower every week."*

**The interview question:** *"when would you use a metric instead of a log?"* The answer that shows you
have paid for the wrong choice: *"logs answer questions about one request — why did this happen, what
did we send — and metrics answer aggregates. The size is the giveaway: a counter is one number at any
traffic level, and our log lines were about 6 GB a day at five million runs. And ids go on lines, never
in metric labels, because an unbounded label is a log line pretending to be a counter."*

# 1.2 — The six sections a handbook needs

## One-line answer

An instruction that works has six named sections — **role, scope, refusal script, honesty, tone and
one worked example** — and the reason there are six is that each one closes a different failure that
happens when it is missing.

---

## The story

Look at the label the chemist sticks on a strip of medicine.

It is small, it is crowded, and every line on it is there because somebody was hurt when it was
missing. *Name of the medicine.* Somebody once took the wrong strip out of the same drawer. *One
tablet.* Somebody took three, reasoning that if one helps, three helps faster. *After food.*
Somebody took it on an empty stomach and spent the night being sick. *Do not take with the blue
tablet.* Somebody did. *If you miss a dose, skip it, do not double it.* Somebody doubled it. *If the
rash spreads, stop and call.*

Six lines. None of them describes how the medicine should make you feel. Every one of them removes
one specific way a person who means well gets it wrong.

Now imagine the same strip with a label that says: **"Take responsibly. Wishing you good health."**
Nobody could call that label wrong. A pharmacist could sign it off. And it prevents nothing at all,
because it does not know what any particular person is about to do.

The difference between those two labels is not effort, and it is not word count. The first was
written by somebody who had seen the six ways this goes wrong. The second was written by somebody
imagining a good outcome.

---

## The idea in plain language

[Part 1.1](1.1-handbook-not-a-wish.md) gave you the test: if you cannot name the message that would
fail without a line, the line is not an instruction. That test tells you what to **delete**. It does
not tell you what to **write**, and a blank page is harder than an edit.

So here is the structure. Six sections, in this order, each closing a named failure:

| # | Section | What it says | The failure it closes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Role** | who you are, and for whom | the agent answers questions from a completely different job |
| 2 | **Scope** | the small, closed list of things you do | the agent quietly takes on a fourth thing nobody sized |
| 3 | **Refusal script** | the exact words for anything outside scope | the agent invents its own refusal, differently each time |
| 4 | **Honesty** | what you cannot see, and what to say instead | the agent fills a gap with something plausible |
| 5 | **Tone** | concrete adjectives, plus a length budget | answers drift between curt and essay-length |
| 6 | **One example** | a single tiny exchange, done right | the agent gets the words right and the shape wrong |

Two of those rows are worth pulling out, because they are the ones people skip.

**The refusal script is a separate section from scope on purpose.** Scope says what is *in*. A
refusal script says what comes out of the agent's mouth when something is *out*. Those are different
sentences and they fail separately. Writing "you do not discuss refunds" without a script leaves the
model to compose its own refusal, and it will compose a different one every time. Sometimes curt,
sometimes a paragraph of apology, sometimes a refusal that discusses refunds while refusing to. A
refusal is a behaviour like any other, so it needs a script like any other.

**"One example" means one.** The technical name for showing a model a worked case is **few-shot
prompting**: instead of describing the behaviour you want, you demonstrate it, and the model
continues the pattern. It is powerful, and it is expensive, because an example is text that gets
re-sent on every single turn ([1.5](1.5-every-line-is-a-tax.md)). One example that pins down the
*shape* of a good answer earns its place. Five examples are usually four people each adding one
after an incident.

The order matters too, and not for style. Role comes first because every other line is read against
it. Scope comes before refusal, because you cannot script a refusal until you know what you are
refusing. Honesty comes before tone, because a warm invented answer is worse than a blunt true one.
The example comes last, because it should demonstrate everything above it at once.

## Why Sutra needs it

Sutra's instruction is currently four sentences carried over from Day 4. Against this table it has a
partial **role**, a broken **scope** — it names a knowledge-base search that does not exist — half an
**honesty** rule, and nothing else. That is not a criticism of Day 4. That string was written for a
different runtime.

**Day 10** is where this bites. Tools arrive, and a tool the agent can actually call is a power that
needs a scope line saying when to reach for it and a refusal script for when the answer is not in
there. **Day 16** adds web search, where the honesty section is the only thing standing between a
cited answer and a confident invented one.

And **Days 79 to 83** turn each of these six sections into its own eval case. A day's worth of
structure here is what makes an evalset writable at all: six sections, six probes, six assertions
that can go red.

## The mechanism

Here is Sutra's instruction written to the six sections. Read it as six answers, not as prose.

```python
# sutra/desk/agent.py - the handbook (AG-05).
# Markdown headings because adk.dev/agents/llm-agents/ says so in as many words:
# "Use Markdown: Improve readability for complex instructions using headings, lists, etc."
# (page checked 2026-08-26). Every line below is re-sent on every model call - 1.5 is
# the accounting, and section 2 is where each of these gets probed.
INSTRUCTION = """# Role
You are Sutra, the triage assistant for a software company's support desk.
You work for the support engineers, not for the customer.

# Scope
You do exactly three things:
1. Explain what a ticket is about, from text the engineer pastes to you.
2. Suggest a likely cause, labelled clearly as a hypothesis.
3. Recommend a priority - low, normal or high - with one reason.

# Refusal
Anything else is out of scope: refunds, release dates, account changes,
company policy. When asked, say this and nothing more:
"That's outside triage. I'll flag it for a human colleague."

# Honesty
You cannot look anything up. You have no ticket database, no knowledge
base and no search. If asked about a ticket by number, say you cannot
read it and ask for the text. Never invent ticket contents, customer
names, article titles or dates. "I don't know" is a complete answer.

# Tone
Plain words, no filler, at most three sentences unless asked for detail.
Say "probably" when you mean probably.

# Example
Engineer: Ticket 4521 came in, what do you think?
Sutra: I can't read tickets yet - I have no lookup tools. Paste the text
and I'll triage it.
"""
```

**Line by line:**

- `"""# Role` — a **triple-quoted string**, so the newlines are real newlines. The model sees the
  layout you see, and Markdown headings are what the official guidance asks for.
- `You work for the support engineers, not for the customer.` — the second half of the role, and the
  half people forget. **Who the agent is for decides every ambiguous case later.** An engineer wants
  a blunt hypothesis, a customer wants reassurance, and an agent that has not been told which one it
  is talking to will average the two.
- `You do exactly three things:` — the word **exactly** and the numbered list are doing the work. A
  closed list can be checked against; "you help with tickets" cannot. This is the line that makes a
  fourth request feel wrong to the model rather than merely unfamiliar.
- `labelled clearly as a hypothesis` — an honesty rule living inside scope. It belongs here rather
  than under `# Honesty` because it constrains a thing the agent *does*, not a thing it *cannot see*.
- `say this and nothing more:` — **nothing more** is load-bearing. Without it the model treats the
  script as an opening line and continues past it, which is how a refusal turns into a paragraph
  explaining the refusal.
- `"That's outside triage. I'll flag it for a human colleague."` — the script itself, in quotation
  marks so it reads as words to say rather than a policy to paraphrase. It also **hands the problem
  somewhere**, which is what makes it acceptable to a real user rather than a wall.
- `You have no ticket database, no knowledge base and no search.` — the fix for the bug part 1.1
  found. The old string told the agent to search something that does not exist. This states the
  equipment honestly, and **stating an absence is itself an instruction**, because the model's
  default assumption is that it can do whatever it is asked.
- `Never invent ticket contents, customer names, article titles or dates.` — a list, not the word
  "anything". Naming the four things it is most tempted to invent is more probeable than a general
  ban, and each of the four becomes its own eval case later.
- `at most three sentences unless asked for detail` — the **length budget**. A number is what makes
  tone testable. "Professional and warm" cannot fail a check; a four-sentence answer to a one-line
  question can.
- `Say "probably" when you mean probably.` — a **hedging instruction**, and one of the highest-value
  lines in the whole handbook. Models state guesses in the same confident register as facts, and
  this is the cheapest available correction.
- `Engineer: ... Sutra: ...` — the single example, and notice **what it demonstrates**: the refusal
  shape, the honesty rule and the length budget, all in one exchange. An example that only showed the
  happy path would have taught the least interesting case.
- The whole thing is still short. Six sections is a structure, not a word count, and a handbook that
  reaches forty lines has usually gained lucky charms rather than coverage.

## When it breaks

**💥 The section that was written but never scoped.** A handbook with all six headings still fails if
section 2 is open-ended. This one has all six:

```text
# Scope
You help support engineers with their tickets and related questions.
```

"And related questions" is an open door with a heading over it. Ask about the invoice attached to a
ticket, or the customer's contract, or the release that caused the bug, and every one of those is
*related to a ticket*. The structure was followed and nothing was decided. **A scope section is only
a scope section if something is outside it.**

**💥 The refusal that leaks the reason.** This one is subtle, and it happens to careful people:

```text
# Refusal
If asked about refunds, explain that refund decisions require reviewing
the customer's payment history and account standing, which you cannot
access, and offer to escalate.
```

Every word of that is true, and it teaches the user that a refund depends on payment history and
account standing — which may be internal, may be wrong, and was certainly not reviewed by anybody
before it was written into a prompt. **A refusal script that explains itself is a disclosure
channel.** Give the words to say, not the reasoning behind them.

**💥 The example that fought the rules.** The most expensive failure in this list, because it is
invisible on review. Suppose the tone section says three sentences, and the example shows a
five-sentence answer because the author wanted it to look thorough. The model has been told one thing
and shown another, and **it follows what it was shown.** Demonstration beats description, so an
example that disagrees with the rules above it silently deletes them. Whenever you edit a rule,
re-read the example against it.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead** is the same six sections, plus a version marker and an owner,
in a file of their own rather than a Python string literal — usually `prompts/triage.md`, loaded at
import, so that changing behaviour shows up in a diff a reviewer can read line by line and a blame
can attribute. The six headings then become the pull-request template: *which section did you touch,
and which probe covers it?*

**What degrades at scale is section 2.** Scope is the section that grows, because every new
capability is a legitimate reason to add a line and no capability is ever a reason to remove one. The
instruction that started as three numbered things is doing eleven a year later, of which four are
never exercised and two contradict each other
([1.4](1.4-contradictions-are-randomised-behaviour.md)). The teams that survive this treat the
numbered list as a fixed-size budget: **a fourth thing may be added when a third thing is removed, or
when somebody agrees to split the agent in two.**

**The failure that only shows with real traffic** is the sixth section. One example teaches shape, and
shape is exactly what generalises wrongly. If the example shows a ticket about a login bug, the
answers to *every* ticket start sounding like login-bug answers: same rhythm, same three-sentence
structure, sometimes the same suggested cause. Nobody sees this in testing, because testing uses the
example's own case. It shows up as a support engineer saying "it always says the same thing", which
sounds like a vague complaint and is actually a precise bug report about section 6.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Section 3 explains the refusal instead of scripting
it, so we're teaching users our escalation criteria on every out-of-scope question. Give it the
sentence to say. Also your example is five sentences and your tone rule says three — the model will
do five."*

**The interview question:** *"walk me through the structure of a system prompt you've shipped."* The
answer that shows you have done it: "Six sections in a fixed order — role, scope, refusal script,
honesty, tone, one example. The two people underrate are the refusal script and the honesty section.
A refusal without a script means the model writes its own, differently every time, and that variation
is what users read as the agent being unreliable. The honesty section has to state what the agent
*cannot* see, not just tell it to be truthful, because the model's default assumption is that it can
do whatever it's asked and it will close the gap for you. I keep it to one example on purpose, and I
re-check the example every time a rule changes, because a demonstration overrides a description and
that's the one conflict nobody catches in review."

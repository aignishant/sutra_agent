# 1.1 — An instruction is a handbook, not a wish

## One-line answer

An instruction is a **specification of behaviour you can check**, not a description of the mood you
hope for, and the test that separates the two is whether you can name the message that would fail if
the line were deleted.

---

## The story

A teacher is away, and somebody else is sent to take the class.

The first time this happens, the note left on the desk says: *"Please keep them busy and be nice to
them."* The substitute is nice. They are also guessing. They let the class talk at the start because
that felt kind, then panic and set a test that was never planned, then promise the class that the
homework is cancelled, because a child asked and saying yes felt nicer than saying no. Every single
decision was made in good faith. Three of them cause trouble the next week, and one of them the
regular teacher has to publicly reverse.

The second time, the note is one page. *You are covering Class 7B. Do pages 14 to 17 of the workbook.
They have done up to 13. Do not set new homework and do not cancel any; if they ask, say the class
teacher decides homework and you will pass the question on. If a child is unwell, send them to the
office with a partner, never alone. Keep the noise down, but you do not have to keep them silent.*

The same substitute. The same class. This time nothing has to be reversed.

Notice what changed and what did not. Nobody made the substitute a better teacher. Nobody gave them
more experience or more authority. They were handed a page that turned "be nice" into a small number
of decisions that had already been made by somebody who knew the class. **The page did not describe a
good outcome. It removed the guesses.**

---

## The idea in plain language

On [Day 5, part 2.3](../../../day-05-first-adk-agent/parts/02-the-agent-object/2.3-the-instruction-is-your-system-prompt.md)
you moved a string called `SYSTEM` into a field called `instruction`, and nothing about it changed.
That part answered *what the field is*: text sent to the model on every turn, addressed to the model,
telling it how to behave.

This part answers a different question: **what should be written in it.**

The model is the substitute. It is enormously capable, and it arrives knowing nothing about your
company, your customers, your policies, or what your support desk is not allowed to promise. It has
no memory of yesterday's conversation. Every turn, it is the first morning again. The instruction is
the page on the desk.

There are two ways to write that page:

| | A wish | A handbook |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it says | "Be helpful and answer tickets well" | "You do exactly three things. Here they are. Here is what you say when asked for a fourth." |
| What it describes | the outcome you want | the decisions you have already made |
| How you know it worked | you read an answer and it felt fine | you send a message designed to break one rule, and watch |
| What happens when it fails | you rewrite the whole thing and hope | you find the line that did not hold, and fix that line |

A **wish** is a sentence about how you would like the answers to feel. A **handbook** is a set of
decisions taken in advance, written down so that somebody who was not in the room can act on them.

The practical difference is testability. "Be helpful" cannot fail a test, because there is no message
you could send whose answer would prove it had been ignored. "If asked to promise a refund, say that
refunds are decided by a human colleague and that you will flag it" can fail a test, because there is
an obvious message to send: *ask it to promise a refund.*

That gives you the rule this whole day runs on, and it is worth memorising in this shape:

> **If you cannot name the message that would fail without a line, the line is not an instruction. It
> is a lucky charm.**

A lucky charm is not harmless. It is re-sent to the model on every single turn, forever
([1.5](1.5-every-line-is-a-tax.md)). It takes up room the model's attention has to cross. And worst
of all it makes the instruction *look* thorough, so the next person to read it assumes a case is
covered when nothing covers it.

One term to define before it appears anywhere else today, because it is used loosely everywhere and
precisely here. A **persona** is not a personality. It is the *observable behaviour* the instruction
produces: what the agent does, what it refuses, and how it sounds. You do not admire a persona. You
send it messages and see what comes back. [Section 2](../02-testing-a-persona/2.1-a-line-you-cannot-probe.md)
is entirely about that.

## Why Sutra needs it

**Day 10** gives Sutra its first real tools inside ADK, and the moment a tool exists the instruction
has to say when to reach for it and when not to. A wish cannot say that. **Day 16** adds search, and
the difference between an agent that cites what it found and one that fills the gaps with plausible
sentences is a handbook line that was written to be probed.

The load-bearing one is **Days 79 to 83**, the evaluation phase. An evalset is a file of messages
with expected behaviours, run automatically, that goes red when the agent changes. Every case in it
is a probe, and every probe is the test of one handbook line. **A persona written as wishes cannot be
turned into an evalset**, because there is nothing to assert. If today's instruction is written
badly, Phase 12 has nothing to grade, and Sutra's behaviour becomes something you can only argue
about.

The nearest one is tomorrow. **Day 7** reads events, and the instruction is what rides inside the
request that produces them.

## The paper behind it

Writing a page of rules only works because somebody trained the model to follow pages of rules. That
is not obvious, it was not always true, and it has a citation.

> **Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback**
> arXiv:2203.02155 · 2022 · <https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155>

Its claim in one sentence: making a language model bigger does not make it better at doing what you
asked, and fine-tuning it on human demonstrations and human preferences does, so instruction
following is a **trained behaviour rather than a property of scale**.

Read it after the parts: [papers/01-instructgpt.md](../../papers/01-instructgpt.md).

## The mechanism

Sutra's current instruction is the one carried across from Day 4. Read it as a handbook, and see how
much of it is decisions and how much is hope:

```python
# sutra/desk/agent.py, as Day 5 left it
INSTRUCTION = (
    "You are Sutra, a support-ticket triage assistant. "
    "Read the ticket before diagnosing it, then search the knowledge base using "
    "the symptom words from the ticket body - not the user's phrasing. "
    "Never state a fact that no tool result gave you; if a lookup found nothing, "
    "say so plainly rather than filling the gap."
)
```

**Line by line:**

- `"You are Sutra, a support-ticket triage assistant. "` — this is a **role**, and it is doing real
  work even though it looks like scene-setting. It is the line that makes "what is your refund
  policy?" feel out of place to the model, rather than like an ordinary question.
- `"Read the ticket before diagnosing it, then search the knowledge base using the symptom words from
  the ticket body - not the user's phrasing."` — this is an **order of operations**, and it is
  probeable: paste a ticket where the user's phrasing and the symptom words differ, and see which one
  the agent searches with. Note that it names a tool the agent does not have. Day 4 wrote it for a
  loop that did have one; today it describes equipment that is not in the room.
- `"Never state a fact that no tool result gave you"` — the **honesty rule**, and the most probeable
  line in the string. Ask about a ticket by number, and see whether an invented summary comes back.
  This is Principle 10 written as behaviour rather than as a rule for you.
- `"if a lookup found nothing, say so plainly rather than filling the gap"` — the **refusal script**,
  in its smallest possible form. It does not merely forbid the bad answer, it supplies the good one.
  A rule that forbids without supplying leaves the model to invent a replacement, and it will.
- What is **missing** matters as much as what is there: no scope (what is Sutra *not* for?), no tone,
  no length budget, and no statement of what it currently cannot see. Parts
  [1.2](1.2-six-sections-of-a-handbook.md) and [1.3](1.3-protocol-does-not-belong-in-prose.md) are
  about those gaps.

Now the part that makes this a mechanism rather than an opinion: **you can see the exact text the
model is given, without spending a single request.** ADK assembles the instruction into the request
in a step that runs before the model is called, so you can run that step yourself.

```python
# lab/show_instruction.py - read the request, call nothing
import asyncio

from google.adk.agents.invocation_context import InvocationContext
from google.adk.flows.llm_flows.instructions import _build_instructions
from google.adk.models.llm_request import LlmRequest
from google.adk.sessions import InMemorySessionService

from sutra.desk.agent import root_agent


async def main() -> None:
    service = InMemorySessionService()
    session = await service.create_session(app_name="sutra", user_id="local")
    context = InvocationContext(
        session_service=service,
        invocation_id="probe",
        agent=root_agent,
        session=session,
    )
    request = LlmRequest()
    await _build_instructions(context, request)
    print(request.config.system_instruction)


asyncio.run(main())
```

**Line by line:**

- `from google.adk.flows.llm_flows.instructions import _build_instructions` — the leading underscore
  says this is ADK's internal function and not a promised interface. Importing it is a **deliberate
  read-only trade**: this is a lab script whose only job is to show you the truth, and reading the
  installed source is the habit [Day 5, part 4.3](../../../day-05-first-adk-agent/parts/04-the-runner/4.3-read-the-signature-not-the-tutorial.md)
  built. Nothing under `sutra/` may import it, because a private name can be renamed in a patch
  release without warning.
- `InvocationContext(...)` — the object ADK carries through one turn. It is built here by hand
  because there is no turn: no runner, no user message, and therefore no model call and no quota
  spent.
- `invocation_id="probe"` — normally a generated id that ties every event of one turn together. Here
  it is a constant, because nothing will ever look it up.
- `LlmRequest()` — an empty request. `_build_instructions` fills it, exactly as it does on a real
  turn.
- `request.config.system_instruction` — **the field the instruction lands in**, which is the subject
  of [3.1](../03-the-instruction-fields/3.1-where-your-instruction-lands.md), and which turns out not
  to be the only possible destination.
- The whole script costs **zero requests**. That matters more than it sounds. The free tier is 20
  requests per day (`docs/PACKAGES.md`, 2026-08-25), and a day about instructions could burn all of
  them on curiosity alone. Almost everything today is arranged to be free like this.

Run it, and you will see the four sentences above printed exactly as the model receives them. That is
the page on the desk.

## When it breaks

**💥 The wish that passed every review:**

```text
INSTRUCTION = "You are a helpful, professional support assistant. Always be accurate,
concise and friendly, and do your best to resolve the customer's issue."
```

Nothing in that sentence is wrong. It reads well, it would survive code review, and it is completely
empty. Ask three questions of it and the emptiness becomes visible:

- What message would prove that "accurate" was ignored? There is none. A confident wrong answer
  sounds accurate, and the line does not say what to do when the agent cannot check something.
- What does "resolve the customer's issue" authorise? Read literally, promising a refund resolves the
  issue. The line does not forbid it, so on some turns the model will.
- What does "concise" mean against "do your best to resolve"? On a hard ticket those pull in opposite
  directions, and **which one the model drops changes from turn to turn**
  ([1.4](1.4-contradictions-are-randomised-behaviour.md)).

The failure this produces is the worst kind, because it has no error message. The agent works. It is
polite. And every so often it says something the business has to walk back, with nothing in the logs
that looks like a bug.

**💥 The rule that forbade without supplying:**

```text
"Never discuss refunds."
```

Send *"can I get a refund for ticket 4521?"* and the model must say something. The rule removed one
answer and offered no replacement, so the model writes its own, and what it writes varies. Sometimes
it is a flat "I cannot discuss that", which reads as rude. Sometimes it explains at length why it
cannot, which is worse. Sometimes it discusses refunds while noting that it should not.

The fix is one clause: *"Never promise or estimate a refund. If asked, say that refunds are decided by
a human colleague and that you will flag the ticket for one."* **A refusal is a behaviour, so it needs
a script rather than a prohibition.**

**💥 The line that named equipment the agent does not have.** This one is live in Sutra right now. The
instruction says *"then search the knowledge base"*, and today's agent has no tools at all. Day 5
constructed `LlmAgent` without a `tools=` argument, and Day 10 is where tools arrive. So the model is
told to use something that does not exist, and when a user asks about a ticket, the most cooperative
thing it can do is *describe having searched*. You have instructed it to invent.

This is worth sitting with, because it is the exact mechanism behind most "the agent hallucinated"
reports: **the instruction promised a capability the runtime did not provide, and the model closed the
gap.** Nobody wrote "make things up". Somebody wrote a handbook for a better-equipped agent.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead** is a handbook with a section per kind of decision, versioned in
the repository beside the code, changed by pull request, and never edited live. The instruction is not
documentation of the agent. **It is the agent.** More of the agent's observable behaviour is decided by
that string than by any other single artifact in the system, and it is usually the only one with no
review requirement, no test, and no owner.

**What degrades at scale is ownership.** On the first day, an instruction is thirty lines written by
one person. A year later it is three hundred lines that four teams have appended to, and nobody can
delete anything, because nobody knows which line is holding which behaviour up. This is the real cost
of untestable lines: a lucky charm cannot be removed safely, because removing it might change
something and there is no probe that would tell you. **Every line you add without a probe is a line
your successor cannot delete.**

**The failure that only shows with real traffic** is the gap between the instruction's imagined user
and the actual one. The handbook is written against the tickets you thought about; production sends
the tickets you did not. Users write in three languages, paste twelve kilobytes of log output, ask
about invoices in a triage channel, and are sometimes angry. Every one of those is a case the
instruction is silent on, and silence is not neutrality. The model fills it, consistently enough that
nobody notices, until the day it fills it badly in front of a customer.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Three of these lines have no probe. Either add the
eval case that fails without the line, or delete the line. I don't mind which, but I'm not approving
text that we can't tell is doing anything. It'll still be here in two years and nobody will dare
remove it."*

**The interview question:** *"how do you know your system prompt is any good?"* The answer that shows
you have done it: "I don't judge it by reading it, because reading it only tells me it sounds
reasonable. Every rule in it has a probe, meaning a message designed so that the answer is wrong if
the rule isn't being followed, and those probes are an evalset that runs in CI. If I can't write the
probe, I don't write the rule, because a rule I can't test is also a rule I can never safely delete.
The thing that surprises people is how much of prompt work is deletion. Most of the lines that get
added after an incident are there to make somebody feel better, and they cost tokens on every request
and crowd out the rules that are actually load-bearing."

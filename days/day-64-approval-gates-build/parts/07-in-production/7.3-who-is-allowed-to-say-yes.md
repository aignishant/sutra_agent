# 7.3 — Who is allowed to say yes

## One-line answer

The desk records **who** approved and never checks whether they were allowed to — `by` is a string
the client sends, so the gate authenticates nobody, and an approval gate without authorisation is a
gate with a signature line and no lock.

## The story

The school sends a form home before the trip. It has to be signed by a parent or guardian.

Your elder brother, who is nineteen, walks you to school every day, knows the teachers, and is
entirely responsible. He is not a parent or guardian, and the office will not accept his signature,
which annoys everybody involved on the morning of the trip.

The rule is not about competence and everyone knows it. It is about *who is answerable if something
happens*. The signature is not evidence that somebody thought about it; it is a record of who has
taken responsibility, and that only means something if the person who signed is the person the school
can go back to afterwards.

Which is also why the office looks at the signature and compares it against what they have on file.
An unchecked signature line is a formality. A checked one is a control.

## The idea in plain language

Everything in this day has been about **whether** an action needs a human. This part is about
**which** human, and the desk currently has no opinion.

Look at what arrives when a shift lead approves:

```
{"approved": true, "by": "shift_lead", "at": "2026-09-05T21:40:00Z"}
```

`by` is a string in a payload the client sent. The gate reads it, part
[5.1](../05-the-record/5.1-the-decision-is-already-written-down.md) records it, and part
[3.5](../03-binding-the-answer/3.5-the-key-names-the-decision.md) puts it in the idempotency key. At
no point does anything verify that the sender is who they claim, or that whoever they are is permitted
to approve *this* action.

There are three separate questions hiding in "who approved", and collapsing them is the mistake:

- **Authentication** — is this actually the shift lead? Answered by the transport carrying the reply,
  not by the payload. A field in a JSON body is a claim, not a credential.
- **Authorisation** — is a shift lead allowed to approve *a refund*? This is a policy question, and it
  belongs in the same file as the gating policy, because it is the same kind of rule: a property of
  the action, not of the code path.
- **Separation of duty** — is this person allowed to approve *this particular* action, given that they
  may have proposed it? An approver who can also be the requester turns a two-party control into a
  one-party one.

Day 63 designed the gate around the first two. This part is where the third one becomes concrete,
because an agentic system has a specific version of it: the agent proposes, and if the same operator
who configured the agent can also approve its proposals, then the "human in the loop" is the person
who wanted the action to happen.

One term, defined once: **the confused deputy** is a program that holds authority and acts on
instructions from somebody who does not. Day 68 teaches it as its own subject; here the relevant edge
is narrower — the desk holds the authority to send, and it will exercise it on the say-so of whoever
can put a payload on the resume path.

## Why Sutra needs it

Day 65's gate asks the desk's audit record to answer *who approved what, on what evidence, when*. Part
[5.2](../05-the-record/5.2-what-the-record-has-to-answer.md) showed it answers all four. What this
part adds is that the *who* is currently unverified, so the record answers the question with a claim
rather than a fact — and a gate audit that accepts a claim has audited the client, not the control.

It also sets up Phase 10 directly. Day 68 is least privilege for tools and Day 66 is the threat model;
an approval endpoint that trusts a `by` field is one of the first things a threat model finds.

## The mechanism

The mechanism here is a gap, and the honest way to teach a gap is to measure it. Here is what the
desk's gate consults about the approver:

```bash
cd days/day-64-approval-gates-build/lab
uv run python -c "
import pathlib
import re

source = pathlib.Path('desk.py').read_text(encoding='utf-8')
body = re.search(r'def gate\(.*?\n\n\n', source, re.S).group(0)
for field in ('approved', 'by', 'role', 'signature', 'token'):
    print(f'  gate reads {field!r:<12} {field in body}')
"
```

**Line by line:**

- The gate's body is read **from the file** rather than with `inspect.getsource`. `@node(...)` turns
  the function into a `FunctionNode`, and `inspect.getsource` on one raises
  `TypeError: module, class, method, function, traceback, frame, or code object was expected, got
  FunctionNode` — the decorated object is a Pydantic model, not a function.
- The regex takes everything from `def gate(` to the next blank-line gap, which is the node's body and
  nothing else.
- The five field names are the one the gate uses, the one it records, and three an authorisation check
  would need.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
  gate reads 'approved'   True
  gate reads 'by'         True
  gate reads 'role'       False
  gate reads 'signature'  False
  gate reads 'token'      False
```

`approved` and `by`, and nothing else. And `by` is only read to be **recorded** — trace it through
part [6.1](../06-wiring-the-desk/6.1-the-sixth-station.md)'s gate and it goes into `decided["by"]` and
then into the audit trail. It never reaches a comparison.

So the desk's control, stated precisely: *the reply is sent if a payload arrives on the resume path
carrying `approved: True`.* Whoever can reach that path can approve. On the lab that is the script
itself; in a deployment it is whatever sits in front of the resume endpoint.

What authorisation would look like is a second question in the policy file, alongside the first:

```
{
  "action": "send_reply",
  "gated": true,
  "when": {"field": "channel", "in": ["email", "sms"]},
  "approvers": ["shift_lead", "duty_manager"],
  "because": "leaves the building and cannot be recalled"
}
```

**Line by line:**

- `approvers` sits next to `gated` because it is the same kind of rule about the same action, and
  splitting them across two files is how they drift.
- It is a list, because more than one role can usually approve, and because a list of one is a
  single point of failure that part [7.2](7.2-the-deadline-the-approval-must-beat.md)'s deadline will
  eventually run into.
- The **role** is named, not the person. Naming people in a policy file means editing the policy when
  somebody leaves, and the file is a security control that should not change for staffing reasons.
- What it does not contain is the identity check itself. `approvers` is authorisation; authentication
  has to happen before the payload is trusted enough to read this against.

This is deliberately not implemented today. The hub's §4 leaves it as a `TODO(me)`, because writing it
requires deciding how an approver authenticates, and that decision belongs with the deployment rather
than with the lab.

## When it breaks

The gate accepts an approval from a name that does not exist:

```bash
cd days/day-64-approval-gates-build/lab
uv run python -c "
import _actions

for who in ('shift_lead', 'nobody_at_all', '', None):
    _actions.reset()
    answer = {'approved': True, 'by': who}
    if answer.get('approved') is True:
        _actions.send_reply('4655', 'email', 'Your refund is on its way.')
    print(f'  by={who!r:<16} sent: {len(_actions.EFFECTS)}')
"
```

**Line by line:**

- The condition is the gate's real one from part 6.1, reproduced exactly: `answer.get("approved") is
  True`.
- The four values are a real role, an invented one, an empty string, and a missing field — the shapes
  a client can send.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
  by='shift_lead'     sent: 1
  by='nobody_at_all'  sent: 1
  by=''               sent: 1
  by=None             sent: 1
```

Four for four. The refund goes out regardless of who claims to have approved it, including when nobody
does. And the audit trail from part 5.1 will record `"by": null` as faithfully as it records
`"shift_lead"`, so the gap is visible afterwards to anybody who looks — and invisible to anybody
reading a report that counts approvals.

The subtler break is the one that survives adding `approvers`. A role check compares the payload's
claim against a list, and if the payload is still unauthenticated then the attacker simply claims
`shift_lead`. Authorisation without authentication moves the required guess from *"send approved:
true"* to *"send approved: true and a valid role name"*, and role names are in the policy file, which
is in the repository, which goes public in Phase 14.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead.** The identity comes from the transport, never from the body.
The approval arrives over an authenticated channel — a signed token, a session, mutual TLS — and the
runtime attaches the verified identity to the resume before any application code sees it. Application
code then reads that attached identity and ignores any `by` field in the payload entirely. The rule
is: the payload says what was decided, the transport says who decided it, and the two are never the
same source.

**What degrades at scale.** Approver lists rot. People change teams, roles are renamed, and the list
in the policy file is updated by whoever remembers. The list should name roles and resolve them
against whatever the organisation already uses for identity, so that leaving the company revokes
approval rights without anybody editing a JSON file. A policy that has to be edited to stay correct
will be wrong.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic.** Self-approval. The same operator triggers a
run and approves its proposal, entirely innocently — they are on call, it is their ticket, they know
it is fine. Every check passes, the audit trail is complete, and the two-party control was a one-party
control that night. Separation of duty needs the *proposer* recorded as well as the approver, and a
comparison between them, and neither exists in this desk today.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"The approval payload carries `by` and we log it.
Nothing verifies it. Take the identity from the authenticated session on the resume endpoint and drop
the field from the payload — a `by` that the client controls is worse than no `by`, because it looks
like an audit trail."*

**The interview question:** *"Your audit log says who approved an action. How do you know that is
true?"* An honest answer: *"On the desk I built, I do not — `by` is a string in the payload and the
gate never compares it against anything. It reads it only to record it, which is the worst
combination, because the trail looks authoritative. Fixing it is two separate things: authentication,
which has to come from the transport rather than the body, and authorisation, which is a list of
permitted roles per action and belongs in the same policy file as the gating rule. And the third
thing people miss is separation of duty — if the person who triggered the run can also approve it,
there is no second party, and you need the proposer recorded to check that."*

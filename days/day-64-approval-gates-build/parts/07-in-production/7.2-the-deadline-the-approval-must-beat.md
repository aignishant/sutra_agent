# 7.2 — The deadline the approval must beat

## One-line answer

A proposal that is answered late is not the same as one that is answered — under the naive policy
**2 of 14** cross the staleness line, and the system has no correct automatic response to a stale
approval, so the deadline has to be a decision somebody writes down rather than a timeout somebody
picks.

## The story

You deposit a cheque and the bank tells you it will clear.

What that means, exactly, is that the cheque has to reach the clearing house before the cut-off. Get
there in the morning and the money is in your account tomorrow. Get there twenty past the cut-off and
the cheque is not rejected — it simply joins tomorrow's batch, and everything downstream moves by a
day.

Now suppose you had written that cheque for a payment due today. The cheque is valid. The bank did
nothing wrong. The money will arrive. And you have still missed the thing you were trying to do,
because the payment had a deadline and the clearing had a schedule, and nobody ever put those two
facts in the same sentence.

The failure was never in the cheque or in the bank. It was in the gap between a process with a
schedule and an obligation with a deadline, which nobody owned.

## The idea in plain language

Part [7.1](7.1-the-queue-in-front-of-the-approver.md) measured the queue and found two proposals still
pending at the end of a shift. This part is about what should happen to them, and the honest answer is
that there is no good automatic option.

There are four, and each is wrong in a different way:

- **Wait for ever.** The proposal stays pending until somebody answers. Correct on the risk axis, and
  the customer never hears back. It converts a decision problem into an abandonment problem, and
  abandonment is invisible.
- **Time out and proceed.** After some period, act as though it were approved. This deletes the gate:
  an attacker, or a busy Monday, only has to wait. Never defensible for an irreversible action, which
  is the only kind that gets gated.
- **Time out and refuse.** After some period, treat it as rejected. Safe, and it silently converts
  *"nobody looked"* into *"a human declined"* — which then goes into part
  [5.1](../05-the-record/5.1-the-decision-is-already-written-down.md)'s audit trail as a decision
  nobody made. Part [2.3](../02-the-tool-gate/2.3-a-rejection-is-not-a-failure.md) spent a whole part
  keeping those two states apart; this collapses them.
- **Time out and escalate.** Hand it to a different, usually more senior, person. This is the only
  one that does not lose information, and it is not free: it needs a second approver to exist, and it
  moves the same queueing problem one level up.

The reason the first three are so common is that a deadline looks like a configuration value. It is
not. It is a statement about what the business does when nobody is available to decide, and that is a
question for the people who own the consequence.

One term, defined once: a **stale proposal** is one whose age has passed the point where answering it
is still the right thing to do — because the customer has given up, the state has moved on, or the
window the action was for has closed. Staleness is a property of the *proposal's purpose*, not of the
queue, which is why a single global timeout is usually wrong.

## Why Sutra needs it

The desk's gated action is a reply to a customer who is waiting. A refund confirmation that goes out
after the customer has already phoned, escalated and complained is worse than useless: it tells them
the system was slow rather than that it was careful.

Day 65's gate kills the process while it is *waiting for the human* as one of its four kills. What
that kill exposes is precisely this question — the run comes back, the pending proposal is still
there, and the desk has to decide whether it is still worth acting on.

## The mechanism

`latency.py` already carries the deadline, and it is one constant:

```python
DEADLINE = 8  # a proposal older than this many slots is stale: the customer has waited too long
```

**Line by line:**

- `8` is in **arrival slots**, not on a clock. That is deliberate: eight arrivals is a statement about
  how much other work went past while this proposal sat there, which stays meaningful whether the desk
  is busy or quiet.
- The comment carries the *reason*, in the same way `policy.json` requires a `because` on every row
  (part [1.2](../01-the-policy-is-data/1.2-the-policy-is-a-file-not-a-habit.md)). A deadline without a
  stated reason will be tuned by whoever finds it inconvenient.
- It is a module constant rather than a per-action value, which is the simplification this part ends
  by arguing against.

Run the two policies again and read only the last line of each:

```bash
cd days/day-64-approval-gates-build/lab
uv run python latency.py
uv run python latency.py --all-writes
```

**Line by line:**

- These are part 7.1's two arms, unchanged. What changes is which number you are reading them for.
- The deadline is applied identically in both, so the comparison isolates the effect of the arrival
  rate on staleness.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
policy: policy.json
gated proposals: 4 of 24 calls

  longest wait            4 slots
  mean wait               3.0 slots
  past the 8-slot deadline  0
```

```text
policy: gate every write
gated proposals: 14 of 24 calls

  longest wait            8 slots
  mean wait               3.7 slots
  past the 8-slot deadline  2
```

The mean wait barely moved — 3.0 to 3.7 — and that is the number a dashboard would show you. The
longest wait doubled and **two proposals went stale**.

This is the general shape of queueing failure and it is worth internalising: the average stays
comfortable while the tail goes over the edge, and the tail is where the harm is. A report saying
*"average approval time is well within target"* is compatible with customers being abandoned, and
both of those sentences are true of the same run.

Note also that the naive policy's longest wait is exactly 8 — it does not sail past the deadline, it
sits on it. The proposals that go stale here are the ones the approver simply never reached, and their
age is bounded only by the length of the shift.

## When it breaks

The break is that a stale proposal gets answered anyway, and the answer is applied.

Nothing in this day's machinery expires anything. Part
[3.1](../03-binding-the-answer/3.1-the-question-has-to-be-written-down.md) wrote a pending record with
a key; part [3.3](../03-binding-the-answer/3.3-approve-one-thing-execute-another.md) bound the answer
to the arguments so a swapped payload is refused. Neither of them looks at the clock. So an approval
that arrives long after the proposal was made passes every check this desk makes and executes:

```bash
cd days/day-64-approval-gates-build/lab
uv run python -c "
import _actions
import desk
import latency

_actions.reset()
stale_by = 20
print(f'  proposal age when answered: {stale_by} slots')
print(f'  the deadline:               {latency.DEADLINE} slots')
answer = {'approved': True, 'by': 'shift_lead'}
approved = answer.get('approved') is True
print(f'  what the gate checks:       approved is True -> {approved}')
print('  what the gate checks about age: nothing')
_actions.send_reply('4655', 'email', 'Your refund is on its way.')
print(f'  effects:                    {_actions.EFFECTS}')
"
```

**Line by line:**

- `stale_by = 20` is well past `latency.DEADLINE`, which is imported rather than retyped so the two
  numbers cannot drift.
- The middle lines reproduce the gate's actual decision logic from
  [6.1](../06-wiring-the-desk/6.1-the-sixth-station.md) — `answer.get("approved") is True` — to show
  what it consults, which is one field.
- The send is then performed directly, because that is what the gate would route to.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
  proposal age when answered: 20 slots
  the deadline:               8 slots
  what the gate checks:       approved is True -> True
  what the gate checks about age: nothing
  effects:                    [('send_reply', '4655', 'email')]
```

The proposal was two and a half times past its own deadline and the reply went out, because age is
not one of the things the gate looks at. The deadline exists in `latency.py`, where it is measured,
and nowhere in `desk.py`, where it would have to be enforced. That gap is real in this lab and it is
left open deliberately: closing it is the hub's build brief, and closing it *correctly* requires
picking one of the four bad options above, which is not a decision a lab script should make for you.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead.** They put the deadline on the **proposal**, not in a module
constant, and they derive it from the action: a refund reply to a waiting customer expires sooner than
a memory write. The pending record carries `expires_at` alongside the fields part
[5.2](../05-the-record/5.2-what-the-record-has-to-answer.md) requires, and the resume path compares it
before applying the answer — so a late approval fails closed with a reason rather than executing.

**What degrades at scale.** Expiry needs a sweeper, and a sweeper is a second system that can be
wrong. If it expires proposals that were about to be answered, approvers stop trusting the queue; if
it never runs, the pending table grows for ever, which is part
[3.1](../03-binding-the-answer/3.1-the-question-has-to-be-written-down.md)'s production warning
arriving from the other side. Sweep on read as well as on a schedule, so a proposal cannot be applied
while it is stale even if the sweeper is behind.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic.** Overnight and weekends. A deadline tuned
against weekday traffic expires everything proposed after the shift ends, so Monday morning starts
with a queue of expired proposals and an approver who has nothing to approve, only work to redo.
Deadlines have to know about the approver's schedule, and the moment they do, they are a business rule
rather than a timeout.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"There is a `DEADLINE` in the latency model and
nothing in the gate. We are measuring staleness and not acting on it. Put `expires_at` on the pending
record and check it on resume — and write down what happens when it fires, because 'treat it as
rejected' will end up in the audit trail as a human decision."*

**The interview question:** *"What happens when nobody answers your approval request?"* An honest
answer: *"There is no correct automatic answer, which is why it has to be decided rather than
configured. Waiting for ever abandons the customer silently, timing out and proceeding deletes the
gate, and timing out and refusing writes a decision into the audit trail that no human made. Escalation
is the only option that keeps the information, and it just moves the queue up a level. On our desk I
measured the naive policy pushing two of fourteen proposals past their staleness line while the mean
wait looked fine — the average is the number that hides this. And our gate checks the approval and not
its age, so a stale answer still executes; that is a known gap with an `expires_at` on the pending
record as the fix."*

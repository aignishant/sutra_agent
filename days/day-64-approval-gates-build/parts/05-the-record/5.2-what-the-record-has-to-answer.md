# 5.2 — What the record has to answer

## One-line answer

A record that says *approved by shift_lead* is not a record — it has to carry the **evidence the
approver saw and the policy version that produced the question**, because the only reader who will
ever need it is somebody reconstructing a decision they were not present for.

## The story

You need your school leaving certificate for something, twenty years on.

It is a single sheet, and everything on it is dull. Name, father's name, date of birth in figures and
in words, date of admission, date of leaving, the class you were in, conduct: satisfactory. A
signature and a round stamp.

Nobody who filled it in was thinking about you standing in an office two decades later. They filled
it in because that is the form. And that is exactly why it works — every field on it is a field
somebody once needed and could not get, and the form is the accumulated memory of those failures.

The thing it never says is *why*. There is no line for "we thought he was a good student". It records
the facts a stranger can check, in a form a stranger can read, without any of the people involved
being available to explain. If it had been written as a note by a teacher who remembered you, it
would be warmer, and it would be worth nothing at all in that office.

## The idea in plain language

Part [5.1](5.1-the-decision-is-already-written-down.md) established that the record is derived from
the events rather than written by hand. That settles *how* the record is produced. It does not settle
what has to be in the events for the derivation to be worth reading.

The test is a single question: **can somebody who was not there reconstruct the decision?** Not "was
it approved" — that is one bit. Reconstruct it: understand what was proposed, what the approver was
looking at when they agreed, under what rules, and what followed.

That gives a checklist, and each row exists because leaving it out breaks a specific reconstruction:

| Field | The question it answers | What breaks without it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The proposal | What was going to happen? | You know a refund was approved; not for which ticket or how much |
| The evidence shown | What did they see? | You cannot tell an informed approval from a rubber stamp |
| The policy version | Under which rules? | A decision correct in March looks wrong under June's policy |
| The decider | Who? | Nobody is accountable, and you cannot check they were allowed |
| The moment | When? | You cannot order it against anything else that happened |
| The outcome | What actually happened next? | You have a decision with no evidence it was carried out |

The one people leave out is the **evidence shown**, and it is the one that matters most, because it is
the only field that distinguishes an approval from a click. Day 63 argued that a gate whose approver
cannot see what they are approving is decoration; this is that argument in its recorded form. If the
record cannot say what was on the screen, then a year later nobody can tell whether the approver was
careless or the system never showed them anything.

The second most-forgotten is the **policy version**, and it is subtle. A record of a decision is
incomplete without the rules the decision was made under, because policies change. A refund approved
under version 3 — where refunds are gated and notes are not — is a different fact from the same
refund approved under a version where nothing was gated.

One term, defined once: **provenance** means the record of where something came from and how it came
to be. An approval's provenance is the chain from proposal to decision to effect, and a record with
gaps in that chain cannot support the conclusion somebody will want to draw from it.

## Why Sutra needs it

Day 65's gate has a criterion phrased exactly as the reconstruction test: *the run's audit record
answers who approved what, on what evidence, when*. Part
[5.1](5.1-the-decision-is-already-written-down.md) showed the desk's record answers it today, and the
reason it does is that the `ask` node's payload was designed to.

The repository also goes public in Phase 14, which changes the audience for every record in it.
Whatever the desk stores is something a stranger will read.

## The mechanism

The mechanism is a payload, and the whole of it is three lines in the asking node:

```python
def ask():
    yield RequestInput(
        interrupt_id=INTERRUPT,
        message="Send the refund reply for ticket 4655 to the customer by email?",
        payload={**PROPOSAL, "policy_version": _policy.version()},
    )
```

**Line by line:**

- `payload` is the field part [4.1](../04-the-node-gate/4.1-a-node-that-stops-and-asks.md) noted was
  `None` on the tool path. Everything the record will later report as *evidence shown* comes from
  here, because this is the only structured thing that reaches the approver.
- `**PROPOSAL` spreads the ticket, the channel and the resolution. It is the same dict the message was
  written from, which is what stops the sentence and the record from drifting apart.
- `_policy.version()` reads the version out of `policy.json` at the moment the question is asked. Not
  a constant, and not read at import time — if somebody edits the policy between two runs, the two
  records honestly disagree.

**Line by line:**

- This is the same command as part 5.1. The point here is not the mechanism but the audit: read the

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
  {
    "asked": "Send the refund reply for ticket 4655 to the customer by email?",
    "shown": {
      "ticket": "4655",
      "channel": "email",
      "resolution": "refund",
      "policy_version": 3
    },
    "answered": {
      "approved": true,
      "by": "shift_lead",
      "at": "2026-09-05T21:40:00Z"
    },
    "then": [
      {
        "approved": true,
        "by": "shift_lead",
        "at": "2026-09-05T21:40:00Z"
      },
      {
        "sent": "4655",
        "channel": "email",
        "chars": "26"
      }
    ]
  }
```

Against the six rows: the proposal is in `shown` (ticket, channel, resolution). The evidence shown is
`shown` itself, plus `asked` for the sentence. The policy version is `policy_version: 3`. The decider
is `by`. The moment is `at`. The outcome is the last entry of `then` — `sent`, with the channel it
went out on.

Six for six. And notice that five of the six were free: they are in the record because the runtime
stores events, and only `policy_version` had to be deliberately added. That ratio is typical, and it
is the argument for deriving the record — the expensive part is not the plumbing, it is thinking once
about what to put in the payload.

One honest gap. `chars: '26'` tells you the reply was twenty-six characters long. It does not tell
you what the reply **said**. The record can prove a refund reply was approved and sent to ticket 4655
by email; it cannot reproduce the customer-facing text. Whether that is a gap or a feature is Day 69's
question, and the next section is where it has to be answered rather than noticed.

## When it breaks

The record is complete and it is still wrong, because the payload was built from state that changed
between the question and the answer.

`policy_version` is the one you can watch. Ask the question under version 3, edit `policy.json` while
the approver is deciding, and the answer arrives against a different rulebook:

```bash
cd days/day-64-approval-gates-build/lab
uv run python -c "
import json
import tempfile
from pathlib import Path

import _policy

original = json.loads(_policy.POLICY_PATH.read_text(encoding='utf-8'))
print('  version the question was asked under:', _policy.version())

with tempfile.TemporaryDirectory() as tmp:
    edited = Path(tmp) / 'policy.json'
    edited.write_text(json.dumps(dict(original, version=original['version'] + 1)), encoding='utf-8')
    _policy.POLICY_PATH = edited
    _policy._rules.cache_clear()
    print('  version in force when the answer arrived:', _policy.version())
"
```

**Line by line:**

- The edited policy goes into a temporary directory and `_policy.POLICY_PATH` is repointed at it, so
  the real `policy.json` is never written to. Editing the checked-in file to demonstrate a failure is
  how a lab ends up subtly different from the one the document describes.
- `_rules.cache_clear()` matters because `_policy` caches the parsed rules with `functools.cache` —
  without it the second read returns the stale value and the demonstration silently proves nothing.
- Only `version` differs between the two files. The rules are identical, so what is being measured is
  the stamp and not a change in behaviour.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
  version the question was asked under: 3
  version in force when the answer arrived: 4
```

The record says `policy_version: 3` and is correct — that *is* the version the question was asked
under, which is precisely why it is stamped at ask time rather than at execution time. But the code
about to run the effect is now operating under version 4, and nothing compares the two.

That is the same family as part
[3.3](../03-binding-the-answer/3.3-approve-one-thing-execute-another.md)'s
time-of-check-to-time-of-use, moved from the arguments to the rules. The arguments were bound; the
rulebook was not. The fix is the same shape: compare the stamped version against the current one
before executing, and treat a mismatch as a reason to re-ask rather than as a detail.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead.** They store a **hash of the rendered evidence**, not just the
structured payload. The approver saw a screen, and the screen was rendered from the payload by client
code that can change. Hashing what was actually rendered means the record proves what was displayed
rather than what the server intended to display, and those come apart the first time somebody changes
a template.

**What degrades at scale.** The payload is duplicated per proposal and stored for the life of the
session. Payloads that embed the full drafted reply — tempting, because it is exactly what the
approver wants to see — multiply your storage by the size of your outbound text and put customer
content in a second place. Reference the draft by id and keep the payload to the facts of the
decision; the approver's client can fetch the draft.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic.** Approvers acting on stale evidence. The
payload is a snapshot; the ticket keeps moving. By the time it is approved, the customer has replied,
the classification has changed, or another agent has already handled it. The record will faithfully
show a correct decision made on information that had stopped being true, and the only defence is to
re-check the proposal against current state at execution time — which is what makes the
`policy_version` comparison above worth writing rather than merely noting.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"The record says who approved and what the outcome
was. It does not say what we put in front of them. Six months from now, when somebody asks whether
this refund should have gone out, we will not be able to tell whether the approver was shown the
amount. Put the evidence in the payload and stamp the policy version."*

**The interview question:** *"What has to be in an approval's audit record?"* An honest answer:
*"Enough for somebody who was not there to reconstruct the decision, which is more than the outcome.
The proposal, the evidence the approver was actually shown, the policy version the question was
generated under, the decider, the time, and what happened afterwards. The two people forget are the
evidence — without it you cannot tell an informed approval from a rubber stamp — and the policy
version, because rules change and a decision that was right in March looks wrong under June's rules.
Most of it comes free from the event log; the payload is the part you have to design."*

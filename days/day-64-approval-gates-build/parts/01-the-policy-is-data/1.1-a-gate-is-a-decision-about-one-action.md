# 1.1 — A gate is a decision about one action

## One-line answer

An approval gate is not a property of an agent or of a tool; it is a decision made about **one
call, with the arguments it is about to run with**, and everything else today follows from taking
that sentence literally.

## The story

You go to a chemist's shop with a list. Cough syrup, a strip of paracetamol, a tube of antiseptic
cream, and one more thing the doctor wrote down.

The man behind the counter reaches for the first three without looking up. He has done it a
thousand times. Then he reads the fourth line, stops, and turns the paper over.

"Do you have the prescription?"

You do not. You have a photograph of it on your phone, which is not the same thing, and he says so
politely and puts the box back on the shelf.

Notice what happened, because it is the whole of today's subject. He did not decide that *you* were
untrustworthy. He did not decide that *this shop* is a shop where things get checked. He looked at
one item on one list and knew, without thinking about it, that this item is in a different class
from the other three. The first three he can hand over on his own authority. The fourth needs
somebody else to have already said yes, in writing.

And he did not check the antiseptic cream, because checking the antiseptic cream would waste your
afternoon and buy nobody anything. A shop where every item needs a prescription is not a safer
shop. It is a shop with a queue out of the door, staffed by somebody who has stopped reading the
prescriptions.

## The idea in plain language

An **approval gate** is a check placed in front of an action, which stops that action from
happening until a person has said yes.

That definition is easy. The part people get wrong is *what the check is about*.

There are three different things you could attach a gate to, and only one of them works:

- **The agent.** "The support agent needs approval." This sounds sensible and means nothing. The
  agent reads tickets, searches an archive, writes notes and sends replies. Gating "the agent" gates
  reading a ticket, which is absurd, or it gates nothing, which is worse.
- **The tool.** "The `close_ticket` tool needs approval." Closer, and this is where most systems
  stop. But `close_ticket(4610, "note")` files a note, and `close_ticket(4655, "refund")` moves
  money. One of those is a Tuesday afternoon and the other is a conversation with your finance team.
  A gate on the tool treats them identically, and whichever way it is set, it is wrong for half the
  calls.
- **The call.** "This call, `close_ticket(4655, resolution='refund')`, needs approval." This is the
  only one that can be right, because it is the only one that has all the information. The action
  and the arguments together are what determines the **blast radius** — how much of the world
  changes if this goes ahead and turns out to be wrong.

So a gate is a function of two things, and the shape of it is this:

> **decision = policy(action, arguments)**

Everything in this day is an elaboration of that line. Section 2 is what ADK does with it for a
tool. Section 4 is what you do with it inside a graph node. Section 3 is the machinery that stops
somebody handing you an approval for a *different* call than the one you asked about.

Two terms this day will use constantly, defined here once:

- **Blast radius** — what changes in the world if the action happens and was wrong, plus how hard
  the change is to undo. Day 63 built the classification; today implements it. A note on a ticket
  has a small blast radius: correct it and nobody outside ever knew. An email to a customer has a
  large one, because there is no undo for an email that has been read.
- **The approver** — the person who answers. They are not a function call. They are slow, they are
  sometimes asleep, and their attention is the scarcest resource the gate spends. Part
  [7.1](../07-in-production/7.1-the-queue-in-front-of-the-approver.md) measures what happens when
  you spend it carelessly.

## Why Sutra needs it

Day 58 built the triage graph: intake, classify, research, draft, review. It runs a real ticket
from the archive to a drafted reply, and it stops there, because Phase 8's version had nowhere safe
to put the last step. Day 59 gave it guards so it cannot run away. Days 60 and 61 gave it the
ability to stop and be picked up again.

That last capability is what makes today possible. A gate is a stop that lasts as long as a person
takes to answer, and until Day 61 the desk had no way to stop for that long without a process
sitting there holding the whole run in memory.

Now the piece that is missing is the sixth station: the one where the drafted reply actually goes
to the customer. Phase 9's gate — *kill it mid-run; it resumes; a human approved the write* — is
half durability and half this. Day 65 audits both. Today you build the half that says **a human
approved the write**, and the reason it is a whole day rather than an `if` statement is that four
of the five ways to write it are wrong in ways you cannot see by reading them.

## The mechanism

The mechanism for this part is a definition rather than a library call, so here is the definition
as code — the smallest thing that deserves the name *policy*:

```python
def needs_approval(action: str, args: dict[str, str]) -> bool:
    """True when this call, with these arguments, must be approved by a human."""
```

**Line by line:**

- `action: str` — the name of the thing being done, not the name of the tool that does it. Two
  tools can perform the same action (part
  [6.2](../06-wiring-the-desk/6.2-three-roads-to-one-function.md) shows exactly that happening), and
  the policy must give both the same answer.
- `args: dict[str, str]` — the arguments of **this** call. Without them the function cannot tell a
  refund from a note, which is the distinction the whole day turns on.
- `-> bool` — one question, one answer. It does not ask the human, it does not log, it does not
  notify. The moment a policy function also *does* something, it stops being reviewable, because a
  reviewer now has to read its side effects to know what it decides.
- No `tool_context`, no session, no agent. The policy must be callable from a test with two
  arguments and no framework, or nobody will test it.

Now the same shape asked about Sutra's real actions. `_policy.py` in the lab loads
`policy.json` and answers exactly that question; the file itself is part
[1.2](1.2-the-policy-is-a-file-not-a-habit.md)'s subject.

```bash
cd days/day-64-approval-gates-build/lab
uv run python _policy.py
```

**Line by line:**

- `_policy.py` carries its examples as doctests rather than as comments, so "the policy says a note
  is not gated" is a claim that fails loudly if it stops being true.
- Running the module directly runs the doctests. It is the cheapest possible check that the file on
  disk and the sentences in this document still agree.

Measured on 2026-09-05:


## When it breaks

The first way this breaks is an action nobody classified. A tool is added on a Thursday, it is not
in the policy file, and the policy is asked about it.

There are two possible behaviours and only one of them is defensible:

```traceback
Traceback (most recent call last):
  File "<string>", line 3, in <module>
  File "...\days\day-64-approval-gates-build\lab\_policy.py", line 73, in needs_approval
    rule = _rule(action)
           ^^^^^^^^^^^^^
  File "...\days\day-64-approval-gates-build\lab\_policy.py", line 65, in _rule
    raise UnknownAction(
_policy.UnknownAction: action 'issue_refund' has no row in policy.json - refusing it. Known actions: read_ticket, search_archive, write_memory, close_ticket, send_reply, escalate
```

That is `needs_approval("issue_refund", {...})` against a policy file that has never heard of
`issue_refund`. The alternative — returning `False` and letting it through — is the same bug that
`send_from_retry_path` has in part
[1.4](1.4-the-guard-that-was-only-added-in-three-places.md), except automated: every new tool
arrives ungated and nobody is told.

This is **deny by default**, and the reason it lives inside `_policy.py` rather than at the call
site is that a caller who remembers to handle `UnknownAction` is a caller who did not need the
protection. The exception is raised where forgetting is impossible.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead.** They separate the three questions that this part has been
careful to keep apart, and they name them separately in the code: *is this action gated* (policy),
*who may answer* (authorisation, part
[7.3](../07-in-production/7.3-who-is-allowed-to-say-yes.md)), and *what happens while we wait*
(durability, Day 61). A single `should_i_ask()` function that quietly does all three is the most
common shape in real repositories and it is unreviewable, because there is no place to stand and
read the policy.

**What degrades at scale.** The policy function is called on every action of every run. It must be
cheap and it must not do input/output. A policy that reads a database per call turns a routing
decision into a network dependency, and the first time that database is slow, every agent in the
fleet is slow. Load the policy once, keep it in memory, and reload it deliberately.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic.** Actions arrive that nobody in the design
meeting imagined — a tool added for a demo, a batch job that reuses the same function, a retry path.
Deny-by-default means these show up as loud refusals in the log on the day they appear, rather than
as a quiet stream of ungated writes discovered during an incident review a quarter later.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"`needs_approval` takes the tool name and not the
arguments. That means `close_ticket` is either always gated or never gated, and both of those are
wrong for most of our traffic. Pass the args in — the decision is about the call."*

**The interview question:** *"How do you decide what an agent needs human approval for?"* An honest
answer: *"Per call, not per tool and definitely not per agent, because the arguments are what
determine the blast radius. On our support desk `close_ticket` with a note is not gated and
`close_ticket` with a refund is, and it is the same function. I keep the decision in a pure function
of `(action, args)` that returns a boolean and does nothing else, so it can be unit-tested without a
framework and reviewed without reading the call sites. Unknown actions raise rather than return
False, because the failure I actually expect is somebody adding a tool and not thinking about it."*

# 6.2 — The log line and four alarms

## One-line answer

Five fields on every cache lookup — `decision`, `key_recipe`, `age_s`, `saved_calls`, `reason` — make
four alarms computable, and `age_s` is the one everybody omits and the only one that can see a stale
answer.

## The story

The security guard at an office gate keeps a register. Name, flat or company visited, time in, time
out, signature.

Most days it is a formality. Then a laptop goes missing from the third floor and the register is the
only record of who was in the building. Everybody crowds around it and discovers what it actually
contains: names in handwriting nobody can read, "time out" blank for two-thirds of the rows because
people leave past the guard without stopping, and a "company visited" column where half the entries
say `office`.

The register was not ignored. It was filled in, every day, by somebody doing their job. It simply was
not designed by anyone who had thought about the question it would one day be asked.

The fields on a log line are decided in a calm afternoon and read during an incident, by which time
the fields are whatever they are.

## The idea in plain language

Day 22 established Sutra's logging format: one JSON object per line, machine-readable, with the fields
decided before the incident rather than during it. A cache needs five of them.

| Field | Values | The question it answers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `decision` | `hit` · `miss` · `refused` | did we save a call? |
| `key_recipe` | the name of the key function | is a key change what moved the graph? |
| `age_s` | seconds, or `null` on a miss | how old was what we served? |
| `saved_calls` | `0` or `1` | the saving, in the rationed unit |
| `reason` | why, on `refused` | which of the gates declined |

Four of those are obvious once written down. The fifth, **`age_s`**, is the one that gets left out, and
it is worth being explicit about why it matters: without it, a hit that served a fresh answer and a hit
that served [5.4](../05-the-response-cache/5.4-the-answer-that-was-right-when-it-was-stored.md)'s
six-clock-hour-old refund policy are the **same log line**. The staleness failure has no other
signature anywhere in the system. If `age_s` is missing, that failure is not merely hard to detect; it
is undetectable from logs at all.

`key_recipe` is the second one people skip, and its job is attribution. When a hit rate jumps, the
first question is always *"did somebody change the key?"* — and if the recipe name is on the line, the
answer is a filter rather than an archaeology exercise through commits.

From those five fields, four alarms are computable:

| # | Alarm | Fires when | What it means |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | hit rate floor | hits/total < 30% | the cache is not paying for itself |
| 2 | wrong-hit rate | any wrong hit at all | a key collision or a stale entry |
| 3 | p95 served age | > 7200 s | entries are old enough to be claims about a changed world |
| 4 | bookkeeping ratio | (creates+deletes)/saved > 1.0 | the cache costs more than it saves |

Alarm 4 is the one nobody implements and the only one that catches
[2.4](../02-the-context-cache/2.4-the-three-ways-a-cache-dies.md)'s `--intervals 1` disaster, where
199 bookkeeping calls were spent to save payload on 200 requests.

Alarm 2 has an honest limitation that has to be stated rather than hidden: **it cannot be computed
from the log.** A wrong hit and a right hit produce identical JSON. It needs an answer key — a sampled
set of questions whose correct answers are known, replayed on a schedule. `lab/ops.py` can compute it
only because `_log.py` carries `answer_id`.

## Why Sutra needs it

Day 45 audited what Sutra emits and Day 84 will put tracing across the whole app. Between those two,
this day adds a subsystem that can be *wrong* while looking healthy, which is a new kind of thing for
this codebase.

Everything Sutra has built so far fails loudly. Day 21 made errors surface rather than being swallowed;
Day 44 made timeouts and retries explicit. A cache does not fail loudly. It is the first component in
this system whose failure mode is *a correct-looking response*, and the only defence available is
fields on a line.

## The mechanism

`lab/ops.py` replays the 60-ask log, emits the five-field line, and runs the four alarms over the
result.

```bash
cd days/day-51-caching-the-quota-lifeline/lab
uv run python ops.py
```

**Line by line:**

- `replay(key, ttl)` produces one record per lookup, in arrival order, with the five fields. It carries
  a sixth, `wrong`, which is stripped before printing — it is the answer key, and a real deployment
  does not have it on the line.
- `age_s` is `clock - stored_at` on a hit and `None` on a miss. `None` rather than `0`, because zero is
  a real age and "not applicable" is not a number.
- `alarms()` takes the records plus the create/delete counts and returns `(fired, sentence)` pairs. The
  sentence carries the measured value **and** the threshold, so an alarm that fires explains itself
  without anybody opening a dashboard.
- The four thresholds are module constants with names, not literals buried in comparisons — they are
  numbers somebody has to defend in a review, so they are visible.
- `creates, deletes = 0, 0` because the desk's context cache never fires
  ([3.2](../03-the-floor-we-never-reach/3.2-thirty-seven-per-cent-of-the-way-to-a-cache.md)). Written
  as a named pair rather than inlined as zeros, so the day it stops being zero there is a line to
  change.

Measured on 2026-09-05:

```text
key_recipe = normalised, ttl = 3600

{"t": 0, "decision": "miss", "key_recipe": "normalised", "age_s": null, "saved_calls": 0}
{"t": 95, "decision": "miss", "key_recipe": "normalised", "age_s": null, "saved_calls": 0}
{"t": 180, "decision": "miss", "key_recipe": "normalised", "age_s": null, "saved_calls": 0}
{"t": 240, "decision": "hit", "key_recipe": "normalised", "age_s": 240, "saved_calls": 1}
{"t": 310, "decision": "miss", "key_recipe": "normalised", "age_s": null, "saved_calls": 0}
{"t": 420, "decision": "hit", "key_recipe": "normalised", "age_s": 325, "saved_calls": 1}
... 54 more lines

alarms
    ok    1 hit rate 45% against floor 30%
    ok    2 wrong-hit rate 0% (0 of 27 hits)
    ok    3 p95 served age 3490 s against ceiling 7200 s
    ok    4 bookkeeping/saved = 0/27 = 0.00
```

Four green alarms, and each one is a sentence rather than a boolean. `hit rate 45% against floor 30%`
tells a reader both where they are and what the rule was, which is the difference between an alarm you
can act on and one you have to go and look up.

Read `p95 served age 3490 s`. At a TTL of 3600 seconds, the ninety-fifth percentile age is 3,490 — the
cache is serving entries that are almost exactly at their expiry. That is not a problem, and it is
information: it says the TTL is the binding constraint on this traffic, exactly as
[4.3](../04-the-adk-config/4.3-choosing-the-three-numbers.md) said to look for. Raise the TTL and this
number rises with it, which is how alarm 3 turns into an early warning for
[5.4](../05-the-response-cache/5.4-the-answer-that-was-right-when-it-was-stored.md)'s failure before
any wrong answer is served.

Now break the key and watch which alarm notices:

```bash
uv run python ops.py --break-key
```

**Line by line:**

- `--break-key` swaps `normalised` for `first_three_words`, the lossy key from
  [5.3](../05-the-response-cache/5.3-the-key-that-dropped-the-field.md). Nothing else changes.
- The `key_recipe` field on every line changes with it, which is the attribution the field exists for.

```text
{"t": 420, "decision": "hit", "key_recipe": "first_three_words", "age_s": 325, "saved_calls": 1}
... 54 more lines

alarms
    ok    1 hit rate 50% against floor 30%
  FIRING  2 wrong-hit rate 10% (3 of 30 hits)
    ok    3 p95 served age 3490 s against ceiling 7200 s
    ok    4 bookkeeping/saved = 0/30 = 0.00
```

**Alarm 1 got better. Alarm 2 fired.** The hit rate went from 45% to 50% and three of the thirty hits
are wrong. Three of the four alarms are still green, and if this deployment had only the standard one
— hit rate — the change would read as an improvement and ship.

That is the argument for having four rather than one, made in a single command.

## When it breaks

The break is `age_s` omitted, and the way to feel it is to look at what remains.

A hit line without `age_s`:

```json
{"t": 21500, "decision": "hit", "key_recipe": "normalised", "saved_calls": 1}
```

That is the log line for
[5.4](../05-the-response-cache/5.4-the-answer-that-was-right-when-it-was-stored.md)'s worst moment:
the entry stored at `t=500` and served at `t=21500`, twenty-one thousand seconds old, six clock hours
after the refund policy changed, telling an agent to promise a customer 14 days instead of 30.

There is nothing wrong with that line. `decision` is correct. `saved_calls` is correct. It is
byte-identical to a line from a hit served four seconds after it was stored. Alarm 3 cannot be
computed. Nothing in the system distinguishes the two events, ever, at any point in the future.

The smallest fix is a subtraction — `clock - stored_at` — which requires the store to keep the
timestamp, which is why `lab/gate.py` finding 7 refuses a `ResponseCache` with no `get_with_age`. The
field exists on the line only if the data structure was built to supply it, and the data structure is
decided long before the incident.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead.** They emit the line on **every lookup**, including misses.
Logging only hits is a common shortcut and it makes the hit rate uncomputable from the log — you have
the numerator and not the denominator, and you end up joining against request logs to get it. They
also log `refused` with a `reason`, so ADK's three `info`-level gate messages from
[3.1](../03-the-floor-we-never-reach/3.1-two-floors-and-a-first-turn-rule.md) become a field on a line
rather than a message in a log level nobody prints.

**What degrades at scale.** One line per lookup is a lot of lines. At high volume the honest answer is
to sample the line and keep the counters unsampled: a percentage of full lines for debugging, and
complete counts for `decision`, `saved_calls` and an age histogram. Sampling the counters is what
produces caching dashboards that disagree with the bill.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic.** Percentiles need distributions and averages
hide them. Mean served age is a useless number — it is dominated by the many fresh hits — while p95 and
p99 are where staleness lives, because the stale entries are by definition the old ones. A team that
logs `avg_age` will watch it stay flat while the tail grows, which is the exact shape of the failure
they were trying to catch.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Add the age to this log line. Right now a hit that
served a four-second-old answer and one that served a six-hour-old answer are the same JSON, and the
only failure this cache actually has is the second one. And log misses too, or nobody can compute a hit
rate without joining two log streams."*

**The interview question:** *"What would you log for a cache?"* An honest answer: *"Decision, the key
recipe, the age of what was served, whether a call was saved, and a reason on refusals. The age is the
one people leave out and it's the one that matters, because a cache's real failure is serving something
that stopped being true, and without an age a stale hit and a fresh hit are the same line. The key
recipe is for attribution — when the hit rate jumps, the first question is whether someone changed the
key. From those I can compute four alarms: hit rate against a floor, wrong-hit rate, p95 served age,
and bookkeeping calls over calls saved, which is the one that catches a cache costing more than it
saves. And I'd be honest that the wrong-hit alarm can't come from the log — a wrong hit and a right hit
are identical JSON, so that one needs a sampled answer key."*

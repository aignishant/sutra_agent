# 3.2 — Facts, not blobs

## One-line answer

Send the **finding**, not the evidence: a sixty-line log distilled to its two interesting lines is
twenty-seven times smaller, and the model answers the question either way — but only one of those
versions still fits in a conversation.

## The story

Telling the mechanic what the car does.

*"There is a grinding noise when I brake, only when the car is cold, only turning left."* Fifteen
seconds, and the mechanic knows where to look before the bonnet is open.

The other version of that conversation exists too. Somebody hands over a phone with forty minutes of
dashcam video and says *"it's in there somewhere"*. Every fact the mechanic needs is genuinely in the
video. It is also forty minutes of a road, and the two useful seconds are not marked.

Both people supplied the same information. Only one of them supplied it in a form that could be used.

## The idea in plain language

This rule applies to two organs at once, which is why it gets its own part.

**Templated facts.** Day 17 let you inject state into the instruction with `{key}`
([17.4.1](../../../day-17-state-scopes-and-lifetimes/parts/04-state-in-the-prompt/4.1-state-steers-the-next-turn.md)).
The rule is that the values you template must be **facts**: `severity: high`, `ticket_id: 4521`, a
customer tier, a preference. Small, atomic, and meaningful on their own. The moment a state value is
a paragraph, templating it means pasting a paragraph into every call for as long as the key is set.

**Documents.** When a tool returns a log, a search result or an artifact
([18.4.2](../../../day-18-artifacts-that-survive/parts/04-in-the-run/4.2-reading-it-back.md)), the
temptation is to hand the whole thing over and let the model find what matters. It will try. It will
also pay for every line, on this call and — because the result lands in the history — on every later
call of the conversation ([1.3](../01-the-binder/1.3-the-organ-that-grows-by-itself.md)).

The move in both cases is the same, and it has a name worth using: **distil before you send**. Extract
the finding in code, send the finding, and keep the evidence where it belongs — in an artifact, with
its filename in state, ready to be fetched if somebody asks
([18.1.1](../../../day-18-artifacts-that-survive/parts/01-not-a-state-key/1.1-the-thing-state-cannot-hold.md)).

Two objections are worth answering, because both are reasonable.

*"What if I filter out the important line?"* Then you have a bug, and it is a bug you can see, fix and
test — unlike a model quietly failing to notice the line in a sea of noise
([3.3](3.3-position-is-not-presence.md)). Filtering makes the selection explicit and reviewable.

*"The model is better at reading logs than my regular expression."* Sometimes. That is an argument for
a *cheap model* doing the distilling — a small, fast call whose output is a summary — not an argument
for putting the raw log in every subsequent request. Day 20 builds exactly that pattern for history.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because a support ticket is a delivery mechanism for blobs.

Customers paste stack traces. Engineers paste log excerpts. Vendors' status pages arrive as pages. Every
one of those lands in Sutra's context if nobody intervenes, and each one is paid for on the turn it
arrives and every turn after.

There is a second reason that is about correctness rather than cost. Sutra's job is triage — deciding
what a ticket is about — and a triage decision made from two lines of ERROR is easier to review than
one made from four thousand lines of INFO. The distilled version is better for the human reading the
result, not only for the model producing it.

## The mechanism

A log, and the two lines in it that matter:

```python
# days/day-19-context-engineering-selection/lab/distil.py
"""Sending the finding instead of the evidence. Zero model calls."""

import re

LOG = "".join(
    f"2026-09-03T09:{minute:02d}:41Z INFO auth session refreshed tenant=northwind-prod\n"
    for minute in range(0, 58)
) + (
    "2026-09-03T09:58:12Z WARN auth.session cookie_rejected samesite=lax redirect=true\n"
    "2026-09-03T09:58:13Z ERROR auth.session login_failed reason=no_session_cookie\n"
)

INTERESTING = re.compile(r"\b(WARN|ERROR)\b")


def distil(log: str, keep: int = 5) -> str:
    """The lines that are not the same as every other line, and nothing else."""
    lines = [line for line in log.splitlines() if INTERESTING.search(line)]
    return "\n".join(lines[-keep:])


finding = distil(LOG)
print("the whole log :", len(LOG), "chars,", len(LOG.splitlines()), "lines")
print("the finding   :", len(finding), "chars,", len(finding.splitlines()), "lines")
print(f"ratio         : {len(LOG) / len(finding):.0f}x smaller")
print()
print(finding)
```

**Line by line:**

- The generated `LOG` is fifty-eight identical INFO lines and two that are not. That is what real logs
  look like around an incident: the interesting thing is rare and the boring thing is voluminous.
- `INTERESTING` is a regular expression over severity words — the crudest possible selection, chosen to
  make the point that crude beats absent.
- `keep: int = 5` — a cap, because the failure mode of a filter is a log where *everything* matches. A
  distiller with no bound is a distiller that does nothing on the worst day.
- `lines[-keep:]` takes the **last** matches rather than the first: in an incident log, the end is where
  the failure is.
- The three printed lines are the argument, and the fourth block is the check — you can read the
  finding and see that nothing needed was dropped.

**Line by line:**

- No model, no key, no network: distilling is code.

Measured on 2026-09-03:

```text
the whole log : 4278 chars, 60 lines
the finding   : 159 chars, 2 lines
ratio         : 27x smaller

2026-09-03T09:58:12Z WARN auth.session cookie_rejected samesite=lax redirect=true
2026-09-03T09:58:13Z ERROR auth.session login_failed reason=no_session_cookie
```

Twenty-seven times smaller, and read the two surviving lines: they contain the cause (`cookie_rejected
samesite=lax`) and the effect (`login_failed reason=no_session_cookie`). Everything that was dropped
was the system working.

And now combine this with [1.3](../01-the-binder/1.3-the-organ-that-grows-by-itself.md)'s finding,
because that is where the number becomes serious. The whole log pasted into turn three of a ten-turn
conversation is paid for on turns three through ten: **eight times**. The distilled version is paid for
eight times too, and eight times 159 is less than one payment of 4,278.

## When it breaks

**The filter drops the line that mattered.**

No exception — a smaller context that is missing the answer, and a model that answers anyway. This is
the real cost of distilling, and the mitigations are ordinary engineering: keep the evidence as an
artifact so it can be fetched
([18.1.1](../../../day-18-artifacts-that-survive/parts/01-not-a-state-key/1.1-the-thing-state-cannot-hold.md)),
say in the prompt that the log was filtered and how, and test the filter against real logs.

**Everything matches.** A log that is all warnings distils to itself, and the cap is the only thing that
saves you. Without `keep`, a distiller is a function that is small on the days you test it.

**The blob was in state.** The variant Day 17 warned about: a paragraph written to a state key that the
instruction templates. It is then in the instruction — the organ with the *worst* growth behaviour,
since it is re-sent on every call for ever
([2.4](../02-anatomy/2.4-a-subscription-not-a-purchase.md)) — and it is invisible, because reading the
agent's source shows `{summary}` rather than four hundred characters.

## In production

**Distil at the boundary, keep the evidence, and say that you did.**

The version a professional writes has three parts. The tool returns the finding, not the payload. The
payload is saved as an artifact with its filename in state, so nothing is lost and a later step can
fetch it. And the prompt says what happened — *"showing the 2 warning lines from a 60-line log"* —
because a model told it is looking at an excerpt behaves differently from one that believes it has
everything.

**What changes at scale** is that distilling becomes a model call of its own. A regular expression
handles logs; it does not handle a PDF or a page of prose. The pattern is the same shape — a cheap
small-model call that produces a short summary, and the summary is what enters the expensive context —
and Day 20 builds it for the history organ.

**What fails only under real traffic** is variety. Your filter was written against the log format you
had. A different service, a different version, a JSON-formatted log, a stack trace with no severity
words: each one produces either everything or nothing, and both are silent.

**The review comment**: *"this returns the whole file from the tool. Return what the step needs, keep
the file as an artifact, and tell the model it is an excerpt."*

**The interview question**: *"a tool of yours returns a 4,000-line log. What do you do with it?"* The
answer that shows the cost model: *"distil it — the lines that are not like the others — send that,
store the whole thing as an artifact, and say in the prompt that it is an excerpt. Otherwise it is in
the history and I pay for it on every later turn."*

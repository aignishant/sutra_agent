# 2.4 — A subscription, not a purchase

## One-line answer

The system instruction is sent **again on every single call**, unchanged, for the life of the agent —
so every sentence in it is a standing charge, and the test for whether a sentence belongs there is
whether it would be true on a call you have not thought of yet.

## The story

The direct debit you forgot about.

Three ninety-nine a month, set up years ago for something you used twice. It is small enough that it
has never crossed the threshold where you look at it, and it has now been going out for long enough
that the total is a genuinely annoying number.

The mistake was not signing up. The service was fine, the price was fine, and at the moment of signing
up the decision was correct. The mistake is that a recurring charge was evaluated once, as though it
were a purchase, and nothing in the process ever brought it back for review.

## The idea in plain language

[2.1](2.1-opening-the-envelope.md) measured the instruction at 145 characters on turn one and 145 on
turn four. That constancy is the whole subject of this part, and it cuts both ways.

**It is a virtue.** The instruction is how an agent stays itself. It does not drift as the conversation
grows, it cannot be forgotten, and it is the one part of the context you control completely
([Day 6](../../../day-06-instructions-and-personas/LESSON.md) built Sutra's handbook on exactly that
property).

**And it is a recurring cost.** Every word is re-sent on every call, for every user, for the life of the
agent. A paragraph you add today is paid for by every conversation from now on. It is not expensive —
Sutra's instruction is a fraction of its tool declarations
([2.3](2.3-the-menu-costs-more-than-the-handbook.md)) — but it is *permanent*, which changes what
belongs in it.

The test that follows is simple and unusually effective:

> **Would this sentence be true and useful on a call I have not thought of yet?**

If yes, it belongs in the instruction: the persona, the refusal policy, the format rules, the honesty
requirement. If it is about *this ticket* or *this document*, it belongs in a templated fact
([17.4.1](../../../day-17-state-scopes-and-lifetimes/parts/04-state-in-the-prompt/4.1-state-steers-the-next-turn.md))
or in the message, where it is paid for when it is relevant.

There is a second, subtler reason to keep it short, and it is about attention rather than tokens. An
instruction that has grown to forty rules through months of *"add a line to stop it doing that"* is an
instruction where every individual rule matters less, because the model is weighing forty of them at
once. Long instructions do not fail loudly; they fail by having each rule followed slightly less often.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because Sutra's instruction is the load-bearing document of the whole system, and the pressure on it
only ever goes one way.

[Day 6](../../../day-06-instructions-and-personas/LESSON.md) wrote it: role, scope, refusal, honesty,
tone, an example. Every day since has produced a candidate line — *say when you cannot search*
([16.7.1](../../../day-16-built-in-tools-with-brakes/parts/07-failure-lab/7.1-the-spare-that-does-not-fit.md)),
*state the numbers the code printed*
([16.5.3](../../../day-16-built-in-tools-with-brakes/parts/05-code-that-runs/5.3-reading-the-code-and-its-output.md)),
*never invent a ticket*. Each of those is a good sentence. All of them together is a document nobody
re-reads.

The discipline this part asks for is to sort them: standing rules stay, situational rules become
templated facts that appear only when they apply, and the instruction stays something a person can
read in one breath.

## The mechanism

The measurement is the constancy, and the arithmetic that follows from it:

```python
# days/day-19-context-engineering-selection/lab/subscription.py
"""What an instruction costs over a conversation, and over a month. Zero generate calls."""

SHORT = (
    "You are Sutra, the triage assistant for a software company's support desk. "
    "Explain what a ticket is about, suggest a likely cause labelled as a hypothesis, "
    "and recommend a priority with one reason. Say 'I don't know' when you do not know."
)

GROWN = SHORT + (
    " Always check the vendor's status before blaming our own systems. "
    "When you cannot search, say so and answer from the ticket alone. "
    "State numbers exactly as the code printed them. "
    "Never invent ticket contents, customer names, article titles or dates. "
    "Use British spelling. Do not apologise more than once in a reply. "
    "If the engineer seems frustrated, acknowledge it in one short sentence. "
    "Prefer bullet points for anything with more than three items."
)

CALLS_PER_CONVERSATION = 6
CONVERSATIONS_PER_DAY = 3
DAYS = 30


def report(label: str, instruction: str) -> None:
    per_call = len(instruction)
    per_month = per_call * CALLS_PER_CONVERSATION * CONVERSATIONS_PER_DAY * DAYS
    print(f"{label:8} {per_call:>5} chars/call   {per_month:>9,} chars/month")


report("short", SHORT)
report("grown", GROWN)
print()
print(f"the seven extra rules cost {len(GROWN) - len(SHORT)} characters on every call")
```

**Line by line:**

- `SHORT` is close to Sutra's real instruction from
  [Day 6](../../../day-06-instructions-and-personas/LESSON.md): role, task, honesty. Four sentences.
- `GROWN` is the same instruction after seven perfectly reasonable additions, each of which was written
  in response to something going wrong. **None of them is a bad sentence.** That is the point.
- `CALLS_PER_CONVERSATION = 6` — a triage conversation with a couple of tool calls; the number is stated
  rather than hidden so you can change it to yours.
- `per_call * ... * DAYS` — the standing charge. It is arithmetic, not a measurement, and it is the
  arithmetic nobody does.
- The last line isolates the marginal cost of the seven additions, which is the number to put next to
  the eighth proposal.

## When it breaks

**Nothing errors. The instruction simply stops working as well.**

There is no message for an instruction that has grown too long. What you observe is that a rule you
added last month is followed less often than it was when it was the only rule, and the natural response
— add a stronger sentence about it — makes the problem worse.

**A situational rule fires when it should not.** The rule *"always check the vendor's status before
blaming our own systems"* is right for a ticket about a vendor and noise for a ticket about a
password reset. Because it is in the instruction, it is present for both. Templating it from state
([17.4.1](../../../day-17-state-scopes-and-lifetimes/parts/04-state-in-the-prompt/4.1-state-steers-the-next-turn.md))
means it appears only when the fact that makes it relevant is set.

**The instruction contradicts itself.** The most expensive version, and it takes months to arrive: two
rules added a long way apart that cannot both be satisfied. The model picks one, apparently at random,
and the behaviour looks like flakiness rather than like a contradiction in a document nobody has read
end to end recently.

## In production

**Review the instruction as a whole, on a schedule, and delete.**

The version a professional keeps is short enough to read aloud, and it gets read aloud occasionally —
because the failure mode is accretion, and accretion is only visible when somebody looks at the whole
thing rather than at the diff. Every added sentence should also name what it replaces, if anything.

**What changes at scale** is that instructions multiply. A graph has an instruction per node, each
carrying its own standing charge, and the shared rules — honesty, refusal, format — get copied into all
of them. That is the point at which a common prefix belongs in one place and is composed in, rather
than being maintained in five agents that have quietly diverged.

**What fails only under real traffic** is the rule written for one incident. Somebody adds a sentence
on a bad afternoon to stop one specific wrong behaviour, it works, and it is still there two years
later shaping every answer. Instructions need the same treatment as feature flags: an owner, a reason,
and a date somebody looks at them again.

**The review comment**: *"this adds a sentence about one ticket type to the instruction, so it is on
every call for every ticket. Can it be a templated fact instead?"*

**The interview question**: *"what belongs in a system instruction and what does not?"* The answer that
shows the cost model: *"standing rules that would be true on a call I have not thought of yet. Anything
about this particular task goes in the message or a templated fact — because the instruction is a
subscription and the message is a purchase."*

# 3.2 — Who says the turn ended

## One-line answer

A request ends when the request ends, but an open microphone does not end — so **somebody has to
decide that a pause is the end of a sentence rather than a breath**, and there are exactly two
candidates: the far end inferring it from the sound, or the client saying so out loud with turn
markers.

## The story

You ring somebody and get their answering machine.

*"Hi, you've reached the flat, we're not in at the moment, so — "* and there is a gap. You wait. Is
that it? Have they finished? You start to say *"hi, it's — "* and the greeting starts up again over
the top of you: *" — leave your name and number and we'll get back to you."* Now you have talked
over the end of the message, you do not know whether the machine was recording yet, and you have
lost your thread.

Then: **beep.**

And everything is easy. You talk. You do not think about it at all.

The beep is the most boring sound in the recording and it is doing all the work. It carries no
information — no words, no name, no instructions. Its entire job is to say *the greeting is over, it
is your turn now.* Without it you were left staring at a silence, trying to work out from the
silence alone whether it meant "finished" or "still thinking". The silence looks identical in both
cases. That is the whole problem, and one short tone that means nothing else removes it completely.

Hold on to two things from that. First: the doubt was real, and it was not caused by a bad recording
or a bad line. It was caused by silence being ambiguous. Second: the fix was not a cleverer
listener. It was **one extra signal, sent on purpose, that exists for no other reason.**

## The idea in plain language

Say two words first, because everything below is made of them.

A **turn** is one person's go at speaking. A **turn boundary** is the moment it ends and the other
side's go begins. In a request/response service you have never had to think about either, because
the boundary is free: the request ends when the bytes stop, the server answers, done. Part
[1.1](../01-the-open-line/1.1-a-call-not-a-request.md) took that away. Once both sides hold the line
at once and the microphone is simply *on*, nothing about the connection tells you when the caller
stopped. The audio keeps coming. Silence is audio too — part [3.1](3.1-audio-is-bytes.md) measured
exactly that: two seconds of an open microphone was 64,000 bytes of nothing.

So the boundary has to be decided by somebody. There are two candidates and only two.

**Voice activity detection**, usually shortened to **VAD**, is the first. The far end looks at the
incoming sound, slice by short slice, and classifies each slice as *speech* or *not speech*. When
enough consecutive slices come back *not speech*, it declares the turn over and starts answering.
The decision is made from the audio alone, by whoever is receiving it. Nobody has to be told
anything.

A **turn marker** is the second. The client sends a small explicit message alongside the audio that
says *the caller has started speaking* or *the caller has stopped speaking*. It contains no sound.
It is the beep: a signal whose only purpose is to name a boundary. `LiveRequestQueue` offers exactly
this pair — `send_activity_start()` and `send_activity_end()` — and the hub's §8 lists them next to
`send_realtime` because they travel the same road.

Neither one is the safe default, and that is the point of this part. They fail in opposite
directions:

| Mechanism | Who decides | How it fails |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Voice activity detection (the far end infers from the sound) | The receiver, from the audio | Cuts you off when you pause to think, because a thinking pause and a finished sentence sound the same. Holds the floor when a door slams or a television is on, because a noise that is not speech can still look like speech |
| Turn markers (the client says so) | The client, explicitly | The decision now lives in a browser on a bad connection. A marker that never arrives leaves the far end waiting for the end of a turn that already ended — silence in both directions, and nobody talking |

Read the two "how it fails" cells against each other. The first row's failures are *wrong guesses
made from real data*. The second row's failures are *right answers that did not arrive*. Those are
different kinds of problem and they want different fixes, which is why picking one is a design
decision with consequences rather than a flag to flip.

## Why Sutra needs it

The day's build brief has one `TODO(me)` that this part exists to inform: *decide whether your
client sends turn markers or lets the far end infer the turn from the audio.* It is phrased as a
decision, and the brief says out loud that there is no default worth inheriting.

That phrasing is deliberate, because the library will happily make the choice for you by omission.
Send nothing but blobs and you have chosen inference — not because you weighed it, but because you
did not know there was anything to weigh. The hub's trap 9 is exactly this: letting the far end
guess where turns end, when a pause is a breath, a bad line and the end of a sentence all at once,
and only the client is in a position to know which.

There is a second reason this part is here rather than in section 2. Detecting that the caller has
*started* speaking mid-answer is the same question as detecting that they have *stopped* — it is the
turn boundary problem, asked at the other edge. Part
[2.1](../02-talking-over/2.1-the-caller-interrupts.md) detected an interruption with `not
self.inbox.empty()`, which is the easy way and is honest about being the easy way: something
arrived, so the caller is talking. A real system with a live microphone does not get that. Audio is
arriving continuously whether anybody is speaking or not, so "something arrived" is always true, and
the client or the far end has to decide what counts. Same question, both edges, one answer.

## The mechanism

`lab/vad.py` sends three blobs of audio through a real `Runner.run_live` into the
`ScriptedConnection` from `lab/_live.py`, and prints every single thing the connection received on
`send_realtime`, in order. With the markers, and then without them.

```bash
cd days/day-75-the-bidi-voice-loop/lab
uv run python vad.py; echo "exit: $?"
```

**Line by line:**

- `cd .../lab` — every script on this day imports `_live` as a plain sibling module, so it has to be
  run from the lab directory rather than from the repo root.
- `uv run python vad.py` — `uv run` uses the project's pinned interpreter and dependencies, so the
  `google-adk` being exercised is the one in `pyproject.toml` and not whatever is on the path.
- `; echo "exit: $?"` — prints the process exit code. It matters here because the two runs of this
  script disagree about it on purpose: the marker run exits `0` and the ablation exits `1`, which is
  the script encoding its verdict where a test runner can read it (Principle 11).

```text
the client sent turn markers: yes

  things the connection received on send_realtime   5
    ActivityStart
    blob
    blob
    blob
    ActivityEnd

  audio blobs                3
  turn markers               2

  The far end was told where the turn began and where it ended. It did not have to
  work anything out from the sound, which means a cough, a pause for breath and a
  bad line cannot be mistaken for the end of a sentence.
```

Five things arrived, and **two of them are not sound**. The connection did not have to look at the
audio to know where the turn was. It was told, twice, at both edges.

Now the ablation — the same script with the two marker calls skipped and nothing else changed.

```bash
uv run python vad.py --no-markers; echo "exit: $?"
```

**Line by line:**

- `--no-markers` is read in `main()` as `markers = "--no-markers" not in sys.argv`, and it is the
  only thing that differs between the two runs. In `drive()` it guards two lines and nothing else:
  the `queue.send_activity_start()` before the loop and the `queue.send_activity_end()` after it.
  The audio, the agent, the runner, the session, the `RunConfig` and the consumer are identical.
- The exit code is `1` this time, and that is the script's opinion rather than a crash. Nothing went
  wrong; the run simply produced a connection that has to guess, and the script says so in the only
  way an automated check can read.

```text
the client sent turn markers: NO (ablation)

  things the connection received on send_realtime   3
    blob
    blob
    blob

  audio blobs                3
  turn markers               0

  Three identical blobs of sound and nothing else. Everything the far end knows about
  where the turn ended, it has to infer from the audio - and a pause long enough to be
  the end of a sentence is also long enough to be somebody thinking.
```

Three against five. The same sound, the same bytes, the same everything — and in the second run the
far end has been handed a problem that in the first run did not exist.

### The finding nobody was looking for

Read the first output again and notice where the markers turned up. `ActivityStart` and
`ActivityEnd` arrived at **`send_realtime`** — the same method as the audio. Not a separate method,
not a control channel, not a field on some envelope. The same one.

That is worth stopping on, because the method's declared parameter type says otherwise. From
`google/adk/models/base_llm_connection.py` in `google-adk==2.7.1`, read on 2026-09-06, the abstract
method is `async def send_realtime(self, blob: types.Blob) -> None` — the parameter is named `blob`
and typed `types.Blob`. An `ActivityStart` is not a `types.Blob`. So how did it get there?

The runtime put it there. From `google/adk/flows/llm_flows/base_llm_flow.py` in the same version,
read on the same day, at the package's own two-space indentation:

```text
      if live_request.activity_start:
        await llm_connection.send_realtime(types.ActivityStart())
      elif live_request.activity_end:
        await llm_connection.send_realtime(types.ActivityEnd())
```

`LiveRequestQueue.send_activity_start()` does not talk to the connection. It puts a `LiveRequest`
with `activity_start` set on a queue, and the runtime's send loop — the branch above — translates
that into an `ActivityStart` object and hands it to `send_realtime`. The queue's vocabulary and the
connection's vocabulary are different vocabularies, and the runtime is the translator between them.
Part [1.2](../01-the-open-line/1.2-five-methods.md) said a live model is five methods; this is the
five methods carrying more kinds of traffic than their signatures advertise.

**The lesson is bigger than this one method.** A declared type and the observed traffic can differ,
and there is exactly one way to find out which you are dealing with: write the other end yourself
and print what actually arrives. That is what this lab is. Principle 8 says never invent an API and
always verify it against the documentation on the day you use it, and this is that principle in its
useful form — not "check the signature", but *check the signature and then check the wire, because
the signature is a promise about the common case and the wire is the truth.* A `send_realtime`
written from the type hint alone, which reached straight for `payload.data`, would have thrown
`AttributeError` the first time a caller pressed a push-to-talk button.

### What the far end has to do about it

Which is why `ScriptedConnection.send_realtime` classifies what it is handed instead of assuming.
Here it is in full from `lab/_live.py`, docstring included. On disk it is a method, so it is
indented by four spaces; it is dedented to column zero here and is otherwise unchanged.

```python
async def send_realtime(self, payload: object) -> None:
    """Everything that arrives while the caller is speaking.

    Three different things come through this one method, which is worth knowing before you write
    a `send_realtime` of your own: a `Blob` of audio, and the two turn markers `ActivityStart`
    and `ActivityEnd`. The runtime translates `LiveRequestQueue.send_activity_start()` into an
    `ActivityStart` and sends it here - see part 3.2.
    """
    name = type(payload).__name__
    data = getattr(payload, "data", None)
    if isinstance(data, bytes):
        self.blobs_in += 1
        self.bytes_in += len(data)
        self.realtime_log.append("blob")
        await self.inbox.put("<audio>")
        return
    self.markers_in += 1
    self.realtime_log.append(name)
```

**Line by line:**

- `payload: object` — **the parameter is renamed from `blob` and re-typed, and that rename is the
  finding.** The abstract method calls it `blob: types.Blob`. Writing this implementation and
  printing what arrived showed that "blob" is wrong for two of the three things that come through,
  so the name changed to match reality. A parameter name is documentation, and documentation that
  says `blob` when an `ActivityEnd` is coming down the same pipe will mislead the next person who
  reads it. `object` is the honest type: this method genuinely accepts more than one kind of thing.
- `type(payload).__name__` is the class name as a string — `"Blob"`, `"ActivityStart"`,
  `"ActivityEnd"`. Nothing else about a marker is interesting. It has no payload, no data, no
  duration. **The name is the entire content of the message**, so the name is what gets recorded,
  and that is what makes `ActivityStart` and `ActivityEnd` visible as separate lines in the output
  above rather than as a number that went up.
- `getattr(payload, "data", None)` — **a duck-typed test, not an `isinstance` check.** *Duck typing*
  is the practice of deciding what something is by what it can do rather than by what class it
  belongs to: if it has a `.data` attribute holding bytes, treat it as audio. It is used here
  because from the connection's point of view the three arrivals share no useful base class.
  `types.Blob`, `types.ActivityStart` and `types.ActivityEnd` are separate types in
  `google.genai.types`, and an `isinstance` chain against all three would have to be updated every
  time the runtime learns to translate a fourth thing — and the whole reason this method exists as
  written is that the set of things arriving here was not what the signature said. `getattr(...,
  None)` asks a question that cannot raise: absent attribute, `None`, move on.
- `isinstance(data, bytes)` is what **actually** distinguishes sound from a marker, and the two-step
  matters. `getattr` alone is not enough — an attribute called `data` could exist and hold something
  that is not audio, and a truthiness test would additionally mis-classify a legitimately empty
  chunk. Sound is bytes. Markers have no bytes anywhere. That single condition is the whole
  classifier.
- The audio branch counts three separate things — `blobs_in` for how many arrived, `bytes_in` for
  how much, `realtime_log` for the order — because part 3.1 needed the volume and this part needs
  the sequence, and a counter cannot be un-summed back into an order afterwards.
- `await self.inbox.put("<audio>")` puts a placeholder in the inbox so the scripted model answers,
  the same way it answers a typed message. A marker does **not** go in the inbox, which is correct:
  a marker is a statement about the turn, not a thing to reply to.
- `return` ends the audio branch, so the two counters never both fire for one arrival. Everything
  that is not sound falls through to the last two lines and is recorded by name — which means an
  unrecognised fourth kind of payload would show up in the log as its own class name rather than
  being silently dropped. That is Principle 10 at the size of two lines: surface it, do not swallow
  it.

## When it breaks

**Start with what this lab did not do, because it is the biggest thing on the page.** No voice
activity detection ran here. Not a single slice of real sound was classified. The audio in `vad.py`
is `b"\x00" * 3_200` repeated three times — digital silence, chosen because it is free and
reproducible — and the connection at the far end never looks at it beyond counting its length. So
**nothing on this page is evidence about how well inference works.** How accurately a far end can
tell a finished sentence from a thinking pause, what threshold it uses, how it behaves with an
accent or a fan running or two people in the room: none of that was tested, and this part is not
entitled to an opinion about it. What was measured is narrower and still worth measuring — *what the
far end is given*. Five things or three. Told, or left to work it out. That is a fact about the
wire, and it is the fact the design decision actually turns on.

**Inference fails by cutting people off.** A caller says *"the reference is, hang on — "* and looks
for a piece of paper. The silence crosses whatever threshold the far end uses, the turn is declared
over, and the model answers a half-question. The caller experiences this as being interrupted by a
machine, which is worse than being interrupted by a person, because there is nobody to apologise to.
The natural fix is to lengthen the threshold, and that buys the opposite complaint: every reply now
lags, and the line feels dead. There is no threshold that is right for a person reading out a serial
number and also right for a person saying *"yes"*.

**Inference also fails by never letting go.** A television in the room, a car passing, a cough, one
side of somebody else's phone call — sound arrives, it is plausibly speech, the far end concludes
the caller is still going and holds the floor. The caller has finished, is waiting, and nothing
happens. Both of these come from the same root: the only evidence available is the sound, and the
sound is genuinely ambiguous.

**Markers fail somewhere else entirely, and the failure is quieter.** `send_activity_end()` is a
message like any other, sent from a client that may be a browser on a train. If it is lost, or the
connection stalls, or a JavaScript error kills the handler that was supposed to send it, then the
caller has finished, the far end was never told, and it waits. There is no error text to paste for
that one — nothing throws, nothing times out at the protocol level, and the log on both sides looks
healthy. It is two sides being polite at each other for ever, which is the front-door goodbye from
part [1.3](../01-the-open-line/1.3-who-ends-the-call.md) with nobody at the door. The far end's own
inference is the backstop for this, which is a hint about the answer that *In production* takes up.

**A marker can also be a lie.** Explicit means the client asserts it, and a client asserting *the
caller has stopped speaking* while the caller is mid-word will truncate them just as effectively as
a bad threshold — the truncation is now your bug rather than the provider's. A push-to-talk button
released early is exactly this.

**And there is a third thing on the queue this lab does not exercise.** The hub's §8 lists
`send_audio_stream_end` alongside `send_activity_start` and `send_activity_end`, and `LiveRequest`
carries a matching `audio_stream_end` field. It is not the same as an activity marker: an activity
marker says *this turn is over*, while `audio_stream_end` says *the audio stream itself is
finished*. `vad.py` never calls it, nothing on this day counts it, and so this part has nothing
measured to say about how it behaves — only that it exists, that it is a third distinct thing, and
that a reader building a real client should go and read that branch of the runtime rather than
assume it is a synonym for `activity_end`.

## In production

**Real systems use both, and the choice is made per source of audio rather than once for the
service.** The question a professional actually asks is not *markers or inference* but **can this
particular client be trusted to know when the caller stopped?**

Sometimes it plainly can. A push-to-talk button is a turn marker with a person behind it: the caller
holds it while speaking and releases it when done, and *release* is not a guess about a pause — it
is a human being stating that they have finished. A "send" tap in a mobile app is the same. A
hardware device with a wake word and a physical end-of-utterance signal is the same. Where a client
has that kind of evidence, throwing it away and asking a model to re-derive it from silence is
losing information you were handed for free.

Sometimes it plainly cannot. An always-on microphone in a room has no button and no moment of
intent. A phone call arriving over a telephony gateway gives you audio and nothing else — there is
no client of yours at the far end to send anything. In those places inference is not a compromise,
it is the only mechanism available.

So the shipped design is usually a hybrid with a stated precedence: **send markers wherever the
client knows, run inference underneath as a backstop, and write down which one is authoritative when
they disagree.** The last clause is the part teams forget, and it is where the confusing bugs live —
a client that sends `activity_end` while the far end's detector still believes the caller is
speaking produces behaviour that neither mechanism would produce alone.

**What a professional records is which mechanism decided each turn boundary.** One field per turn:
*marker* or *inference*, and for inference how long the silence was that triggered it. This costs
almost nothing and it converts the single most common voice complaint — *"it cut me off"* — from an
argument into a query. Without it you have a caller's impression and a transcript that looks fine,
because a truncated turn is not an error anywhere: the audio was received, the model answered, every
component did its job. With it you can ask whether the turns people complained about were ended by a
threshold or by a button, and those two answers send you to completely different code.

**And be clear about the stakes, because they are not what they look like on this page.** Everything
here is five items against three in a list. But a turn boundary is the moment the system starts
talking, and how that moment is chosen determines how the thing *feels* more than almost anything
else on this day. A voice assistant with a good model and an impatient boundary is experienced as
rude. One with a slow boundary is experienced as stupid. The model is the same model in both. Nobody
who uses it will ever describe the problem as a turn-detection problem — they will say they do not
like talking to it.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"We're sending blobs and nothing else, so we've
picked far-end VAD without saying so anywhere — that's a decision by omission and it needs to be a
decision. The web client has a push-to-talk button, so on that path we already know when the caller
stopped; send `activity_start` and `activity_end` and stop making the model re-derive something we
were told. The telephony path has no button, so it stays on inference and that's fine. Two more
things before this ships. Log which mechanism ended each turn, and the silence duration when it was
inference — the first 'it cut me off' ticket is unanswerable without it, and we'll get that ticket.
And write down what happens when the client's `activity_end` never arrives: right now the far end
waits and nothing anywhere times out, which on a phone line is a caller listening to silence and
hanging up on us."*

**The interview question:** *"On an open microphone, how does the system know the caller has
finished speaking?"* An honest answer: *"It doesn't, unless somebody decides — and there are only
two candidates. Either the far end infers it from the sound, which is voice activity detection: it
classifies short slices as speech or not-speech and treats a long enough run of not-speech as the
end of a turn. Or the client says so explicitly with turn markers. I measured the difference against
a connection I wrote myself: with markers the far end received `ActivityStart`, three audio blobs
and `ActivityEnd` — five things, two of which aren't sound. Without them it received three identical
blobs and nothing else, so everything it knows about the boundary it has to guess from the audio.
The two fail in opposite directions. Inference cuts you off when you pause to think, because a
thinking pause and a finished sentence sound the same, and it holds the floor when a television is
on. Markers move the decision into a client that might be a browser on a bad connection, and a
marker that never arrives leaves the far end waiting for a turn that already ended, with no error
anywhere. So in production it's both — markers wherever the client genuinely knows, like a
push-to-talk button, which is a marker with a person behind it, and inference underneath for the
paths where there's no client to ask, like a phone call over a gateway. And I'd log which one
decided each boundary, because 'it cut me off' is the commonest voice complaint and it's
unanswerable otherwise. One thing I'd flag: I've only measured what the far end is told, not how
good inference is — I never ran a detector on real speech, so I don't have numbers on that."*

# 3.1 — Audio is bytes, and bytes are seconds

## One-line answer

A typed question costs what it says; an open microphone costs **how long it was open**, whether
anybody spoke into it or not — measured today, two seconds of silence went down the wire as
**64,000 bytes** to ask a question that is **20 bytes** as text, which is 3,200 times as much for the
same question, and that one fact is why the rest of this day is about ending sessions rather than
about answering questions.

## The story

You park in town on a Saturday and feed the parking meter. You are only nipping in to collect one
thing, so you pay for the smallest slot the machine will sell you, stick the ticket on the
windscreen, and go.

Inside, the shop is busier than you expected. There is a queue, and then the thing you came for is
not on the shelf, and then somebody has to go and look in the back.

You come out and there is a ticket on your windscreen that you did not put there.

Here is the part worth sitting with. The meter did not care what you were doing. It did not know
whether you were loading a van, unloading a van, sitting in the driver's seat eating a sandwich, or
standing in a queue two streets away while your car did absolutely nothing at all. **The car
occupying the space is the entire transaction.** A car being frantically loaded and unloaded and a
car sitting perfectly still cost exactly the same, because what you bought was the space being
occupied, not anything that happened in it.

Now put a microphone where the car is.

## The idea in plain language

Everything you have paid for in this curriculum so far has been priced by *what you asked*. Day 70
built a router around exactly that unit: a budget denominated in requests per day, where one question
is one request, and you can count them on your fingers. A longer question costs a little more than a
short one, and a question you do not ask costs nothing.

An open microphone does not work like that.

When a live client is holding a line open, it is not waiting for you to finish thinking and then
sending a message. It is sending sound, continuously, in small slices, for as long as the connection
is open. Sound is not words — it is a measurement of air pressure taken many thousands of times a
second and written down as numbers. Silence is a number too. It is zero, taken many thousands of
times a second, and it takes exactly as many bytes to write down as a shout does.

That is the whole idea. **The cost of a voice session is its duration.** A caller who says nothing
for two seconds and a caller who says something urgent for two seconds send the same number of bytes,
because the microphone was open for the same length of time. The parking meter does not know what you
were doing in the shop.

Four words need pinning down before the mechanism, because they all appear in it.

A **blob** — in the sense ADK and the underlying `google.genai` types use it — is a small object
carrying two things: some raw bytes, and a label saying what kind of bytes they are. Part
[1.2](../01-the-open-line/1.2-five-methods.md) met it as the argument to `send_realtime`. It is the
envelope; the sound is what is inside.

A **mime type** is that label. It is the short string the web uses to say what a lump of bytes is —
`text/plain`, `image/png`, and here `audio/pcm;rate=16000`. Without it the bytes are just numbers,
and the far end has no way to know whether it is holding sound, a picture, or nonsense.

**PCM** is what that label says the bytes are. It stands for *pulse-code modulation*, and it is the
plainest possible way of writing down sound: no compression, no cleverness, just the raw
measurements one after another. It is the format a microphone produces before anything has been done
to it, and the reason it is used on a live connection is that it needs no unpacking at the other end.

The **sample rate** is how many of those measurements are taken per second — the `rate=16000` in the
label above means sixteen thousand of them a second. Together with the size of each measurement, it
is what turns a count of bytes into a length of time, and it is the number this part is going to be
careful about, because it is the one figure here that was not measured.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because the desk is about to be something people **talk to**, and the moment it is, every instinct
built up over the last several days about what a request costs stops applying.

Day 74 part 4.2 ran a free-quota check on bidirectional voice and parked the paid model, recording
one fact it could not verify rather than guessing at it. That decision protected the budget. It did
not answer the sizing question, because the sizing question is not *how many requests does a voice
call make* — it is *how long was the line open*, and that is a number nobody can put a bound on from
the outside.

And it is the reason the rest of this day looks the way it does. Part
[4.1](../04-what-it-costs/4.1-a-connection-per-caller.md) asks what capacity means when the resource
is connections held open. Part [4.2](../04-what-it-costs/4.2-the-session-that-never-ended.md) is a
session that nobody closed. Neither of those is interesting in a request/response system, where a
request that nobody is waiting for is at worst a wasted answer. Here, a session nobody is on is a
meter running on an empty parking space, and this part is the number that makes that sentence
expensive rather than merely untidy.

## The mechanism

`audio.py` asks one question — *"why is my pdf blank?"* — twice. Once by microphone, once by
keyboard. Everything else about the two runs is the same agent, the same queue, the same runtime and
the same scripted connection from `_live.py`, which counts every blob, every byte and every whole
message that reaches it.

The first thing to read is the block of constants at the top, because one of them is not like the
others:

```python
# ASSUMPTION, not a measurement: 16-bit samples, one channel, 16000 samples a second, which is
# 32000 bytes for every second of sound. Change it and every derived figure below changes; none of
# the conclusions do.
RATE = 32_000
CHUNK = RATE // 10  # a tenth of a second per blob, which is a common size for a live microphone
SECONDS = 2
QUESTION = "why is my pdf blank?"
DEADLINE = 3.0
```

**Line by line:**

- The comment is doing real work and is quoted here verbatim from the file. `RATE` is **stated**, not
  discovered. Sixteen thousand measurements a second, at two bytes each, is thirty-two thousand bytes
  a second — but the script never checks that against anything. It cannot: the wire format a
  particular model actually wants is a page you read on a date (Principle 7), and a script that
  guessed it would be inventing a figure.
- `RATE = 32_000` uses Python's underscore digit separator, which is ignored by the parser and is
  there so a human reading `32_000` does not have to count zeroes. The value is bytes per second, not
  samples per second — the two get confused constantly, which is why the comment spells out the
  arithmetic that connects them.
- `CHUNK = RATE // 10` is a tenth of a second of sound per blob: 3,200 bytes. `//` is integer
  division, so the result is a whole number of bytes rather than a float, because you cannot send
  half a byte.
- `SECONDS = 2` is how long the microphone is held open. It is the only knob in this file that
  changes what is *measured*; `RATE` only changes what is *derived*.
- `QUESTION` is the same sentence the microphone run is pretending to ask. It exists so the two runs
  are asking the same thing and the comparison is honest.
- `DEADLINE = 3.0` is this day's standing rule: every script gives up rather than hanging, because a
  demonstration that never returns has demonstrated nothing.

Then the sending. This fragment lives inside `drive()`, so it is indented in the file; it is shown
here dedented to column 0 apart from its own inner indentation, and is otherwise character for
character what is on disk:

```python
for _ in range(SECONDS * 10):
    queue.send_realtime(types.Blob(mime_type="audio/pcm;rate=16000", data=b"\x00" * CHUNK))
```

**Line by line:**

- `types.Blob(mime_type=..., data=...)` is the whole of what a blob is: a label and some bytes. Note
  where the label lives — it is **on the message**, not agreed once when the connection opened. Every
  slice of sound carries its own description of what it is, which is what makes it possible for a
  session to carry more than one kind of thing on the same method. Part
  [3.2](3.2-who-says-the-turn-ended.md) is about the two things that come through here and are not
  sound at all.
- `b"\x00" * CHUNK` is 3,200 zero bytes. This is **literal silence**, and that is the point of the
  whole file: nothing was recorded, nobody spoke, no microphone was involved. If the argument only
  worked with a real recording it would be an argument about recordings. It is not. The bytes are
  zeroes and they cost the same as any other bytes.
- `SECONDS * 10` is twenty blobs — ten a second, because `CHUNK` is a tenth of a second's worth. A
  live client does not record you and then upload the file; it sends small slices continuously so the
  far end can start working before you have finished the sentence. How small is a trade the client
  picks: smaller slices mean the far end hears you sooner, and more messages, more headers and more
  work per second of sound. There is no correct answer, only a position on that line.
- `queue.send_realtime(...)` is **not awaited**, and that is not an oversight. `LiveRequestQueue` is a
  place to put things, not a call to a model — putting something in a queue does not wait for
  anything. The runtime is on the other side of that queue, taking items out and handing them to the
  connection's `send_realtime`, which *is* a coroutine. Part
  [1.2](../01-the-open-line/1.2-five-methods.md) walked that seam; this is the caller's end of it.

Now run it both ways:

```bash
cd days/day-75-the-bidi-voice-loop/lab
uv run python audio.py
```

**Line by line:**

- `cd` into the lab because `audio.py` imports `_live` as a plain sibling module, so the working
  directory is what makes the import resolve.
- With no flag, the caller asks by microphone. This is the measurement run.

```text
the caller asked by: microphone
assumed wire format: 32000 bytes per second of sound

  realtime blobs sent        20
  bytes of audio sent        64000
  content messages sent      0
  the question, as text      20 bytes

  seconds of sound (derived) 2.0
  audio bytes per text byte  3200

  Every one of those bytes is silence. Nobody said anything; the microphone was
  open, which is the only thing an open microphone needs to be in order to cost
  you something. A voice session is priced by its duration, not by its content.
```

Then the ablation — the same question, typed:

```bash
uv run python audio.py --text
```

**Line by line:**

- `--text` swaps twenty `send_realtime` calls for one `send_content` call. Nothing else in the
  program changes: same agent, same queue, same runtime, same deadline. That is what makes it an
  ablation rather than a different demo — exactly one thing is switched off, so whatever moves in the
  output is attributable to that one thing.

```text
the caller asked by: keyboard (ablation)
assumed wire format: 32000 bytes per second of sound

  realtime blobs sent        0
  bytes of audio sent        0
  content messages sent      1
  the question, as text      20 bytes

  One message, 20 bytes, and the connection carried nothing else. Everything
  about the session is otherwise identical to the run above.
```

Side by side:

| | asked by microphone | asked by keyboard |
| --- | --- | --- |
| realtime blobs sent | 20 | 0 |
| bytes of audio sent | **64,000** | 0 |
| content messages sent | 0 | 1 |
| bytes to ask the question | 64,000 | **20** |

**What is measured and what is arithmetic — read this before quoting any of it.**

The two counts in bold were **measured**. Real `types.Blob` objects went into a real
`LiveRequestQueue`, the real ADK runtime took them out, and the connection on the far end added up
`len(data)` as each one arrived. Nobody typed 64,000 into the script. The same is true of the blob
count, the content-message count and the 20 bytes, which is `len(QUESTION.encode("utf-8"))` on the
actual string.

The **ratio is arithmetic on measurements**: 64,000 ÷ 20 = 3,200. Both of its inputs were counted, so
it stands on its own. It says that asking this question with an open microphone put 3,200 times as
many bytes on the wire as typing it.

**One figure in that output depends on the assumption, and it is `seconds of sound`.** It is
64,000 ÷ `RATE` = 64,000 ÷ 32,000 = 2.0, and `RATE` is the number the file's own comment labels
*"ASSUMPTION, not a measurement"*. Change `RATE` and 2.0 changes. Nothing else in either run moves —
not the blob count, not the byte count, not the 3,200.

One coincidence, flagged so nobody builds on it: 64,000 ÷ 20 blobs is 3,200 bytes per blob, which is
also `CHUNK`, and the bytes-per-text-byte ratio is *also* 3,200. Those two 3,200s are unrelated. They
match only because the question happens to be 20 bytes long and the run happens to send 20 blobs.
Rename the question and one of them moves.

## When it breaks

**The assumption is the honest limit, and it is worth being precise about what it limits.** Set
`RATE` to 16,000 and the run reports one second of sound; set it to 64,000 and it reports half a
second. The bytes did not change and neither did the argument — the microphone still cost its
duration, and the typed question still cost 20 bytes. What changes is only the translation from a
count of bytes into a length of time. That is the correct blast radius for a stated assumption: it
should be able to be wrong without taking a conclusion down with it.

**The real wire format is four numbers, not one, and it is per-model and per-direction.** Sample rate
(how many measurements a second), sample width (how many bytes each measurement takes), channel count
(one microphone or two), and encoding (raw PCM or something compressed). The model you send *to* may
want different values from the ones it sends *back*. There is no universal default and no way to
derive it from the library.

**And a client that guesses wrong does not get an error.** This is the nasty part. Bytes are bytes.
If you send sound recorded at one rate and label it as another, the far end will happily interpret it
— and hear something that sounds like it was recorded at the wrong speed, or like static. There is no
exception, no HTTP status, no traceback. There is a transcript full of plausible nonsense, and an
afternoon spent looking at your prompt.

Which is exactly why these four numbers belong in a **dated row in the repository**, next to the
documentation page they were read off and the date they were read — Principle 7, in the shape it
takes for audio. A wire format held in somebody's memory is a wire format that will be wrong the
first time the provider ships a new model, and nothing will fail loudly when it is.

**And this lab does not measure the return leg at all.** Everything above is audio *in*. Audio *out*
is bytes too, and a spoken answer is priced by its duration in the same way — a model that talks for
longer costs more than one that gets to the point, regardless of what it says. Today's scripted
connection replies in text (`response_modalities=["TEXT"]`), so there is not a single byte of output
audio in any of these numbers. Treat the 64,000 as the floor of a voice session's cost, not the total.

## In production

**The cost line of a voice service is duration, and that reshapes the levers.** In a request/response
service you get cheaper by making fewer or smaller requests. Neither helps here. The things that
actually move the bill are: **ending sessions promptly** — part
[4.2](../04-what-it-costs/4.2-the-session-that-never-ended.md) is the failure that happens when
nothing does, and it is a leak measured in seconds that nobody is watching; **not opening the line
early**, because a session opened when the page loads is billed from the page load rather than from
the first word; and **not holding it through silence**, because silence is full price.

**The general shape, which is worth saying out loud in a design review: a caller who says nothing
costs the same as a caller who says a lot.** Every intuition from text billing points the other way.
A user who opens your voice feature, gets distracted and wanders off is not a cheap user. They are
one of the most expensive users you have, because they are the one who never hangs up.

**So the metric worth putting on the dashboard is connected-seconds, not requests.** Day 70's router
budgets in requests per day, which is the right unit for everything built up to now and the wrong one
for this: one voice call is one request and can cost anything at all. Counting calls tells you
nothing about your bill. Sum of seconds connected — per caller, per tenant, per day — is the number
that tracks the money, and its companion is the distribution rather than the mean: the long sessions
are where the leak lives, and an average hides them by construction.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Your dashboard counts voice calls. Delete that
panel and count connected-seconds instead — one call can be a couple of seconds or it can be a laptop
lid that never got closed, and the count reads identically for both. Then tell me three things: when
we open the connection relative to the user actually speaking, what closes it if they say nothing at
all, and where the sample rate, sample width, channels and encoding are written down. If the answer
to the last one is 'in the client somewhere', put them in a dated table in the repo with the doc page
we read them off, because when we get it wrong we won't get an exception — we'll get a transcript
full of confident nonsense and lose an afternoon to it."*

**The interview question:** *"How is a voice agent's cost different from a chat agent's?"* An honest
answer: *"A chat turn costs what was said; a voice session costs how long the line was open. I
measured it on a scripted connection — two seconds of held-open microphone sent 64,000 bytes through
the real queue and the real runtime, for a question that is 20 bytes as text. Same question, 3,200
times the bytes, and every one of those bytes was silence, because I sent literal zeroes. I should be
precise about which half of that is measured: the byte counts and the ratio are counted on the far
end of the connection, but turning 64,000 bytes into 'two seconds' relies on a stated assumption
about the wire format — sixteen thousand samples a second at two bytes each — because the real format
is per-model and per-direction and is something you read off a documentation page on a date, not
something the code can discover. Change that assumption and the seconds change; the conclusion
doesn't. And the conclusion is what changes the architecture: capacity is connections held open
rather than requests per second, the metric is connected-seconds, and every session needs a deadline,
because a caller who says nothing costs exactly as much as a caller who says a lot."*

**One.** Change `RATE` in `audio.py` to `16_000` and run the microphone case again. Write down, before

# 2.3 — What the client does with it

## One-line answer

Reading the `interrupted` flag is the easy half; acting on it is three things **in this order** — stop
the player, throw away the audio you have already queued, then render whatever comes next — and only
the first is unambiguously code, because the third is a decision about what an abandoned answer
contributes to the conversation's transcript and there is no default worth inheriting.

## The story

You are at the supermarket checkout with a full trolley, unloading it onto the belt while the cashier
scans. You have got most of the way down the trolley when you look at the last few things you put on
and change your mind — the second bag of rice, the big tub of yoghurt you will never finish.

"Sorry — I've changed my mind about the last few, I'll put them back."

The cashier stops scanning straight away. No argument, no delay: you said stop and the scanning
stopped.

And the belt keeps moving. The rice and the yoghurt are already on it, and they carry on travelling
towards the till exactly as before, because a belt does not know what you just said. Somebody has to
lift them off. Until somebody does, the two of you stand there watching the things you have both
agreed you do not want arrive one after another.

Nothing here is broken. The cashier did the right thing instantly. It is just that stopping the
person and stopping the things already in motion are two different acts, and only the first one
happened when you spoke. And then there is the receipt, which is a third question again: does the
yoghurt appear on it or not?

## The idea in plain language

**A note before anything else, because it changes how you should read this part.** Part
[2.2](2.2-the-event-that-says-stop.md) measured the event: it ran the same call twice and counted
what the caller got with the flag honoured and with it ignored. This part has **no measurement of its
own**, and it is not going to pretend otherwise. The lab has no speaker, no audio player and no
buffer — it counts chunks in a terminal. Everything below about what a *client* does is reasoning
and field knowledge, not a run. Where you would reasonably want evidence, you will find the exact
thing to go and do, marked `TODO(me)`, rather than an output block that would have to be invented.

Two words first, because the rest of the part leans on them.

**Playback** is the act of turning sound data into sound: your program hands bytes to whatever in the
system makes noise, and from that moment the noise is happening whether your program is paying
attention or not.

**A buffer** is a holding area for data that has arrived but has not been used yet. A voice client
has one because audio arrives in pieces and must come out as a continuous sound: if the client played
each piece the instant it arrived and then waited for the next, the caller would hear gaps. So the
client keeps a small run-up of audio ahead of what is being spoken. That run-up is exactly the belt
with the rice on it.

Now the flag arrives. Part [2.1](2.1-the-caller-interrupts.md) established that the caller talking
over the answer is normal rather than an error, and 2.2 established that one event and nothing else
reports it. Reading that event is a single `if`. What follows is three separate acts, and people
build the first, sometimes build the second, and usually never notice the third exists.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because the hub's build brief has this as a `TODO(me)` — *handle the `interrupted` event, and decide
what your client does when it arrives* — and it is phrased that way deliberately. If it were only
"handle the event", the honest answer would be five lines and no thought. The reason the brief points
at this part is that one of the three items is not code the curriculum can write for you.

There is also a Sutra-shaped trap sitting here. `sutra/voice.py` is going to be the desk's voice front
end, and the desk already has a conversation history that every later turn is prompted with. A voice
client that stops its speaker correctly and writes the abandoned answer into that history has fixed
the thing the caller can hear and broken the thing the model reads. That is a worse bug than the one
it fixed, because it is silent.

## The mechanism

There is no run to show you here. What there is instead is one line from `lab/_live.py`, the ordering
argument, and the table at the end that tells you what you can and cannot claim afterwards.

### 1 — Stop the player

The model stopping production does not stop a speaker. These are two different machines with a
network between them, and the only thing the flag has done is travel from one to the other.

By the time your code reads the event, some quantity of the abandoned answer has already been handed
to the thing that makes noise. That audio is going to be heard. Nothing about the model going quiet
reaches into the caller's device and silences it; the only thing that can do that is your client,
explicitly, on purpose.

This is the one of the three that is unambiguously code. Whatever your platform's player is, it has
some way of being told to stop, and the handler for the `interrupted` event calls it. That is the
whole of item one, and it is why most people believe interruption handling is easy — they have done
the visible part and stopped.

Here is the line from the lab's scripted connection that produces the event, quoted exactly as it is
on disk with the common leading whitespace removed so it sits at column 0:

```python
yield LlmResponse(interrupted=True, turn_complete=False)
```

**Line by line:**

- `yield` — the connection's `receive()` hands this out in the same stream as the content chunks, so
  a client that is looping over responses meets it in sequence, not on a side channel. There is
  nothing to subscribe to.
- `interrupted=True` answers **"is anything more coming?"** — no. The turn is over. This is the field
  your handler branches on.
- `turn_complete=False` answers a different question — **"did it reach the end?"** — and the answer
  is also no. The turn is over *and* it did not finish, which are not the same statement.
- The two together are the whole reason a client cannot get away with watching one field. A client
  that only checks `turn_complete` sees `False`, concludes the answer is still in progress, and waits
  politely for the rest of a sentence that nobody is going to produce. A client that only checks
  `interrupted` knows to stop but has no record that what it played was a fragment — which is item
  three's problem, arriving early.
- There is no text, no content and no parts on this response. It is not something to render. Its
  entire payload is the two booleans.

### 2 — Discard the buffer, before you render anything new

Item one stopped the sound that was in the air. It did nothing about the queue behind it.

A client that stops its player and leaves the queued audio in place has built a trap for itself. The
player is stopped, so nothing plays — for a moment. Then the caller's new question is answered, new
audio arrives, and the client starts the player again to speak it. The player picks up from what is
in the queue, and what is at the front of the queue is the tail of the answer the caller already
talked over.

So the caller hears the last part of the abandoned answer, and *then* the new one. Read that back
slowly, because it is worse than it sounds. It is not merely that they heard something they did not
want — they heard it **out of order**, detached from the sentence it belonged to, immediately after
asking about something else. It is the same wrong words as the do-nothing client, rearranged into
something that sounds less like a machine that ignored you and more like a machine that has lost
track of which conversation it is in. Doing nothing at least produces a coherent wrong answer.

That is why the one-line answer states an order rather than a list. Stop, discard, render is not
three independent chores you can do in any sequence — it is a sequence, and the failure that comes
from getting it wrong is not a smaller version of the failure that comes from skipping it. It is a
different, more confusing failure.

The practical shape of item two: the discard has to happen in the handler for the `interrupted`
event, not lazily "before the next play call", because the next play call is exactly the moment when
it is too late to tell the difference between stale audio and fresh audio. They are the same kind of
bytes.

### 3 — Decide what the transcript says

The third item is not code at all until you have made a decision, and the decision has no default
worth inheriting.

An answer that was produced, transmitted and half-played is three different claims, and they are not
interchangeable. The model produced a certain amount. The connection carried some of it before the
flag. The speaker played some smaller amount of that before you stopped it. In a text interface these
three collapse into one fact — *the assistant answered* — and on an interruptible voice loop they
come apart.

So: does the half-spoken answer go into the conversation's history?

**If it does**, the model believes it said things the caller may never have heard. On the next turn
it will refer back to them — *"as I mentioned, you'll need the reference number"* — and the caller,
who talked over that exact sentence, hears an assistant confidently reminding them of something that
was never said to them. The model is not hallucinating. It is reading a record that is wrong.

**If it does not**, the caller can refer to something the model has no record of. They heard the
first half, interrupted precisely *because* they had heard enough, and their next sentence is a
follow-up to it — *"no, the second option"* — arriving attached to a history in which no options
were ever offered.

Both branches are defensible and neither is free, which is the reliable sign that it is a decision
rather than a default. **This curriculum does not pick one for you.** The hub's build brief carries it
as a `TODO(me)`, alongside a test in `tests/test_voice.py` for whichever branch you take, because a
test is how a decision survives the person who made it.

You have met this fork before. Day 74 part 5.1 asked it about a reader who closed a tab half-way
through a streamed answer, and found that the session kept the question and no reply at all. That is
the same question with one person in the room. This is the same question with two — and it is harder
here, because a reader who closes a tab is gone, while a caller who interrupts is still on the line
and will speak next.

### What you can claim afterwards

For one interrupted turn, these four can all give different answers, and a system that records only
one of them cannot reconstruct the others:

| Question about one interrupted turn | What it can say |
| --- | --- |
| What the model did | produced up to the point it was told to stop — which is more than left the server |
| What the connection reported | the chunks that were actually sent, plus the flag: fewer than were produced |
| What the caller heard | only what reached a speaker before playback stopped: fewer still, and unknown to the server |
| What the transcript says | whatever you decided in item three — and nothing forces it to match any of the three above |

The row that catches people is the third, because it is the only one nobody on the server side can
observe. What the caller heard is a fact that exists only on the client, and if the client does not
report it, no amount of server logging will recover it.

## When it breaks

**Stopping the player is not instant either, and this part cannot tell you how far from instant.**
Your program hands sound onward, and what it hands it to has holding areas of its own before anything
physically makes noise. Telling it to stop takes effect at the front of that chain; a residue behind
the point of control still comes out. On most systems that residue is short enough not to matter and
on some it is not, and the honest position is that this is a property of the specific stack you
build on, which you measure rather than assume. Nothing in this lab measures it.

**Flushing one buffer is not flushing all of them.** A real client usually has audio sitting in more
than one place: whatever the network layer has received but not yet processed, whatever has been
prepared for playback, and whatever the sound device is already holding. "Discard the buffer" is a
single sentence describing a job with as many steps as your client has holding areas, and a client
that clears the obvious one and forgets a quieter one produces exactly the out-of-order failure item
two describes, only intermittently, which is much harder to find.

**Discarding audio and discarding the record are different discards.** If your answer to item three
is "the abandoned answer goes into history, marked incomplete", then item two must throw away the
*sound* while keeping the *text*. Those live in different places for a reason, and a handler that
treats "flush everything" as one operation will quietly delete the thing you decided to keep.

**And the ordering argument above is reasoning, not a measurement.** It follows from what a queue is
and it matches what people who run voice systems report, but this part did not build a client, play a
sound, interrupt it and record what came out of the speaker. If you want it as evidence rather than
as an argument, the `TODO(me)` in *Check yourself* is exactly the experiment, and it is small.

## In production

**Record what was delivered separately from what was produced, and get the client to tell you.** Part
2.2 arrived at this from the cost side; item three above is the other reason for it. Once you accept
that the four rows in that table can differ, "the assistant said X" stops being a single field in your
logs. Support questions are answered by the *heard* row, cost questions by the *produced* row, and
debugging questions by the *sent* row. A voice system that logs one number is guessing at the other
two, and the heard row in particular has to be sent up from the client because the server has no way
to see it.

**The transcript decision belongs in a docstring, not in a handler.** Whichever branch of item three
you take, the next person to read `sutra/voice.py` will encounter its consequences before they
encounter its reasoning — a history containing a truncated assistant turn, or a history with a gap
where an answer plainly was. Write the two sentences: what your client stores after an interruption,
and why. If you cannot write the second sentence, you inherited a default rather than made a choice.

**What changes at scale is that interruptions stop being an edge case.** On a system nobody uses, an
interrupted turn is a curiosity. On a system with real callers it is one of the most common ways a
turn ends, because interrupting is how people steer conversations — that is what the day's paper
found in ordinary human talk, and it does not stop being true because one party is a model. Which
means the code path handling it is a **main** path, tested like one, and the fraction of turns ending
in an interruption is a metric worth watching: a sudden rise says your answers got longer or worse,
and no eval will tell you that.

**And the buffer you are discarding has a size you should know.** Part
[3.1](../03-what-goes-down-the-wire/3.1-audio-is-bytes.md) is about audio being priced by duration
rather than by content, and the same arithmetic answers a question item two raises: how much sound is
in your queue when the flag arrives? A queue measured in bytes is a queue measured in seconds of
speech, and how many seconds it is decides whether the out-of-order failure is a small oddity or a
whole sentence. Part [4.3](../04-what-it-costs/4.3-what-a-real-voice-system-adds.md) covers what else
a real voice client has to carry beyond this.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"The handler stops the player and that's it. Two
things missing. First, flush the queued audio in the same handler, before anything can start playing
again — otherwise the next answer gets the tail of the old one in front of it, and that's a stranger
bug than the one you're fixing. Second, and this is the one I actually want an answer to: what goes
into the session history for a turn that got cut off half-way? Pick a branch, put it in the docstring
with the reason, and write the test. And have the client report how much it actually played, because
right now the only thing we can say about what the caller heard is that it was somewhere between
nothing and everything we sent."*

**The interview question:** *"You get the interruption event. What does your client actually do?"* An
honest answer: *"Three things, and only one of them is obvious. Stop the player, because the model
going quiet doesn't reach into the caller's speaker — whatever's already been handed to the audio
device will be heard unless something stops it. Then discard whatever audio is queued behind it, and
do that before you render anything new, because otherwise the player picks up the tail of the
abandoned answer in front of the new one — same wrong words, but now out of order, which sounds worse
than just ignoring the interruption. I'll be straight that the ordering point is reasoning about what
a queue is rather than something I measured; I measured the event arriving, not a speaker. And the
third thing isn't code until you've decided it: whether the half-spoken answer goes into the
conversation history. If it does, the model thinks it said things the caller never heard and will
refer back to them. If it doesn't, the caller refers to something the model has no record of. Both are
defensible, neither is free, so it goes in the docstring with a test on it. The related thing I'd add
is that what the model produced, what the connection sent and what the caller actually heard are three
different numbers, and only the client can report the third."*

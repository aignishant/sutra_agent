# 2.1 — The caller interrupts

## One-line answer

Being interrupted is not an error the voice loop has to survive — it is **the reason the line is
held open at all**, and mechanically it is one question asked between chunks: *did anything arrive
while we were talking?*

## The story

You take the same coach every week. It pulls out, and the recorded safety announcement starts:
doors, seat belts, where the hammer is, what to do if the driver is unwell. You know it. You could
say it along with the speaker.

A few sentences in, a passenger near the front works out that they are on the wrong coach — this one
does not stop at their town. They want to say so *now*, while there is still a roundabout to turn
at.

With the recording, they cannot. There is nobody to say it to. The announcement is a thing that was
decided before they got on, and it plays to the end whether or not it is still the most important
thing happening on this coach. By the time it finishes and the passenger can catch the driver's eye,
the coach is on the bypass.

Now put a driver saying it live instead. The passenger says *"sorry — does this stop at Aldby?"* The
driver stops mid-sentence and answers. And here is the interesting part, the part that is actually a
decision somebody has to make: **what happens to the rest of the announcement?** The driver either
picks it up where it broke off, or starts it again, or decides it does not matter and never finishes
it. Whichever one, the sentence that was cut in half was still said out loud, and everyone on the
coach heard half of it.

Nothing went wrong in the second version. Being talked over is not a fault in the driver. It is what
a live announcement is *for* — it can be steered, and a recording cannot.

## The idea in plain language

**Barge-in** is the term for this: the caller starts speaking while the system is still speaking,
and the system stops. The word comes from telephony, where a caller who already knows the menu wants
to press on without sitting through it.

Every system built in this curriculum before Day 74 was the recording. You send a request, the
server produces an answer, and only then is it your turn again. There is no mechanism for
interrupting because there is no channel to interrupt *on*: the caller literally has no way to say
anything until the answer has finished arriving. That is not a design decision anyone made. It falls
out of the shape.

Part [1.1](../01-the-open-line/1.1-a-call-not-a-request.md) changed the shape. Both parties now hold
the line at the same time, and either can speak whenever they like. The moment that is true,
somebody talking over somebody else stops being an edge case and becomes the ordinary way a
conversation gets steered — the way you stop a colleague who is explaining something you already
know.

| Shape | What the caller can do mid-answer | What the producer can do about it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Request/response (everything up to Day 73) | Nothing. The channel is one-way and it is not their turn | Nothing to do; it produces the whole answer and hands it over |
| One-way stream (Day 74, SSE) | Leave. That is the entire vocabulary | Notice the reader went away, if it bothers to check |
| Bidirectional open line (today) | Speak, at any moment, including mid-word | Hear it, and stop |

Read the third row against the first. The capability that arrived is not "the answer comes out in
pieces" — Day 74 already had that. It is that **the caller has something to say and a way to say it
while you are still talking.** A voice assistant that cannot be stopped is one nobody talks to
twice, because the only way to escape a wrong answer is to sit through all of it.

So the question this section answers is not *how do we defend against interruptions.* It is *where,
in a program with two things happening at once, does the decision to stop live?*

None of this is new to anyone who has studied actual conversation, which is why the day ends with a
paper about it: [A simplest systematics for the organization of turn-taking for
conversation](../../papers/01-turn-taking.md) is an account of how people hand the floor back and
forth with nobody allocating it, and it treats two speakers overlapping as a normal and briefly
tolerated state rather than a breakdown. Read it after the parts, the way Principle 4 says — build
the turn-taking first, then read the study of it.

## Why Sutra needs it

The day's build brief asks for one function in `sutra/voice.py` that owns a voice session from hello
to hang-up, and one of its `TODO(me)` lines is *handle the `interrupted` event, and decide what your
client does when it arrives.* That is phrased as a decision rather than as a fix on purpose. There
is no correct default to inherit: stopping playback, discarding what you have already buffered, and
deciding what the half-spoken answer becomes in the transcript are three separate choices, and part
[2.3](2.3-what-the-client-does.md) is where they get made.

This part is the one that has to land first, because a reader who thinks of an interruption as an
error condition will write it as one — a `try`, a log line at `WARNING`, a metric called
`interruption_rate` that somebody puts on a dashboard as a health signal. On a voice line a rising
interruption rate is not a health signal. It might mean the answers are too long, or it might mean
the callers are engaged enough to steer. It is a fact about a conversation, not a fault.

The hub's trap 5 says the same thing in one line, and this is the part it points at.

## The mechanism

Everything today runs against `lab/_live.py`, a `BaseLlmConnection` of our own at the far end of a
real `Runner.run_live`. It has a scripted six-piece reply and one switch, `interruptible`, and the
whole of interruption is six lines inside its `receive()` method.

The fragment below is quoted from `ScriptedConnection.receive` in `lab/_live.py`. On disk it sits
inside a method and inside a `for` loop, so it is indented by sixteen spaces; it is dedented to
column zero here and is otherwise unchanged, comment included.

```python
# The interruption check happens *between* chunks, because that is the only place a
# speaker can stop: mid-word is not a turn boundary for anybody.
if self.interruptible and not self.inbox.empty():
    self.interruptions += 1
    yield LlmResponse(interrupted=True, turn_complete=False)
    break
```

**Line by line:**

- `not self.inbox.empty()` **is the entire detection.** `inbox` is the queue that everything the
  caller sends lands in — a typed message, a transcript, a blob of audio. A queue that was empty is
  no longer empty, so something arrived while we were talking. That is all "the caller interrupted"
  means mechanically. There is no analysis of intent, no comparison of voices, no model deciding
  whether the caller meant to take the floor. Something arrived; we were mid-answer; that is the
  event.
- `self.interruptible and ...` is the ablation switch, and it is here rather than higher up so that
  the two runs differ in exactly one condition. Part [2.2](2.2-the-event-that-says-stop.md) turns it
  off and measures what the caller sits through.
- **The check sits *between* chunks, and that placement is the design.** A chunk is the smallest
  thing that has already been committed to — by the time this loop is inside a chunk, that piece of
  the answer exists and, on a speaker, is on its way to being audible. There is no way to un-say
  half a word. So a speaker can stop at a chunk boundary and nowhere else, and the code asks its
  question exactly there. Everything about how responsive barge-in feels follows from how big a
  chunk is.
- `self.interruptions += 1` is a counter on the connection, so a script can read afterwards how many
  times the model was cut off. Counting on the connection rather than in the consumer matters: it is
  evidence from the far end, independent of whatever the client believed happened.
- `yield LlmResponse(interrupted=True, turn_complete=False)` carries **two facts, not one.**
  `interrupted=True` says *the turn is over.* `turn_complete=False` says *it did not finish.* Those
  are different claims and they are both needed: the first is what tells a client to stop playing
  audio it is already holding, and the second is what stops the half-spoken sentence being filed as
  a complete answer. Part [2.3](2.3-what-the-client-does.md) uses one for the speaker and the other
  for the transcript. Leaving `turn_complete` at its default would not be a shortcut; it would be a
  missing fact.
- `break` abandons the four chunks of `REPLY` that were never sent. In this scripted connection that
  is a `for` loop ending early. **In a real model it is the server deciding to stop generating** —
  and that is a different thing from the client deciding to stop listening. Worth its own sentence,
  because the two get conflated: the model stops **producing**, the client stops **playing**, and
  there is a network between those two events. Both have to happen. Neither one implies the other.
- The `break` also skips the `for` loop's `else:` branch a few lines below, which is what would
  normally emit the aggregated `turn_complete=True` event at the end of a turn. That is why an
  interrupted turn produces no completed turn: not because something suppressed it, but because the
  loop never reached the end.

Now the line that makes any of this observable. A few lines below, at the bottom of the same loop:

```python
# Yield to the event loop so a caller's input can actually arrive mid-answer.
# Without this the whole reply is emitted before anything else in the program runs,
# and the interruption above could never happen.
await asyncio.sleep(0)
```

**Line by line:**

- The **event loop** is the single scheduler underneath all of this. It runs one piece of async code
  at a time and switches to another piece only when the running one says *I am waiting for
  something.* Nothing is running in parallel; things take turns, and a piece of code that never
  waits never gives anyone else a turn.
- `asyncio.sleep(0)` sleeps for no time whatsoever. Its only effect is the `await`: control goes
  back to the loop, anything else that is ready gets to run, and then we come back here. In this
  program the "anything else" is the runtime's send loop, taking the caller's message off
  `LiveRequestQueue` and putting it into `inbox`.
- Without it, the `for chunk in REPLY` loop runs start to finish without the scheduler getting a
  look in. The caller's message would still be sitting in the queue, unmoved, at every single check
  — so `inbox.empty()` would be true every time and the interruption above could never fire. The
  detection would be correct and the demonstration would show nothing.

**Be honest about what that line is.** It is a property of the simulation, not a technique to copy.
A real model produces its chunks over time, arriving over a network, and every read of that socket
is a genuine wait — so the yield happens by itself, dozens of times a turn, without anybody writing
it. `asyncio.sleep(0)` is the scripted connection paying back the time it did not spend. A fake that
skips it is a fake that has quietly made interruption impossible, which is exactly the class of bug
that makes a test suite green and a product broken.

The caller side is `lab/barge_in.py`, and it is deliberately blunt: the consumer counts chunks, and
after `PATIENCE = 2` of them it sends a message — *"yes, I know — the fonts"* — as a normal input on
the same queue it used to ask the question. Nothing about sending it is special. It is the same call
as the first one. The interruption is not a signal or a control frame; it is just the caller
speaking at a moment when the model happened to be speaking too.

Part [2.2](2.2-the-event-that-says-stop.md) runs that script twice, with the switch on and off, and
measures what the caller heard: three chunks against twelve. That measurement belongs there, and so
does the output.

## When it breaks

**The detection cannot tell you *what* arrived.** `not self.inbox.empty()` is true for a caller
taking the floor, and it is equally true for a stray keep-alive, a duplicated transcript, or the
client re-sending something it already sent. On a real audio line the nastiest version of this is
echo: the microphone picks up the model's own voice coming out of the speaker, the client dutifully
forwards it as caller audio, and the model interrupts itself. It stops talking because it heard
itself talking. Part [4.3](../04-what-it-costs/4.3-what-a-real-voice-system-adds.md) is where echo
cancellation and voice activity detection sit, and this is the failure they exist to prevent.

**Chunk size sets the floor on how fast stopping can be.** The check only runs at a boundary, so a
model that emits one big chunk per sentence cannot stop inside a sentence, however quickly the
caller speaks. The caller experiences that as being ignored for the rest of the sentence. Smaller
chunks buy responsiveness and cost more events, and that trade is real rather than theoretical.

**And the failure with the switch off has no error text at all.** That is the honest finding here
and it is worth stating plainly: run the same call against a connection that does not honour
interruptions and there is no exception, no traceback, no HTTP status, no log line, and nothing
anywhere in the event stream saying stop. The caller talks, the model keeps talking, and on a
speaker that is two voices at once. There is nothing to paste, because nothing is emitted. The only
way to catch it is to assert on what the caller actually received — which is what part
[2.2](2.2-the-event-that-says-stop.md) does, with both runs' output side by side.

## In production

**What changes at scale is that interruptions stop being rare.** On a demo with one friendly tester,
barge-in is a feature you show off. On a live service the callers are impatient, the answers are
sometimes wrong in the first sentence, and a meaningful share of turns end early. That changes what
the numbers mean. Average answer length stops being a property of the model and becomes a property
of the audience. A "turns completed" metric quietly under-counts every steered conversation. And the
transcript store fills with fragments rather than answers, which the next thing that reads it — an
evaluator, a summariser, the session history handed back to the model on the next turn — was
probably not written for.

**Two decisions a professional makes before the first caller, not after.**

The first: **does an interruption cancel the model's work, or only stop its delivery?** The `break`
above is a scripted loop ending, but a real provider is generating tokens on a machine somewhere,
and possibly running a tool call that has already left the building. Stopping the audio is cheap.
Cancelling the generation needs the provider to support it, and cancelling a tool call that has
already written to a database is not cancellation at all — it is a compensating action somebody has
to write. You are also still billed for whatever was produced before the stop, so "interrupted
early" does not mean free. Decide, write it down, and make the tool layer's side of it explicit; Day
60's work on durable execution is the same question wearing different clothes.

The second: **does a half-spoken answer count as having been said?** The driver on the coach got
half a sentence out and every passenger heard it. Your model got three chunks out and the caller
heard them. If the transcript records nothing, then on the next turn the model has no idea it said
anything, and may cheerfully repeat itself — which to the caller is the system ignoring them twice.
If the transcript records the whole intended answer, the history now claims the caller was told
things they never heard. Neither is obviously right. The workable answer is usually to store the
fragment, marked as a fragment, which is precisely what `turn_complete=False` is *for* — and it is
why that field being set alongside `interrupted` is not redundant. Part
[2.3](2.3-what-the-client-does.md) turns this into code.

**A third thing that only shows up under load:** the interruption event races the audio the client
already has in its buffer. The client is playing sound it was handed a moment ago, so "stop" always
arrives while there is still a queue of words to come out of the speaker. Stopping the stream and
forgetting the buffer means the caller hears several more words *after* the system supposedly
stopped, which reads as the system arguing with them. That is a client-side bug with a server-side
event, and it is trap 7 on the hub.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"This wraps the interrupted event in a try/except
and logs it at WARNING, which tells me we think it's a fault. It isn't — it's the caller steering
the conversation, and it'll be a large fraction of our turns. Two things to change. Take it out of
the error path and handle it as a normal event, and drop the `interruption_rate` alert: a rise in
that number could mean our answers got too long or it could mean callers are engaged, and paging
someone at 3am over it will teach them to ignore the pager. Second, you're only acting on
`interrupted` — also read `turn_complete`. Right now the half-spoken turn goes into the session as a
completed assistant message, so on the next turn the model believes it finished a sentence the
caller cut off, and it won't offer the rest. Store the fragment and mark it as one. And please write
down, in the module docstring, whether we cancel generation upstream or just stop playing — I can't
tell from the code, and the billing answer is different for each."*

**The interview question:** *"You're building a voice assistant. Why is interruption a first-class
feature rather than an error case?"* An honest answer: *"Because it's the reason you hold a line
open at all. In request/response the caller can't interrupt — there's no channel to interrupt on,
they have no way to say anything until the answer's finished arriving. The moment both sides can
speak whenever they like, someone talking over the other is the normal way a conversation gets
steered, not a fault. I built it against a scripted connection: the detection is literally 'is the
inbox non-empty' checked between chunks, because a chunk is the smallest thing already committed to
— there's no un-saying half a word. So chunk size sets the floor on how fast you can stop. The event
carries two facts, `interrupted=True` and `turn_complete=False`, and you need both: one tells the
client to stop playing, the other tells the transcript this wasn't a finished answer. The two things
I'd decide before shipping are whether an interruption cancels the model's work upstream or only
stops delivery — you're billed for what was produced either way, and an in-flight tool call can't
just be un-run — and whether a half-spoken answer counts as having been said, because if it doesn't
go in the history the model will repeat itself and the caller has now been ignored twice. And the
failure mode with no barge-in is silent: no exception, nothing in the stream, just two voices
talking at once on the speaker."*

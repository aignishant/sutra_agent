# 2.2 — The one event that says stop

## One-line answer

Exactly one thing in the stream says the answer is no longer wanted — an event with `interrupted`
set — and without it the caller sits through **6 of 6** chunks of an answer they already interrupted
and then a second one, **12 chunks** against **3**, with nothing anywhere reporting a problem.

## The story

You are driving and the satnav is talking you through a route you have decided not to take.

You turn off early because you know a better way through the estate, and for the next stretch the
voice carries on with the old plan. *"In two hundred metres, turn left."* There is no left. *"Turn
around when possible."* You are not turning around. The instructions are being delivered clearly, in
order, on time, and every one of them is about a journey that stopped existing when you took the
turning.

Then it says *"recalculating"*, and everything is fine again.

That word is doing something none of the instructions could. It does not tell you where to go. It
tells you that what you were being told is no longer what you are being told, which is the one piece
of information you actually needed — and until it arrives, a device working perfectly is filling the
car with directions to somewhere you are not going.

## The idea in plain language

Part [2.1](2.1-the-caller-interrupts.md) established that on an open line the caller can talk over
the answer, and that the connection detects it: something arrived while the model was talking.

What the model does next is stop producing. What the **client** does next is a different question,
and it is the one that decides whether the caller has a good experience or a ridiculous one — because
the client is not a passive display. It is holding audio it has already been given, part-way through
playing it, with more in a buffer behind that. The model going quiet does not make a speaker go quiet.

So the client needs to be told. And the thing that tells it is an event with `interrupted` set — no
text, no content, nothing to render, just a flag whose entire job is to mean *stop*.

Two properties of that event are worth stating plainly before the measurement.

**It is the only such signal.** Nothing else in the stream distinguishes an abandoned answer from a
short one. The chunks that arrived are the chunks that arrived; the sequence simply stops. Without the
flag, a client that renders whatever it is handed has no way to know the difference.

**And it is not the same as `turn_complete`.** A turn that was interrupted is over, but it did not
finish, and part [2.3](2.3-what-the-client-does.md) needs both facts. The connection in this lab sets
`interrupted=True` and `turn_complete=False` together, which is how it says *that is the end of it,
and it is not an ending*.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because `sutra/voice.py` is going to be the desk's voice front end, and the gate spends a check
reading its source for the word `interrupted`. A `call` that never mentions it is a client that cannot
be interrupted, and — as the measurement below shows — a client that cannot be interrupted does not
fail loudly. It talks.

There is also a Sutra-shaped reason. Day 74 built a streaming client that renders chunks as they
arrive. That client, pointed at a live session, is *almost* right: it handles partials, it handles the
final event, and it will happily go on rendering an answer that the caller has already talked over,
because nothing in the streaming world ever needed a way to say stop.

## The mechanism

The measurement is one call in which the caller listens to two chunks, decides they already know the
answer, and speaks. Everything else — the question, the reply, the connection, the runtime — is
identical between the two runs; the only difference is whether the connection honours the
interruption.

```bash
cd days/day-75-the-bidi-voice-loop/lab
uv run python barge_in.py; echo "exit: $?"
```

**Line by line:**

- `barge_in.py` drives a real `run_live` session and pushes a second message into the live queue after
  the second chunk arrives, which is a caller talking over an answer.
- `echo "exit: $?"` prints the exit code, because the claim is the exit code: `0` when something in
  the stream said stop.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
the connection honours interruptions: yes
the caller spoke after hearing 2 chunks

  of the first answer, heard   3 of 6 chunks
  chunks heard in total        3
  an 'interrupted' event       yes
  the session ended            yes

  the events, in order: ['chunk', 'chunk', 'chunk', 'INTERRUPTED']

  The answer stopped mid-sentence and the stream said so. That event is the only
  thing that tells a client to stop playing audio it has already been handed.
```

Three chunks, then the flag. Note that the caller spoke after **two** and heard **three**: one more
chunk was already on its way, because the check happens between chunks and the one in flight was
already committed to. That is not sloppiness in the lab, it is the shape of the thing — there is no
way to un-say the word you are in the middle of.

Now the same call against a connection that does not honour interruptions:

```bash
uv run python barge_in.py --ignore; echo "exit: $?"
```

**Line by line:**

- `--ignore` constructs the connection with `interruptible=False`. The caller still speaks at the same
  moment; the difference is entirely in what happens after.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
the connection honours interruptions: NO (ablation)
the caller spoke after hearing 2 chunks

  of the first answer, heard   6 of 6 chunks
  chunks heard in total        12
  an 'interrupted' event       NO
  the session ended            yes

  the events, in order: ['chunk', 'chunk', 'chunk', 'chunk', 'chunk', 'chunk', 'turn_complete', 'chunk', 'chunk', 'chunk', 'chunk', 'chunk', 'chunk', 'turn_complete']

  Nothing told the client to stop. The model finished the sentence it was on, and
  the caller listened to an answer they had already interrupted - which on a speaker
  means two people talking at once, and on a screen means text nobody wanted.
```

| | interruptions honoured | ignored |
| --- | --- | --- |
| of the first answer, heard | **3 of 6** | 6 of 6 |
| chunks heard in total | **3** | 12 |
| an `interrupted` event | yes | **no** |
| the session ended | yes | yes |
| exit code | 0 | 1 |

**Twelve chunks against three**, for the same call, with the caller doing exactly the same thing at
exactly the same moment. And read the fourth row: the session ended cleanly in both runs. Nothing
crashed, nothing timed out, nothing was dropped. The ablation is not a broken system — it is a system
that finished everything it started, which is a virtue everywhere except here.

The failure is also perfectly ordered and perfectly correct. Six chunks, a `turn_complete`, six more
chunks, another `turn_complete`: two complete, well-formed answers to two questions that were asked.
On a screen that reads as an assistant that ignored you. On a speaker, where the first answer is still
being played aloud as the second one starts arriving, it is two voices at once.

## When it breaks

**The lab detects an interruption the easy way and a real system does not.** Here, "the caller spoke"
means a message arrived in the connection's inbox — an unambiguous, discrete event. With a live
microphone the equivalent question is *"was that speech, or a door, or the caller's radio?"*, and
answering it is voice activity detection, which part
[3.2](../03-what-goes-down-the-wire/3.2-who-says-the-turn-ended.md) is about. Everything measured here
is what happens *after* that decision; nothing here makes the decision easier.

**Interrupting is not free, and the cost is invisible in this lab.** The three chunks the caller heard
were produced, transmitted and thrown away, and in a real session so was whatever the model had
already generated internally. Day 72 part 5.2 called this class of thing wasted requests; on a voice
loop it is wasted generation, and a caller who interrupts constantly is a caller whose session costs
more than a patient one's.

**And the flag can arrive after the damage.** The event says stop, and by the time it is read the
client may already have handed several seconds of audio to a speaker. Reacting correctly means undoing
something already in flight, which is part 2.3's subject and is genuinely harder than reading a flag.

## In production

**What a voice client does the moment the flag arrives**, in order, because the order matters: stop
playback, discard the buffer, and only then start rendering whatever comes next. A client that does
them in the other order plays the tail of the abandoned answer *after* the new one has started, which
is worse than not handling the interruption at all — it is the same wrong words, rearranged into
something confusing.

**What changes at scale is what you can no longer tell.** In a text interface, "the assistant answered"
is a fact. On an interruptible voice loop, an answer may have been produced, transmitted, half-played
and abandoned, and each of those is a different claim about what the caller actually heard. Teams that
run voice systems end up recording *delivered* separately from *produced*, because support questions
are about the first and cost questions are about the second.

**What a professional decides before writing the handler.** Whether an interrupted answer counts as
having been said — which is not a display question, it is a transcript question. The half-spoken answer
either goes into the conversation's history, in which case the model will believe it said things the
caller may not have heard, or it does not, in which case the caller can refer to something the model
has no record of. Day 74 part 5.1 met the same fork with a reader who closed a tab. Neither branch is
free and the mistake is inheriting one by accident.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Handle the interrupted event, and handle it before
you render anything else — stop the player, flush what's queued, then take the next chunk. Right now
we'd play the tail of the old answer over the start of the new one, which is worse than the bug we're
fixing. Also decide what goes in the transcript for an answer that was cut off half-way, write it in
the docstring, and put a test on it. And record produced chunks separately from delivered ones;
otherwise the first time somebody asks what a caller actually heard, we won't know."*

**The interview question:** *"What happens when a user talks over your voice assistant?"* An honest
answer: *"Two separate things, and people only build the first. The model stops producing — on an open
connection that's the server's decision, and it shows up in the event stream as an `interrupted` flag.
The client stopping is a different act, because it's holding audio it's already been handed and
part-way through playing it. That flag is the only signal that says stop; nothing else distinguishes
an abandoned answer from a short one. I measured it: with it honoured the caller heard three chunks of
a six-chunk answer, and with it ignored they heard all six and then a second complete answer — twelve
chunks against three, with the session ending cleanly both times and no error anywhere. The subtle part
is what the client does on receiving it: stop the player, discard the buffer, then render what comes
next, in that order. And you have to decide whether a half-spoken answer goes in the transcript,
because the model and the caller will otherwise disagree about what was said."*

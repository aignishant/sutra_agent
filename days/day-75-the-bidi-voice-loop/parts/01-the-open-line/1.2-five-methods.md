# 1.2 — Five methods and a context manager

## One-line answer

A live model, to ADK, is exactly **five methods behind a context manager** — `BaseLlm.connect`
returns an async context manager that hands you a `BaseLlmConnection`, and that connection is
`send_history`, `send_content`, `send_realtime`, `receive` and `close` — so anything that implements
those five can be driven by the real runtime, which is the whole reason this day costs nothing while
`Runner.run_live`, `LiveRequestQueue` and every event stay exactly as they ship.

## The story

The neighbour two doors down is going away for the week and asks whether you would look after the
cat. You say yes before you have thought about it, and then you think about it, and you start
worrying. You have never had a cat. You do not know what a cat needs. You picture arriving on the
first evening and standing in somebody else's kitchen with no idea what you are supposed to be doing.

She hands you the key and walks you round the kitchen, and the whole job turns out to be a list on
the side of the fridge, in biro, five lines long.

Dry food, one scoop, from the blue tin. Rinse the water bowl and refill it. Change the litter tray,
bag goes in the outside bin. Look at the bowl before you top it up — if yesterday's is still there,
ring me. Lock the back door and put the key through my letterbox on the last day.

That is it. That is the entire post. Not "understand cats", not "be a cat person" — five things, and
anyone who can do those five things can hold the post for the week. You do not need to know what she
knows. You need to be able to do what she asked, in the order she asked it, and the cat cannot tell
the difference between you and her.

Two things about that list are worth carrying into the rest of this part.

First, four of the lines are things you *do* to the kitchen and one is a thing you *read* out of it.
The bowl is the only way the cat says anything back. You cannot ask it how the week is going. You put
things in, and one small piece of evidence comes out, and that is the whole conversation.

Second, the last line is not like the others. Feeding, water and litter only happen on the days you
turn up. Locking the door happens **no matter what** — whether the cat ate, whether you were in a
rush, whether you got half-way through and the phone rang and you had to go. If there is one line on
that list you are not allowed to skip, it is the one about the door.

Hold on to the fridge list. Everything below is that list, for a model.

## The idea in plain language

Part [1.1](1.1-a-call-not-a-request.md) said that a live session is a call rather than a request:
both sides may speak at any moment, so the program has two things happening at once instead of one
thing waiting on another. That leaves an obvious question hanging. If a live model is not "a function
you call and await", then what *is* it, in code?

The answer is smaller than people expect. ADK does not define a live model as a protocol, a
websocket, an audio codec or a provider. It defines it as an **interface**: a named set of methods
that something must have, with agreed shapes, so that any object carrying those methods can be
dropped in where the interface is expected. The fridge list, in other words.

Three words have to be exact before the signatures make sense, because all three appear in them.

A **coroutine** is a function declared with `async def`. Calling it does not run it; it returns an
object that runs when something `await`s it. The practical reading is *this will take a while and is
allowed to let other work happen while it waits* — which is precisely what sending something over a
network is.

An **async generator** is an `async def` that contains `yield` instead of `return`. It does not
produce one value; it produces a stream of them, one at a time, and the caller reads it with
`async for` rather than `await`. It is the shape you use when you do not know how many things are
coming or when they will arrive.

A **context manager** is an object with a defined entry and a defined exit, used with a `with`
statement, where the exit is guaranteed to run — normally, on an exception, or on cancellation. An
**async context manager** is the same idea for asynchronous code, entered with `async with`, and
whose entry and exit are themselves things you await. It is the fridge list's last line expressed as
a language feature: *the door gets locked no matter how you leave.*

Put those together and the shape of a live model in ADK reads straight off:

- opening a session is an **async context manager** — you enter it, you get a connection, and leaving
  the block closes the connection whatever happened inside it;
- pushing things at the model is **coroutines** — three of them, for three different kinds of thing;
- reading from the model is an **async generator** — one stream, of unknown length, that you sit in an
  `async for` over;
- and hanging up is a coroutine of its own.

Four ways to push, one stream to pull. That asymmetry is not an accident of the library; it is what a
conversation is.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because it is what makes this day exist at all.

Day 74 part 4.1 read `Runner.run_live`'s signature off the installed package, found that a live run
takes a queue you keep pushing into rather than a single message, and then **parked the model** — the
free-tier status of the Live API could not be verified, so the day recorded that fact and refused to
guess (Principle 15, and Principle 10 about the honesty of not guessing). That left the shape of the
program unbuilt, which is the more interesting half.

This part is the way out of that. Because a live model is a five-method interface reached through a
documented seam, the model is the **only** thing that has to be replaced. `Runner.run_live` is real.
`LiveRequestQueue` is real. `RunConfig(response_modalities=...)` is real, the event objects are real,
and the flow that turns queue items into connection calls is the one that ships in the package.
`lab/_live.py` swaps out the far end and nothing else, so every number this day quotes was produced
by the runtime you would deploy, and the request budget in the hub's §6 is zero to every provider.

It matters for the build brief too. `sutra/voice.py` is asked to own a voice session from hello to
hang-up, and you cannot own a lifetime you cannot name. Once you have seen that opening a session is
a context manager and that closing is a method somebody has to call, the two failures the next parts
measure stop being mysterious and start being obvious in hindsight: part
[1.3](1.3-who-ends-the-call.md) is about who calls `close`, and part
[3.2](../03-what-goes-down-the-wire/3.2-who-says-the-turn-ended.md) is about what actually arrives at
`send_realtime`.

## The mechanism

### Ask the package, do not trust this page

Every symbol below was read off the installed `google-adk==2.7.1` on 2026-09-06 with
`inspect.signature`. Here is the command that does it, so that the record is reproducible rather than
quoted:

```bash
uv run python -c "
import inspect
from google.adk.models.base_llm import BaseLlm
from google.adk.models.base_llm_connection import BaseLlmConnection
print(inspect.signature(BaseLlm.connect))
for name in ('send_history', 'send_content', 'send_realtime', 'receive', 'close'):
    print(name, inspect.signature(getattr(BaseLlmConnection, name)))
"
```

**Line by line:**

- `uv run` executes the snippet inside this project's own environment, so what it inspects is the
  version this repository pins rather than whatever Python happens to be first on your `PATH`. On a
  question about an API's exact shape, that distinction is the whole point.
- `inspect.signature(obj)` returns the parameters and the annotated return type of a callable, read
  from the object itself. Nothing is fetched over the network and nothing is remembered — this is
  Principle 8 turned into a command instead of a claim.
- `BaseLlm` is the base class every model in ADK inherits from, and `connect` is the live half of it.
  `BaseLlmConnection` is a separate class in a separate module, which is itself informative: a
  connection is a different kind of object from a model, with its own lifetime.
- The loop over five names prints them together so you see the interface as a set rather than as five
  lookups. Reading them side by side is how the four-push/one-pull asymmetry becomes visible.
- `getattr(BaseLlmConnection, name)` fetches the unbound method from the class, which is why `self`
  appears in every signature printed. That is expected; it is the class's own view of its methods.
- No output is pasted here, deliberately. Line numbers, formatting and the exact spelling of the type
  annotations move between versions, and printing an invented transcript is precisely the failure the
  command exists to prevent.

### What it said

This is quoted reference material — what the package reported on that date — rather than code being
taught, which is why it is shown as text:

```text
BaseLlm.connect(self, llm_request: LlmRequest) -> AbstractAsyncContextManager[BaseLlmConnection]
BaseLlmConnection.send_history(self, history: list[types.Content]) -> None
BaseLlmConnection.send_content(self, content: types.Content) -> None
BaseLlmConnection.send_realtime(self, blob: types.Blob) -> None
BaseLlmConnection.receive(self) -> AsyncGenerator[LlmResponse, None]
BaseLlmConnection.close(self) -> None
```

**Line by line:**

- `connect(self, llm_request: LlmRequest)` takes the same request object the ordinary non-live path
  builds. A live session still starts from an instruction, a set of tools and a configuration; it is
  the *shape of the exchange* that differs, not the setup.
- `-> AbstractAsyncContextManager[BaseLlmConnection]` is the sentence that decides the design.
  `connect` does **not** return a connection and it does **not** return a stream of responses. It
  returns something you enter with `async with`, and entering it gives you the connection. The
  fridge list's last line is written into the return type.
- `send_history(self, history: list[types.Content]) -> None` takes a list, not a message. It is the
  conversation so far, handed over in one go when the line opens, so the model starts the call
  knowing what was said before it.
- `send_content(self, content: types.Content) -> None` takes exactly one `Content` — a whole message,
  finished, the way typed text or an already-transcribed sentence arrives.
- `send_realtime(self, blob: types.Blob) -> None` is the one for things that arrive *while* the
  caller is still speaking. Hold that declared type in your head; the second code block below is
  about the fact that it is not the whole truth.
- `receive(self) -> AsyncGenerator[LlmResponse, None]` is the odd one out and the important one. Its
  return type is a stream, so you never `await` it — you sit in an `async for` over it and take
  whatever comes, for as long as it comes.
- `close(self) -> None` is a method, which means hanging up is something a caller has to *do*. There
  is no timeout in this interface and no automatic teardown. That single observation is the seed of
  part [1.3](1.3-who-ends-the-call.md).

The same six lines as a job description:

| Method | What it is for |
| --- | --- |
| `send_history` | hand over the conversation so far, once, as the line opens |
| `send_content` | push one complete message — typed text, or a transcript the client already has |
| `send_realtime` | push what arrives mid-speech: audio, and the markers around it |
| `receive` | read the model's stream of responses for as long as the line is open |
| `close` | hang up, so that `receive` ends and the session is over |

### Opening a session, in the lab

This is `connect` from `LiveLlm` in `lab/_live.py`, the scripted model this day runs everything
against. It sits inside a class in the file, so the common leading indentation has been removed here
to put it at the left margin; nothing else is changed.

```python
@contextlib.asynccontextmanager
async def connect(self, llm_request: LlmRequest) -> AsyncIterator[ScriptedConnection]:
    """Open the session. The context manager is the API: entering opens, leaving closes."""
    conn = ScriptedConnection(interruptible=self.interruptible)
    self.connection = conn
    try:
        yield conn
    finally:
        await conn.close()
```

**Line by line:**

- `@contextlib.asynccontextmanager` is the decorator that makes the return type above true without
  writing a class. It takes an async generator that yields **exactly once** and turns it into an
  async context manager. Everything before the `yield` runs when the caller enters the `async with`
  block; everything after it runs when the caller leaves. If the function yielded twice, entering the
  block a second time would be meaningless and Python raises a `RuntimeError` — "generator didn't
  stop" — because a session that opens twice is not a session.
- `async def connect(self, llm_request: LlmRequest)` matches the base class's parameters exactly.
  This is not a coincidence to be admired; it is the requirement. An implementation that renamed the
  parameter or added one would still import cleanly and would still fail the moment the runtime
  called it the way the runtime calls it.
- `-> AsyncIterator[ScriptedConnection]` is what the *undecorated* generator returns, and the
  decorator is what converts that into the `AbstractAsyncContextManager` the base class promises. The
  annotation looks like a mismatch and is not; this is the documented way to write one of these.
- `conn = ScriptedConnection(...)` builds a **fresh** connection on every `connect`. Read that as the
  cost model, because it is: one call, one connection, one object holding state that lives as long as
  the caller stays on the line. Part [4.1](../04-what-it-costs/4.1-a-connection-per-caller.md) is
  what that does to capacity planning.
- `self.connection = conn` is a **test affordance and nothing more**. It exists so that the day's
  scripts can read the counters — `chunks_out`, `blobs_in`, `interruptions` — after the session has
  ended, since by then the connection object is otherwise unreachable. Say plainly what it would be
  in a real model: a bug. One `connection` attribute on a shared model object is wrong the instant
  two callers connect at once, because the second `connect` overwrites the first caller's connection
  and whatever reads the attribute afterwards gets an answer about the wrong call. It is left in on
  purpose because the lab has exactly one caller at a time, and it is pointed at here rather than
  quietly copied, so that nobody carries it into `sutra/voice.py`.
- `try:` / `yield conn` is the open line. That one `yield` is the entire session: the runtime is
  inside the `async with` block for as long as the call lasts, sending and receiving through the
  object that was yielded. Everything on this side of the `yield` is *before the caller says hello*;
  everything past it is *after they have hung up*.
- `finally: await conn.close()` is why a context manager is the right shape here, and it is worth
  being slow about. `finally` runs whether the block ended normally, raised an exception, or was
  cancelled — and in an async program cancellation is not exotic, it is how you stop things. So the
  connection is closed on the happy path, on the error path, and on the path where somebody hit a
  deadline and cancelled the task. Compare a plainer design that simply returned a connection: it
  would put that obligation on every single caller, forever, and the first caller to forget leaks a
  session that nothing will ever clean up. The library moved a guarantee out of everyone's `try`
  blocks and into one decorator, which is exactly what an interface is for.
- `await conn.close()` is awaited, because `close` is a coroutine. Closing is real work — flush,
  hang up, let the other end know — and pretending otherwise is how you get a session that the
  server still thinks is open.

Notice what is *not* in this function: no reconnection, no retry, no keepalive, no protocol. The
seam is small enough to reimplement in nine lines, and that smallness is the day's budget line.

### The one method that is not what it says

The second quote is `send_realtime` from `ScriptedConnection` in the same file, dedented the same
way. It is the method that receives everything arriving while the caller is mid-sentence, and the
first thing it does is work out what it just got:

```python
async def send_realtime(self, payload: object) -> None:
    """Everything that arrives while the caller is speaking.

    Three different things come through this one method, which is worth knowing before you write
    a `send_realtime` of your own: a `Blob` of audio, and the two turn markers `ActivityStart`
    and `ActivityEnd`. The runtime translates `LiveRequestQueue.send_activity_start()` into an
    `ActivityStart` and sends it here - see part 3.2.
    """
    name = type(payload).__name__
    data = getattr(payload, "data", None)
    if isinstance(data, bytes):
        self.blobs_in += 1
        self.bytes_in += len(data)
        self.realtime_log.append("blob")
        await self.inbox.put("<audio>")
        return
    self.markers_in += 1
    self.realtime_log.append(name)
```

**Line by line:**

- `payload: object` is a deliberate disagreement with the base class, which declares this parameter
  as `blob: types.Blob`. The lab widens it because **three different types actually arrive here**: a
  `Blob` carrying audio bytes, and the two turn markers `ActivityStart` and `ActivityEnd`, which the
  runtime sends down this same method when the caller's queue reports that speech started or stopped.
  The hub's §8 records where that was read in the shipped flow.
- This is worth stating as a principle rather than as a lab detail. The declared signature and the
  observed behaviour differ, and the honest thing is to say so out loud in the code that meets it,
  rather than annotate `types.Blob` and quietly do the right thing anyway. That is Principle 10 —
  fail honestly — pointed at somebody else's interface instead of at your own errors. A type
  annotation is documentation written by a person, and documentation can be narrower than reality.
- `type(payload).__name__` takes the class name as a plain string — `"Blob"`, `"ActivityStart"`,
  `"ActivityEnd"` — which is enough to log what came through without importing or matching on each
  type.
- `getattr(payload, "data", None)` asks for the attribute and accepts its absence, returning `None`
  when there is none. This is the classification test: a blob of audio has `data`, a turn marker does
  not. Asking what an object *has* rather than what it *is* keeps the check working if a future
  version adds a fourth thing with bytes in it.
- `isinstance(data, bytes)` is the second half of the same test, and it is there because `None` is
  not the only non-audio answer. It confirms that what came back is actually a payload of bytes
  before anything measures its length.
- `self.blobs_in += 1` and `self.bytes_in += len(data)` are the counters part
  [3.1](../03-what-goes-down-the-wire/3.1-audio-is-bytes.md) reads to price an open microphone. They
  are incremented at the connection, which is the only place that sees the real traffic.
- `await self.inbox.put("<audio>")` is how audio becomes something the scripted model can answer.
  Anything the caller sends — typed or spoken — lands in one inbox, and `receive` answers whatever is
  in it. The string is a stand-in for a transcription, and the file says so.
- `return` ends the method on the audio path, so the marker counters below are not touched. Without
  it, every blob would also be counted as a marker and part 3.2's numbers would be meaningless.
- `self.markers_in += 1` and `self.realtime_log.append(name)` are the marker path: count it, and keep
  its name in order. That ordered list is the measurement — `ActivityStart`, `blob`, `blob`, `blob`,
  `ActivityEnd` — and part
  [3.2](../03-what-goes-down-the-wire/3.2-who-says-the-turn-ended.md) is where it is printed and
  argued about.

Five things down one method, two of which are not sound. You would not learn that from the type
annotation, and you would not learn it from the docstring either. You learn it by writing the far end
and logging what turns up, which is a general technique and not a fact about this library.

## When it breaks

**A signature is a fact with a date on it, not a contract you inherit.** Everything quoted above was
read off `google-adk==2.7.1` on 2026-09-06. Parameter names get changed, annotations get tightened, a
sixth method gets added, a return type moves from `AbstractAsyncContextManager` to something
equivalent-but-spelled-differently. None of that is unusual and none of it will announce itself. So
the correct response to a version bump is not to re-read this page — it is to run the command in
*Check yourself* again and write down what came back, with the version and the date. That is
Principle 7 and Principle 8 in one action: never invent a version, never invent an API, and when
today's answer is all you have, record the command that produced it rather than the belief it
produced.

**Implementing four of the five and leaving `close` empty compiles, imports, runs, and hangs.** This
is the sharpest failure in the interface and it is entirely undramatic. Nothing type-checks the
*behaviour* of `close`; an implementation whose body is `pass` satisfies every signature above. The
consequence appears somewhere else entirely: the runtime calls `close`, nothing happens, `receive` is
still sitting there waiting for something to arrive, and the `async for` in your consumer never ends.
No exception, no error text, no log line — a program that simply does not finish. That is exactly the
bug part [1.3](1.3-who-ends-the-call.md) measures, with a deadline in the script to prove it, because
the only way to catch a hang is to give it a stopwatch and treat reaching the end as a failure. Look
at `ScriptedConnection.close` in `lab/_live.py`: all it does is put a sentinel in the inbox so that
`receive` can return. That is the smallest correct `close` there is, and it is one line, and skipping
it is the whole failure.

**And the same thing said from the other side: a `receive` that never ends is a session that never
ends.** The two methods are one mechanism seen from two directions. `close` is the *cause* of the
stream ending; the stream ending is what lets the consumer's `async for` fall out of its loop and the
context manager unwind. If you write a `receive` whose loop has no path to `return` — and `while
True:` with no exit is very easy to write and looks fine in review — then a perfectly correct `close`
still leaves you hanging. When you build your own connection, the check to run is not "does `close`
exist" but "is there a path from `close` being called to `receive` returning", and you demonstrate it
with a test that fails on a deadline rather than by reading the code and being satisfied.

## In production

**The seam is a supported one, which is what makes today legitimate.** There is an important
difference between replacing a model at the interface the framework publishes and reaching into a
library to patch out the network call. The first is the framework working as designed: `BaseLlm` is
an abstract base class, `connect` is the method it declares for live sessions, and any object that
implements it is a model as far as the runtime is concerned. The second breaks on the next release
and teaches you the shape of somebody's private code rather than the shape of the system. When you
need to test a live path without a provider, look for the declared interface first — and if there
isn't one, that absence is itself a fact worth reporting about the library.

**A real connection is these five methods plus everything the network makes true.** The interface is
small; a production implementation of it is not, and the gap is where the engineering lives. A real
`connect` opens a socket, which can fail, hang or be refused, and it does so under a timeout you
choose. A real `send_realtime` writes to a socket that can apply backpressure, so somewhere there is
a decision about what happens when the caller speaks faster than the wire drains — buffer it, drop
it, or slow the microphone down, and all three are wrong for somebody. A real `receive` has to
distinguish a clean hang-up from a dropped connection, because one is the end of a call and the other
is a reconnect. And a real `close` has to be **idempotent**: safe to call more than once, since the
`finally` above will call it on a connection that may already have closed itself when the far end
went away. None of that changes the five names. All of it changes what the five names cost you.

**Test doubles inherit the sharp edges of the thing they replace, and the counters are the point.**
`ScriptedConnection` is not a stub that returns a canned answer; it is a working far end that counts
what passes through it. That is what makes it useful as a measuring instrument rather than as a
placebo: `blobs_in`, `bytes_in`, `chunks_out`, `interruptions` and `realtime_log` turn every claim on
this day into a number, and the day's scripts exit non-zero when the number is wrong. A fake that
cannot fail proves nothing, and Principle 11 applies to fakes as much as to tests.

**The affordance you leave in has to be labelled.** `self.connection = conn` is fine in a lab with
one caller and a bug in anything with two. The professional move is not to be pure about it — it is
to make the compromise loud where somebody might copy it. In a real implementation the counters would
be returned to the caller, or pushed into a metrics collector at the point of measurement, or held on
an object whose lifetime is the session rather than the model. Any of those survive concurrency;
storing per-session state on a shared object does not, and it is one of the most common ways an
otherwise correct service starts reporting other people's numbers under load.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Two things on the fake. First, I want the
`connection` attribute called out in a comment as a test-only affordance, because as written it is
per-session state on a shared model object and the day we run two calls concurrently it silently
reports the wrong counters. Either return the connection from a factory or hang the counters off the
session. Second, `send_realtime` takes `object` and not `types.Blob`, and I actually agree with that
— but the reason has to be in the code, not in someone's head. Write down that markers arrive on this
method as well as blobs, and say where you read that in the flow. And the five signatures: keep the
`inspect.signature` one-liner in the repo next to the note, with the version and the date, so that
the next person who bumps the pin re-runs it instead of trusting our comment. A signature we typed
into a document is a claim about last month."*

**The interview question:** *"You have no budget and no access to a live voice API. How would you
build and test a bidirectional voice agent?"* An answer that shows the work: *"I'd start by finding
what the framework thinks a live model is, because that's the thing I have to replace. In ADK it's
tiny: `BaseLlm.connect` returns an async context manager that yields a `BaseLlmConnection`, and the
connection is five methods — `send_history`, `send_content`, `send_realtime`, `receive` and `close`.
Three coroutines to push, one async generator to pull, and a close. So I implement those five as a
scripted far end and let the real runner, the real request queue and the real events drive it. That
means the program shape, the lifetime and the event handling are all genuinely tested and the only
thing I've faked is the model, which is also the only thing I'm not paying for. The bit I'd watch is
`close`: nothing enforces that it does anything, and if it doesn't end the receive stream the
consumer's `async for` never finishes and the test hangs instead of failing. So every session script
carries a deadline and treats hitting it as a failure. And I'd say plainly what this does not test —
audio quality, real latency, echo, network drops. It tests the shape, not the sound, and I'd be clear
about that when I put it in front of anyone."*

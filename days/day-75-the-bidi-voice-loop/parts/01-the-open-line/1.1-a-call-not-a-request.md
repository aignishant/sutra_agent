# 1.1 — A call, not a request

## One-line answer

A request has one thing happening at a time in a fixed order — you speak, then it speaks — while an
open line has **two things happening at once** and either party may act at any moment, which is not a
bigger version of the first program but a different one: the ordering, the cancelling and the ending
all stop being the framework's problem and become yours.

## The story

Your first job is on the desk at a small delivery depot, and the drivers are on radios.

The radio has one button on the side. You hold it down, you say your piece, and then you say
"over" — and only when you take your thumb off can the driver say anything at all. Everybody learns
this in the first hour, because everybody gets it wrong in the first hour. You hold the button and
talk, and the driver has been trying to tell you something for the whole time, and you have heard
none of it, because while your thumb was down the channel was pointed one way.

So you learn the discipline. Short bursts. Say the important thing first. Say "over" and mean it,
because "over" is not politeness — it is the only signal in the whole system that says *the channel
is yours now*. And you learn what it costs. A driver sitting outside a locked gate cannot cut in
while you are reading out the next drop; he waits, listening to something he does not need, holding
onto the one fact that matters. By the time you release the button he has been at that gate for a
while and you have sent two more vans to the same street.

Then the depot moves you inside, to the counter, and the same driver comes in and stands in front of
you. There is no button now. He starts talking while you are still talking. You stop mid-sentence
because he said "gate's locked" and that changes everything you were about to say. He nods before
you finish. Neither of you says "over", because there is nothing to hand over: the channel was never
pointed one way in the first place.

Nothing about the second arrangement is *faster*. He does not speak more quickly at the counter than
he did on the radio. What changed is that both of you are in the channel at the same time, and the
thing you spent the whole morning working around — *only one of us may speak, and somebody has to
declare when they are done* — is simply gone.

That is the whole of today, and the rest of this page is that difference written in Python.

## The idea in plain language

The radio has a name. A channel where only one party may send at a time, and the direction has to be
handed over explicitly, is called **half duplex**. *Duplex* just means two-way; *half* means the two
ways take turns. The button and the word "over" are the hand-over made physical.

The counter has a name too. A channel where both parties may send at the same moment, with no
hand-over and no permission, is **full duplex**. Both directions are live at once. Nobody has to
finish before the other may start.

Everything you have built in this curriculum up to now has been half duplex, and it was so
comfortable that the property was invisible. You call an agent with a question. It thinks. It
answers. You call it again. Day 74 turned on streaming and the answer arrived in pieces instead of
one lump — but look at the shape and it has not moved: you still spoke once, then it spoke, and it
was never possible for your side to say anything new while its side was talking. Streaming changed
*when the reader is served*. It did not change whose turn it was. The button was still down.

A live session is the counter. And the moment both directions are live, your program acquires a
property it did not have: it must be doing two things at once. Something has to be pushing input in
while something else is pulling events out, and neither can wait for the other to finish, because if
either one waits you are back on the radio.

Two words for that, and they are not the same word.

**Concurrent** means two pieces of work are *in progress* at the same time, each able to make
progress while the other is stalled. It does not require two processors; Python's `asyncio` runs
concurrent work on one thread by letting a task hand control back whenever it is waiting. The point
is not simultaneity of execution — it is that neither task's progress is gated on the other's
completion.

An **async generator** is the object that hands you the events. A plain generator is a function you
can pull values out of one at a time, and it keeps its place between pulls. An async one is the same
idea where each pull is something you *await*, because the next value may not exist yet — it has to
arrive from a connection. `async for event in stream:` is the pull loop. The important word in that
sentence is *object*: the stream is a thing your code is holding, with a lifetime of its own, and
part [1.3](1.3-who-ends-the-call.md) is about the fact that nobody closes it for you.

Line the two arrangements up:

| Question | A request (`run_async`) | An open line (`run_live`) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who may speak, and when | you, then it — the order is fixed | either party, at any moment |
| How many flows are running | one: ask, then receive | two, concurrently: send and receive |
| What ends it | the answer ends it, by itself | somebody decides to end it — part [1.3](1.3-who-ends-the-call.md) |
| What a second message means | a new request, after this one | input arriving mid-answer — part [2.1](../02-talking-over/2.1-the-caller-interrupts.md) |

Read the bottom two rows twice. Those are not new features to learn; they are questions that used to
have automatic answers and now have none. On a request, "when does it end?" is answered by the
framework: the turn is over when the model stops. On an open line the framework has no opinion, and
whatever your code does becomes the answer. The same is true of a second message: in a request it is
simply the next call, and there is no such thing as saying something in the middle.

That is what "a different program" means. Not harder. Not larger. **Differently shaped**, with a set
of decisions that used to belong to somebody else.

## Why Sutra needs it

Day 74 part 4.1 read `Runner.run_live` off the installed package and found one argument with no
default: `live_request_queue`. That was a survey. It read signatures, opened nothing, and ended
honestly by saying that a signature tells you what a call will *accept* and never what it will *do*.

Today spends it. The desk is about to have somebody speaking to it rather than typing at it, and the
first honest thing to say is that this is not the desk with a microphone bolted on. Every design
question the desk has answered so far — what to do with the final event, what happens when the reader
leaves, how to log a turn — assumed one flow with a beginning and an end. All of them are reopened by
a channel that has two.

There is a second reason and it outlives voice. Once you have written one full-duplex loop, you can
recognise the shape anywhere: a chat socket, a collaborative document, a device streaming telemetry
while you send it commands. They are all the counter rather than the radio, and they all hand you the
same three questions about ordering, cancellation and lifetime. Learning the shape once is worth more
than learning any one API.

## The mechanism

`lab/session.py` opens a session, asks one question and hangs up. Three lines out of it carry the
whole of this part. They are quoted exactly as they sit in the file, with the four spaces of function
indentation removed so they read at the left margin:

```python
queue = LiveRequestQueue()
queue.send_content(types.Content(role="user", parts=[types.Part(text="why is my pdf blank?")]))

stream = runner.run_live(
    user_id="learner",
    session_id=session.id,
    live_request_queue=queue,
    run_config=RunConfig(streaming_mode=StreamingMode.BIDI, response_modalities=["TEXT"]),
)
```

**Line by line:**

- `queue = LiveRequestQueue()` builds the channel **before** the runner exists. A **queue** here is an
  object you push things into at one end while something else reads from the other — Day 74 part 4.1
  introduced it from the signature. Notice the order on the page: the channel is created first, and
  the runner is handed it afterwards. That is the counter, not the radio. You are not composing a
  message to send; you are opening a line and then deciding what to put down it.
- `queue.send_content(...)` puts the first message on that line — and the important word is *first*.
  The queue does not close, empty out or become spent when this returns. It is still there, still
  attached to the running session, still accepting `send_content` calls that nothing in this snippet
  makes. Compare it with `run_async`, where the question is an argument called `new_message` that you
  hand over once and never touch again. Here the message is a thing you *did* to an object that
  outlives it.
- `types.Content(role="user", parts=[types.Part(text=...)])` is the same content structure used
  everywhere else in ADK: a role saying who is speaking, and a list of parts because one message can
  carry several pieces. Text is one kind of part; on a real voice client the interesting parts are
  audio, which is section 3's subject.
- `stream = runner.run_live(` — and **there is no message argument anywhere in this call**. Not an
  empty one, not a `None` one: the parameter does not exist. Everything `run_live` will ever hear from
  you arrives through the queue. This is the single most useful thing to remember from the whole part,
  because it is the API refusing to let you write a half-duplex program by accident.
- `user_id=` and `session_id=` identify whose conversation this is, exactly as on `run_async`. They
  are the boring two.
- `live_request_queue=queue` is the argument with no default — the one thing you cannot leave out. The
  runner is being given the reading end of the line you opened two statements ago.
- `run_config=RunConfig(streaming_mode=StreamingMode.BIDI, ...)` selects the bidirectional mode.
  `BIDI` is the enum member Day 74 part 4.1 argued is not a third setting of the streaming dial but a
  different program, and this call is what that argument looks like once it is executed.
- `response_modalities=["TEXT"]` says what should come back. **This lab measures text, deliberately.**
  Every finding on this day is about ordering, interruption and lifetime, and none of those needs
  sound to be visible — while text can be counted, printed and diffed in a document. This is the field
  that would carry `"AUDIO"` in a real voice client, and swapping the string is the smallest honest
  description of the distance between this lab and a phone line.
- `stream = ...` — the assignment is the part people skim, and it is the one that matters most. What
  comes back is not a result and not a list. It is an **async generator**: an object the caller is now
  holding, which will produce events when awaited and which stays alive until somebody ends it. You
  own it. Part [1.3](1.3-who-ends-the-call.md) is entirely about what happens when nobody remembers
  that.

Run it:

```bash
cd days/day-75-the-bidi-voice-loop/lab
uv run python session.py
```

**Line by line:**

- `cd` into the lab first, because `session.py` does `import _live` as a sibling module and will not
  find it from the repository root.
- `uv run` executes inside this project's pinned environment, so the `google-adk` being exercised is
  the version in `uv.lock`.
- No flag means the default branch, which closes the queue **and** the stream when the turn completes.
  The two other branches exist and both fail on purpose; they belong to part
  [1.3](1.3-who-ends-the-call.md) and are not this part's subject.
- Nothing here contacts a provider. The model at the far end is `lab/_live.py`, a scripted connection,
  so the run costs no quota.

Measured on 2026-09-06. This is only the counting block from the middle of the output — the run also
prints which things the caller closed and what that did, and part [1.3](1.3-who-ends-the-call.md)
pastes that half:

```text
  events received            7
  turn_complete seen         yes
  the loop ended             yes

  the events, in order: ['chunk', 'chunk', 'chunk', 'chunk', 'chunk', 'chunk', 'turn_complete']
```

Two things to read out of it, and only two.

**One question produced six chunks and then a `turn_complete`.** Seven events off a single
`send_content`. The answer did not arrive as a thing; it arrived as a run of pieces with a marker at
the end saying the model had finished speaking. That marker is the model's "over" — and notice that
it is the *model* saying it, about its own sentence. Nothing in that line says the session ended, and
assuming it does is the trap part [1.3](1.3-who-ends-the-call.md) measures.

**Both directions were live for the whole run.** The events in that list were being consumed by an
`async for` loop while the queue sat open at the other end of the same session, ready to accept
another `send_content` at any point between chunk one and chunk six. Nothing had to be handed over
for that to be true. There is no moment in this run when the channel was pointed one way, which is
precisely the property the radio did not have, and it is why the program needed two flows rather than
one.

What travels down the line while it is open — how a live model is driven, and what the five methods on
the far end actually are — is part [1.2](1.2-five-methods.md). Who is allowed to end it is part
[1.3](1.3-who-ends-the-call.md). What happens when the caller uses that always-open queue *during* the
six chunks is section 2, starting at part
[2.1](../02-talking-over/2.1-the-caller-interrupts.md), and it is the reason anybody holds a line open
at all.

## When it breaks

**You write the loop as if the button still existed.** This is the most common failure and it does not
look like a failure. The code sends, then loops over events until `turn_complete`, then sends the next
thing — a half-duplex program written on a full-duplex channel. It works. Every test passes. Then a
real person talks over the answer and their input sits in the queue unread until the model has
finished a sentence they stopped listening to, because your consumer loop was not looking at anything
else. Nothing raises. Nothing logs. The system is simply the radio again, with extra machinery, and
the only symptom is that people find it irritating to talk to.

**Two flows means two places an exception can come from, and one of them has no `try` around it.** The
snippet above is three statements in a straight line, which hides the fact that the sending side and
the receiving side are now separate pieces of work with separate lifetimes. If your consumer raises
half-way through the events, what happens to the producer that is still pushing into the queue? If the
producer dies, does the consumer notice, or does it wait? Those questions have no default answers,
which is exactly what the table above meant by *decisions that used to belong to somebody else*.
`session.py` sidesteps them by having its producer be one line that runs before the loop starts. A
real client cannot.

**A program with two flows and no deadline waits for ever, silently.** Every script on this day carries
a `DEADLINE` for that reason, and `session.py` reports reaching it as a failure rather than printing a
result. A hang is the characteristic bug of a full-duplex program and it produces no error text at all
— the process simply sits there with both ends open. That is why the honest thing to check is not
"did it print an answer" but "did the loop end", which is the third line of the measured block above.

**And a warning about the numbers on this page.** Six chunks and a `turn_complete` come from
`lab/_live.py`, a scripted connection with a hand-written reply, on `google-adk==2.7.1` on 2026-09-06.
The runner, the queue, `RunConfig` and every event are the real ADK ones — only the model is replaced,
at the seam ADK provides for it. So the *shape* is real and the *content* is invented, and no sentence
on this page should be read as a measurement of any provider's live model.

## In production

**Capacity stops being a rate and becomes an occupancy.** A request-shaped service is sized in
requests per second: work arrives, is answered, and lets go, and ten thousand occasional users are
nothing like ten thousand simultaneous ones because their turns do not overlap. An open line overlaps
by definition — it is held for as long as the person is there, whether or not anybody is speaking. So
the question changes from *how many can we answer* to *how many can we hold*, and the failure changes
with it: instead of a queue growing and latency rising, you get a hard edge where the next caller
cannot be admitted at all. Part [4.1](../04-what-it-costs/4.1-a-connection-per-caller.md) is that
arithmetic in full; what belongs here is that the change comes from the *shape*, before any audio is
involved.

**Every layer between you and the user has an opinion about held-open connections.** Load balancers,
proxies and gateways were configured by somebody who was thinking about requests, and their idle
timeouts, connection limits and buffering behaviour were all chosen against that picture. A line held
open with nobody speaking looks, to a middlebox, exactly like a stalled request. The first production
outage of a live feature is very often not in your code at all.

**The ordering questions have to be decided once, in one place.** With two flows there is a real
possibility of the caller's second message and the model's answer crossing in the middle, and what
your system does about that is a design decision rather than a config value. If it lives in the client,
it will be made differently by the web client and the phone client and the test harness, and the three
will disagree in ways nobody notices until a transcript looks wrong. The hub's build brief puts a
single `call(...)` in `sutra/voice.py` for exactly this reason: one owner for the session, from hello
to hang-up.

**Observability has to change shape too.** A request emits one span with a duration and a status. A
session has no single duration worth recording — what you want is when it opened, when it closed, who
closed it, how many turns it carried, and how many of those turns were cut short. A dashboard built
for requests will show you a very healthy service while every caller is being talked over.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"The three lines are right, and I want the comment
above them to say why they are in that order, because the next person will move the `send_content`
into the `run_live` call and be confused when there is nowhere to put it. My real concern is the
consumer. Right now the producer is one statement before the loop, so this file has two flows on
paper and one in practice, and it passes for the wrong reason. Before this goes anywhere near a client
I want to see the version where something is pushing into the queue while the loop is running, and I
want to know what happens to each side when the other one throws. Second: the deadline is not a test
fixture, it is a production requirement, and it should be in the function rather than in the script.
Third, and this is the one I would block on — nothing in this file says who owns the stream object. It
is a generator we are holding. Somebody has to close it, and 'the caller' is not a name."*

**The interview question:** *"You have built request/response services. What actually changes when you
move to a bidirectional session?"* An answer that shows the work: *"The honest version is that it is a
different program, not a bigger one. In a request there is one flow and a fixed order — I send, it
answers, the answer ends the turn. A live session is full duplex: both directions are open at once and
either side can act at any moment, so my program needs two concurrent flows, one pushing input in and
one pulling events out, and neither can block on the other. You can see the API enforcing that. On the
version I used, `run_live` has no message parameter at all — the one argument with no default is the
request queue, and I create that queue before the runner and keep it after the first message, whereas
`run_async` takes a `new_message` I hand over once. What I found harder than the concurrency is that
three questions that used to have automatic answers now have none: what ends the session, what to do
with output already in flight when new input arrives, and what happens to one flow when the other
fails. I measured the first one — a single question gave me six chunks and a `turn_complete`, and
`turn_complete` is the model finishing a sentence, not the session ending, so my loop kept waiting
after it. The other thing I would say up front is the capacity shape: it is connections held open
rather than requests per second, which changes sizing and puts a hard admission edge where you used to
get gradual latency. I should be clear that I drove this against a scripted connection at the model
seam, so what I have verified is the program shape, not any provider's live behaviour."*

## Check yourself

```bash
cd days/day-75-the-bidi-voice-loop/lab
uv run python session.py; echo "exit: $?"
uv run python -c "import inspect; from google.adk.runners import Runner; print(inspect.signature(Runner.run_live))"
```

**Line by line:**

- The first command is the run this part measured. Read three lines of it: the event count, whether
  `turn_complete` was seen, and whether the loop ended. `echo "exit: $?"` prints the exit status, which
  is `0` here because the session ended — the two failing branches are part 1.3's.
- The second prints `run_live`'s parameter list on the version you have installed, with nothing else on
  screen. Look for a message argument. There is not one, and that absence is this part's entire
  argument in one line of terminal output.

Two exercises.

**One.** Take a service you have written that answers requests, and write down, in three sentences,
what it would have to decide if the caller could send a second message while the first answer was
still being produced. Do not design it — just name the three decisions. If you find fewer than three,
you have not yet found the cancellation one.

**Two.** In `session.py`, move the `queue.send_content(...)` line so it runs *after* the `run_live`
call instead of before it, and predict what happens before you run it. Then run it. Write down which
of your assumptions about ordering came from the code and which came from the request-shaped habit of
assuming the question must exist before the call.

**Out loud, without scrolling up:** *both arrangements deliver the same answer, so what exactly does
an open line give you that a request cannot — and which argument on `run_live` is the API refusing to
let you pretend otherwise?*

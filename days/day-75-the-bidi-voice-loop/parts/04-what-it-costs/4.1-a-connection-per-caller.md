# 4.1 — A connection per caller

## One-line answer

A request/response service's capacity is **requests per second** — work arriving and leaving. A
voice service's capacity is **connections held open**, which is a completely different quantity
with a completely different failure: it does not sag under load, it **runs out**. Every concurrent
caller occupies a connection for the whole call whether they are speaking or not, so the number
that matters is concurrent sessions and the lever that matters is how quickly sessions end.

**Nothing on this page has been load-tested, and nothing on this page should be read as though it
had.** The lab runs one session at a time, on one machine, against a scripted connection. What
follows is arithmetic on a byte-rate measured elsewhere in this day, plus the shape of the problem
— and every derived number is labelled as derived.

## The story

You spend a summer helping out at a small guesthouse. Six rooms.

The owner is genuinely proud of how quickly people are checked in. There is a folder open on the
desk, the keys are on hooks in order, and somebody arriving with a booking is upstairs with their
bag down in well under a couple of dozen seconds. On a busy Friday you can process a queue of
arrivals astonishingly fast, and it feels like the thing that determines how good a day the
guesthouse is having.

It isn't. It has nothing to do with it at all.

The guesthouse can hold six parties tonight. If you check people in twice as fast, it can hold six.
If you check them in half as fast, it can still hold six — you have an irritated queue in the
hallway, but the number of people who sleep there does not move. The capacity of the guesthouse is
not how many people pass the desk. It is **how many rooms are occupied at once**, and a room is
occupied for the whole night whether the guest is out at dinner, asleep, or sitting in it doing
nothing.

You find this out properly on the evening a seventh party arrives. Nothing is slow. Nobody is
struggling. The folder is open, the keys are on their hooks, and there is simply no room. The
guesthouse does not get gradually worse as it fills up; it is exactly as good at five as it is at
one, and then at seven it stops.

And there is one guest you learn about later in the summer. He came for a couple of nights, paid,
and then just... stayed. He is polite, he is no trouble, he is barely ever seen. He also has a
room, every single night, and that room is not available to anybody else. The room is not lost
because he is demanding. It is lost because he is *there*. That guest is part
[4.2](4.2-the-session-that-never-ended.md), and he is the most expensive person in the building.

## The idea in plain language

Three words first, because the rest of the page is built out of them.

**Throughput** is how much work a service gets through in a period — requests per second, orders
per day, arrivals processed. It is a *rate*: things arrive, things leave, and what you are counting
is the flow. Every service this curriculum has built before this phase was sized this way, because
every one of them was request/response: a message goes in, an answer comes out, the work is over,
and the resources it used go back in the pot for the next caller.

**Concurrency** is how many things are in progress *at the same moment*. Not a rate — a count,
taken at an instant. Six occupied rooms is a concurrency. A hundred callers currently on the line
is a concurrency. Crucially, concurrency has no time in it: "six rooms occupied" is true right now
and says nothing about how many guests the guesthouse serves in a season.

**A connection pool** is a fixed set of open connections that a program keeps and lends out — to a
database, to an HTTP host, to anything expensive to open. A pool exists precisely because opening a
connection costs more than using one, so a program opens a few and shares them. The number in the
pool is a concurrency limit that somebody chose, usually years ago, usually for a workload where
each borrower held a connection briefly and gave it back.

Now the idea. In a request/response service, a connection is borrowed and returned within one
request. Ten thousand requests per second can run over a small pool, because no single request
holds anything for long. That is why "requests per second" works as a unit of capacity: the
resource is recycled fast enough that the *flow* is what constrains you.

A voice session breaks that. The connection is not borrowed for the duration of a unit of work — it
is held for **the length of the call**, which is decided by the caller and by nothing else. Part
[1.3](../01-the-open-line/1.3-who-ends-the-call.md) measured how easily a session fails to end at
all. So the resource is not recycled, the flow stops constraining you, and the thing that
constrains you is the count.

| | request/response service | voice service |
| --- | --- | --- |
| unit of capacity | requests per second (a rate) | concurrent sessions (a count) |
| what runs out | CPU, time, worker slots — briefly | connections, held for the whole call |
| how it fails | gradually: latency climbs, queues grow, you notice | abruptly: fine at N, refused at N+1 |

The third row is the one worth remembering, and it is the guesthouse hallway. A throughput system
under strain tells you it is under strain — the response times get worse and somebody's graph bends
before anything actually breaks. An occupancy system does not bend. It is exactly as healthy one
caller below the limit as it is at half load, and then the next caller is refused.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because the day's build brief asks for `sutra/voice.py`, and a module that owns a voice session
from hello to hang-up is a module whose capacity question you have to be able to answer out loud
before anybody puts it behind a phone number. Trap 10 in [`../../LESSON.md`](../../LESSON.md) is
"sizing a voice service by requests per second", and this is the page that explains why that is not
a small mistake but the wrong unit entirely.

There is a second reason, and it is about a piece of code this curriculum has already written.
**Day 70 — the quota router** — picks a provider by what is left of its budget, and those budgets
are denominated in requests per minute and requests per day, exactly as Addendum 02 requires. That
router is correct and it is useful, and it **does not model this at all**. A concurrent-session
limit is not a request count; it is a count of things currently open, and a router that has never
seen the concept will happily hand you a provider you already have every available session open
against. Knowing what your existing tooling does *not* measure is worth as much as knowing what it
does.

And a third: **Day 74 part 4.2 — the free-quota check** — parked bidirectional voice and recorded,
as an explicit unverified result, that it could not establish whether the Live API is on a free
tier at all. That verdict stands and this page does not re-open it. It is cited here for the habit
it demonstrates rather than the answer it gave: when you cannot verify a limit, you write down that
you could not, along with the exact way to go and find out. This page owes the same treatment to a
different number.

## The mechanism

**Say the honest thing first.** This day's lab opens one session, on one machine, against a
`ScriptedConnection` that never leaves the process. It has measured lifetime, interruption, byte
counts and turn markers. It has **not** measured concurrency, because it has never had two callers.
No benchmark exists behind this page, no limit was probed, and no figure below was observed under
load. What is available is one measured rate, some arithmetic on it, and ordinary reasoning about
what a held-open connection occupies.

### The arithmetic, and where its one number comes from

Part [3.1](../03-what-goes-down-the-wire/3.1-audio-is-bytes.md) measured a held-open microphone at
**64,000 bytes for two seconds of sound**. That measurement rests on a stated assumption rather
than a discovery: `audio.py` sets `RATE = 32_000` — 16-bit samples, one channel, 16,000 samples a
second, which is 32,000 bytes for every second of sound — and the script's own docstring says in as
many words that the conversion to seconds is an assumption, because the wire format a given model
wants is a page you read on a date.

Take that rate and multiply. Everything from here is **derived**, not measured:

```text
  assumption (from part 3.1)      32,000 bytes per second of sound
  one caller, 60 seconds          32,000 x 60            =      1,920,000 bytes inbound
  100 callers, the same 60s       1,920,000 x 100        =    192,000,000 bytes inbound
  100 callers, per second         32,000 x 100           =      3,200,000 bytes per second

  ...and none of that counts the return direction, which is also audio.
```

Two things about those numbers. The first is that the exact rate does not matter to the conclusion.
If the format turns out to be half that, every figure halves and the shape is identical: the total
is **callers multiplied by duration**, and the *content* of the call — whether anybody said
anything worth hearing, whether they said anything at all — appears nowhere in the multiplication.
Part 3.1's 64,000 bytes were pure silence, and silence costs the same as speech because the
microphone was open, which is the only thing an open microphone has to be in order to cost you
something.

The second is that this is a **continuous** figure, not a total. Three million bytes a second
inbound is not a burst that arrives and clears; it is a floor that holds for as long as those
hundred people are on the line, and it does not drop while they are thinking.

Three separate things become scarce, and it is worth keeping them apart because they run out
independently and are owned by different people.

### One: connections

One per caller, held for the whole call.

Whatever sits between your process and the model is now sized by **concurrent callers** rather than
by throughput. A socket per session. A slot in a connection pool per session, held not for a query
but for a conversation. A file descriptor per socket, against a per-process limit that some
operating system default chose. An entry in whatever your web framework keeps per open client
connection, because the caller is holding one of those too — the browser tab is a session as much
as the model connection is.

Notice the shape of that list: none of those limits were set with voice in mind. They were set for
a world where a connection is borrowed for the length of one request. A pool sized generously for a
request/response service is sized *arbitrarily* for a voice service, and the number in it is now a
cap on simultaneous callers that nobody in your organisation chose on purpose.

### Two: bandwidth, continuously and in both directions

Unlike a request, audio does not arrive in a burst and stop.

A text request is a spike: some bytes go up, some bytes come down, the line goes quiet, and the
capacity is free for somebody else. Audio holds a floor. While the caller has the microphone open,
bytes are arriving at the assumed rate whether or not the caller is speaking — and while the model
is answering, bytes are going the other way at whatever rate its audio output runs at, which this
curriculum has not measured and will not guess. Both directions are busy, and for a genuinely
conversational call they overlap: part [2.1](../02-talking-over/2.1-the-caller-interrupts.md) is
the whole reason a live session exists, and barge-in is by definition both parties transmitting at
once.

The practical consequence is that a network budget for a voice service is a *sustained* figure
multiplied by concurrency, not an average of bursts. Averages are how request services are sized
and they quietly hide this: a hundred callers each averaging a small number does not describe a
floor that never drops.

### Three: the provider's own limit on concurrent sessions

This is a different quantity from requests per day, and it is the one people miss.

A provider that runs live sessions has some limit on how many you may hold open at once. It has to:
each open session occupies something on their side too, for exactly the reason it occupies
something on yours. That limit is not a request count, so it does not appear in an RPM or RPD
table, and — as above — **Day 70's quota router does not model it**. You can be comfortably inside
your daily request budget and unable to open one more session.

**This curriculum has not verified any provider's concurrent-session limit.** Not a number, not a
range, not a "typically". Day 74 part 4.2 could not even establish whether the Live API is offered
on a free tier, because the tables that would say are rendered by JavaScript and are absent from
the HTML a script receives — so a page that then produced a confident concurrency figure would be
inventing something strictly harder to find out than the thing it already admitted it could not.

### The same idea, inside this day's own lab

This part's idea has already appeared in code you have read. From `lab/_live.py`, quoted exactly as
it stands on disk — the class begins at column 0 in the file, so nothing needed dedenting, and the
four-space indentation of its body is the file's own:

```python
class LiveLlm(BaseLlm):
    """A model whose live half is scripted and whose non-live half is never used here."""

    model: str = "fake/live"
    interruptible: bool = True
    connection: ScriptedConnection | None = None
```

**Line by line:**

- `class LiveLlm(BaseLlm)` is the model object the whole day runs against. One object, constructed
  once by `desk()`, shared by every script in the lab.
- `model: str = "fake/live"` is a piece of configuration. It describes the model and is the same
  whoever is calling, so living on the shared object is exactly right.
- `interruptible: bool = True` is also configuration — part 2.2's ablation switch. Same reasoning:
  it is a property of the setup, not of a caller.
- `connection: ScriptedConnection | None = None` is **not** configuration. It is *one caller's open
  line*, stored on the object that every caller shares. It is here for a good reason — the scripts
  need to read the counters afterwards, and `connect` sets `self.connection = conn` on the way in
  so that `audio.py` and the rest can reach them.
- And that is the whole point. With one caller this is correct and convenient. With two, the second
  call's `connect` overwrites the attribute, and now `model.connection` is one arbitrary caller's
  connection while the other caller's counters are unreachable. Nothing raises. Nothing logs. The
  numbers are simply the wrong caller's.
- `| None` is the tell, if you are looking for one. A field that means "the connection, if there
  happens to be one right now" is per-session state wearing a configuration field's clothes.

This is not a bug in the lab — the lab has one caller and says so. It is worth stopping on because
it is the smallest possible version of the mistake this part is about: **something that is
per-caller, stored somewhere shared, in code that works perfectly until there are two callers.** In
a real service the shared thing is a module-level client, a cached singleton, or a connection pool,
and the symptom is not a wrong counter but two callers hearing each other.

## When it breaks

**The failure is a cliff, not a slope.** This is the part that catches people who have only ever
run throughput systems, because every instinct they have was trained on gradual degradation. A
request/response service under pressure gets *slower*: queues lengthen, latency climbs, a dashboard
bends, and somebody has a chance to act before anything is refused. An occupancy system gives you
none of that warning. At N concurrent callers it is completely healthy — the same latency, the same
audio, the same everything as at half load. At N+1 it refuses. There is no intermediate state in
which callers get a slightly worse call, because a connection is not a thing you can have a
fraction of.

**And the refusal appears at whatever layer runs out first, which may have nothing to do with
voice.** That is the genuinely unpleasant part. The message you get is written by whichever
resource hit its ceiling — a pool with no free connection, an operating system refusing another
file descriptor, a proxy with a per-worker connection cap, a container out of memory, or the
provider declining to open another session. Each of those produces a different error, in a
different component, with different words, and only one of them mentions sessions. Part 1.3 made
the same observation about a leaked session: the first symptom is a resource limit somewhere
unrelated, reported by something that has never heard of a phone call.

This is why **no error text is quoted on this page**, and the absence is deliberate rather than an
oversight. Everywhere else in this day, *When it breaks* pastes the real output of a real run,
because a run happened. Here no run has happened. Inventing a plausible traceback for a limit this
lab has never reached would be exactly the fabrication Principle 10 exists to forbid, and it would
be the convincing kind — a reader would recognise the shape and believe it.

**So be clear about the standing of everything above.** One byte-rate was measured, from a stated
assumption. The multiplication is arithmetic. The rest is ordinary field knowledge about what a
held-open connection occupies, which is worth having and is not the same thing as a measurement of
your stack. Before you size anything on it, **measure your own**: open sessions until something
refuses, in an environment you are allowed to break, and find out which layer refuses first and
what it says. That number is yours, it will not match anybody's blog post, and it is the only one
worth writing in a capacity plan.

## In production

**A professional puts their own cap on concurrent sessions, and puts it below whatever would run
out first.** This is the single most important sentence on the page. If you do not choose a number,
a number still exists — it is just chosen for you by whichever of the connection pool, the file
descriptor limit, the memory ceiling or the provider's session limit you happen to hit first, and
it will change without warning when somebody bumps an unrelated configuration value. A cap you own
is a cap you can reason about, alert on, and lower deliberately when something else in the system
gets tight. A cap you inherit is a surprise with a date on it.

The difference in behaviour is the whole argument. Past an inherited limit, a service
**collapses**: the failure lands wherever it lands, mid-call for people already talking, in a
component that reports something incomprehensible. Past a cap you own, a service **refuses
politely**, at the front door, before a session is opened, with a message you wrote — and every
call already in progress is completely unaffected, because refusing the seventh party does not
disturb the six who already have rooms.

**Ahead of the cap goes a queue, or an honest "please try again".** Both are respectable and they
suit different services. A queue holds the caller and admits them when a session ends, which is
right when calls are short and callers will wait; it needs a bound of its own, because an unbounded
queue is just the same failure moved somewhere less visible. A straight refusal is right when calls
are long enough that waiting is worse than being told no. What is not respectable is accepting the
connection and letting it fail later: you have now spent the resource *and* lost the caller.

**The second lever is per-session limits, and it is the one that changes the cap's meaning.**
Because capacity is occupancy, capacity is freed by sessions **ending** — so a maximum call
duration is not a policy detail, it is a capacity control. A hard cap sets how many rooms there
are; a maximum duration sets how often a room comes free. Raise the cap and you buy more concurrent
callers at the cost of more resource held at once. Shorten the maximum session and you buy
throughput out of the same fixed occupancy, at the cost of occasionally cutting somebody off. That
is the guest who never checked out, and it is part [4.2](4.2-the-session-that-never-ended.md)'s
entire subject: the deadline is the only thing standing between you and a room lost for ever.

**What else a real one needs is the rest of this section.** Part
[4.3](4.3-what-a-real-voice-system-adds.md) covers the distance from this lab to a system somebody
would actually phone — echo, the latency budget, transcripts — and every one of those has a
capacity cost that lands on the same count.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Two things about the sizing. First, this
document says 'requests per second' and voice services don't have one that means anything — I want
a number for concurrent sessions, and I want it enforced by us rather than discovered at whichever
pool runs dry first, so put a cap in front of the session opener and make exceeding it a 503 with a
message we wrote. Second, tell me what happens at cap plus one: a queue with a bound on it, or an
honest refusal. Either is fine, deciding it in an incident is not. Then two numbers I'd like
graphed — sessions currently open, and session duration — because the first is the capacity and the
second is the only lever on it that doesn't cost money. And go and find the provider's
concurrent-session limit and write it down with the date; it isn't in the requests-per-day table
and our router doesn't know it exists. If you genuinely can't find it published, write that down
too, and then measure where we break in staging."*

**The interview question:** *"How would you size a voice service?"* An honest answer: *"By
concurrent sessions, not by requests per second — and I'd say why, because the unit is where people
go wrong. Requests per second is a rate: work arrives, work leaves, the resource comes back. A
voice session holds a connection for the whole call whether anybody's speaking or not, so the
resource never comes back until the call ends, and capacity becomes a count of what's open rather
than a flow. The failure mode is different too. Throughput systems degrade — latency climbs and you
get a warning. An occupancy system is perfectly healthy at N and refuses at N+1, and the refusal
surfaces at whichever layer runs out first, which might be a connection pool or a file descriptor
limit and might say nothing about voice at all. So the design is: pick a cap you own, put it in
front of the session opener so the refusal is polite and ours, put a bounded queue or a clear 'try
again' ahead of it, and treat a maximum session duration as the second lever, because capacity is
only freed by sessions ending. As for the numbers, I'd be upfront that I'd have to go and measure
most of them. I'd know my per-session bandwidth from the audio format — I've done that arithmetic,
it's the sample rate times the duration times the number of callers, and the content of the call
doesn't enter into it. What I would not know without measuring is which layer in my own stack runs
out first and at what count, and what my provider's limit on concurrent sessions is, which is a
different limit from requests per day and usually isn't in the same table. I'd find both before I
published a capacity number, and I'd date them."*

## Check yourself

```bash
cd days/day-75-the-bidi-voice-loop/lab
uv run python audio.py | grep "bytes of audio sent"
uv run python audio.py --text | grep "the question, as text"
```

**Line by line:**

- `cd` into the lab first and run through `uv` so the pinned environment is the one being
  exercised.
- The first run is one caller with the microphone held open; `grep` pulls out the single line
  carrying the inbound byte count that all of this page's arithmetic is built on.
- `--text` is the ablation — the same question typed — and its line is what the same question costs
  when it is not audio. Read the two numbers as a pair, then multiply the first one by the number
  of callers you would like to support and by the length of a call you would like to allow. That
  product is your inbound floor, in one direction, and you now have it without any load testing at
  all.

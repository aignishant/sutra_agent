# 3.2 — The newer fact wins

## One-line answer

A fact can be perfectly fresh and still wrong, because something newer about the same subject
replaced it — and unlike expiry, nothing about the memo itself will ever tell you.

## The story

You update a phone number in your contacts.

You do not keep the old one. You do not add a second entry called *"same person, older number"*. You
type over the number that is there and the previous one is gone, and you do this without thinking
about it, because a contact is not a list of every number somebody has ever had. It is the number to
ring **now**.

Now imagine a phone that kept them all. You open the entry and there are four numbers, none marked,
all valid-looking, in no useful order. You ring one. It has been reassigned to a stranger. Nothing
was corrupted — every one of those numbers was correct on the day you wrote it down.

The phone did not lose information. It lost the *ordering*, and the ordering was the only part that
made the information usable.

## The idea in plain language

Two things stop a memo being the right answer, and they are completely independent.

**Expiry** is about time. A memo carries a date and the date passes. It is a property of the memo
alone, and you can check it with nothing but a calendar
([3.1](3.1-true-on-the-day-it-was-said.md)).

**Supersession** is about competition. A memo is fine — recent, unexpired, accurate when written —
and something *else* in the store now speaks about the same thing more recently. You cannot detect
this by looking at the memo. You can only detect it by looking at its neighbours.

That asymmetry is the reason supersession gets forgotten. Expiry is a field. Supersession is a
relationship, and relationships have to be computed.

Three ideas make it work:

**What "the same thing" means.** Two memos compete when they share a `holder` and a `subject`. Not a
kind — that is the mistake [1.4](../01-what-a-conversation-leaves/1.4-a-correction-outranks-it.md)
exists to catch, since the whole point of a correction is to beat something of a different kind.

**What "more recently" means.** Date first. Then, for two memos from the same day, the `authority`
column. Then, for two memos from the same day with the same authority, arrival order. Three levels,
in that order, and the order is not arbitrary.

**Which kinds compete at all.** Some do not. Two decisions about one ticket are two decisions, not a
decision and a stale draft — so `decision` sets `supersede=False` and is never superseded by
anything. That is a row in the table, so it is a policy choice rather than a property of the code.

The verdict is called `superseded` and not `deleted`, deliberately. The memo is still there. It is
still true about the past. It is simply no longer the answer, and section 3.3 is about the gap
between those two states.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because Sutra's customers change, and the desk sees them repeatedly over years. That is the reason it
has a memory at all, and it is the reason the memory is dangerous.

A customer on the Starter plan in 2024 moves to Team in 2025 and to yearly billing in 2026. Three
memos, all `customer_fact`, all about `plan`, all accurate on the day they were written, none of them
expired, because `customer_fact` has no expiry date — a plan is true until it changes, which is
exactly right and exactly the reason expiry cannot help here.

Without supersession, the desk holds three answers to *"what plan are they on?"* and hands all three
to a model. Two of them are wrong. Nothing in the store says which.

This is also what makes [4.3](../04-privacy-and-erasure/4.3-erasure-is-an-obligation.md) tractable:
once the store has a notion of which memo is current for a subject, an erasure request has something
precise to act on rather than a pile of undifferentiated text.

## The mechanism

The contest is computed in one pass over the store, then applied in a second.

```python
# days/day-48-memory-design/lab/policy.py
def _standing(memo: Memo, position: int) -> tuple[date, int, int]:
    """How strongly this memo speaks about its subject: recency, then authority, then arrival."""
    return (memo.recorded_on, RULES[memo.kind].authority, position)
```

**Line by line:**

- The return is a **tuple**, and Python compares tuples element by element, left to right, stopping at
  the first difference. That single fact is what encodes "date first, then authority, then arrival"
  without a single `if`.
- `memo.recorded_on` is first, so a genuine change made this year beats a correction made last year.
  Putting `authority` first would pin the store to old corrections forever, which is the subtle bug
  [1.4](../01-what-a-conversation-leaves/1.4-a-correction-outranks-it.md) warns about.
- `RULES[memo.kind].authority` is read from the table rather than written as a comparison here, so a
  reviewer changes the ranking by editing a number in a row.
- `position` is the memo's index in the store, which is the order it was recorded. It exists for the
  case that actually happens: a fact and its correction, same conversation, same day, same subject.
  Without it those two tie, and a tie means both survive.
- The function is a plain function returning a tuple rather than a method or a sort key class,
  because it is called from two places — building the winners and testing each memo — and both need
  exactly the same answer.

```python
best: dict[tuple[str, str], tuple[date, int, int]] = {}
for position, memo in enumerate(memos):
    if RULES[memo.kind].supersede:
        key = (memo.holder, memo.subject)
        standing = _standing(memo, position)
        if standing > best.get(key, (date.min, -1, -1)):
            best[key] = standing
```

**Line by line:**

- `enumerate(memos)` gives the position, and the store is held in recording order, which is what makes
  position meaningful. If the store were ever re-sorted before this ran, the tiebreak would change
  answer — worth an assertion in production.
- `if RULES[memo.kind].supersede` means a `decision` never enters the contest and therefore never
  appears in `best`. That has a consequence two lines later.
- `key = (memo.holder, memo.subject)` — two fields, no kind. This is the fix from 1.4.
- `best.get(key, (date.min, -1, -1))` gives a floor that every real memo beats: the earliest date
  Python has, and authorities below zero. Using `None` would need a second branch.
- `>` and not `>=`, so the first memo to reach a given standing keeps it. With `position` in the
  tuple, two memos can never have equal standing anyway, which means the winner is always unique —
  a property worth knowing, because it is what stops two memos both being "current".

And the test each memo faces:

```python
elif RULES[memo.kind].supersede and _standing(memo, position) < best[key]:
    verdicts.append(Verdict(memo, "superseded", f"outranked on subject {memo.subject!r}"))
```

**Line by line:**

- `RULES[memo.kind].supersede` is checked **before** `best[key]` is read, and that ordering is load
  bearing rather than stylistic. A `decision` was never added to `best`, so `best[key]` would raise
  `KeyError` for it. Python's `and` short-circuits, so the lookup never happens.
- `_standing(memo, position) < best[key]` — strictly less than the winner. The winner compares equal
  to itself, so exactly one memo per key survives.
- The reason string names the subject rather than the winning memo, because at this point in the loop
  the winning memo is not in hand — only its standing is. Production wants the winner's identity here,
  which is the `superseded_by` pointer discussed below.
- This branch sits after erasure and after expiry, so a memo that is both expired and superseded
  reports as expired. One verdict per memo, and the first applicable reason wins.

**Line by line:**

- The store is three `customer_fact` memos about one subject, recorded in 2024, 2025 and 2026. None

- `--no-supersede` rewrites every `superseded` verdict back to `keep`. It changes nothing about the

Measured on 2026-09-05:

```text
supersession: on

  superseded 2025-06-18  Plan is Team, billed monthly.
  keep       2026-09-05  Plan is Team, billed yearly.

  Plan is Team, billed yearly.  (from ticket 4521)
```

```text
supersession: off
  keep       2024-04-02  Plan is Starter, billed monthly.
  keep       2025-06-18  Plan is Team, billed monthly.
  keep       2026-09-05  Plan is Team, billed yearly.

live answers to "what plan are they on?": 3
  Plan is Starter, billed monthly.  (from ticket 3110)
  Plan is Team, billed monthly.  (from ticket 3998)
  Plan is Team, billed yearly.  (from ticket 4521)

All three are unexpired. Two of them are wrong. Nothing in the store says which.
```

Read the last line of the second block, and then notice what expiry could have done about this:
nothing. Every one of those three memos is inside its retention period, because its retention period
is forever. A `customer_fact` that expired after two years would have removed the 2024 row and left
two contradictory answers instead of three, which is not a fix.

## When it breaks

The first break is subject-name drift, and it is the one that actually happens.

Supersession only works when two memos about one thing agree on what to call it. The moment a second
extractor calls it `billing_address` while the first called it `billing_email`, the two memos land in
different buckets and both go live. From the outside this looks exactly like supersession not
working, and from the inside every line of the code is correct.

```text
live answers to "what is the billing address?": 2
  customer_fact  billing_email    Billing goes to the accounts address.
  customer_fact  billing_address  Billing goes to the old personal address.
```

There is no error and no warning. The defence is that `subject`, like `kind`, comes from a closed list
checked at the boundary — not from whatever string an extractor produced.

The second break is a `KeyError`, and it is the one you will hit if you rearrange the conditions:

```traceback
Traceback (most recent call last):
  File "policy.py", line 219, in what_to_forget
    elif _standing(memo, position) < best[key] and RULES[memo.kind].supersede:
                                      ~~~~^^^^^
KeyError: ('acct-88', 'ticket-4521')
```

That is a `decision` memo. It was never added to `best` because its row says `supersede=False`, so
looking up its key raises. Swapping the two conditions back so the `supersede` check comes first fixes
it, and the reason is short-circuiting rather than anything about memory.

The third break is philosophical and it is worth being honest about: **supersession is a guess about
intent.** A customer saying "Plan is Team, billed yearly" might be correcting the record or might be
describing a different account they also own. The store cannot tell. It applies the rule and produces
one answer where there were two, and it is right most of the time and confidently wrong occasionally.
The mitigation is not cleverness; it is that the superseded memo is kept and marked rather than
destroyed, so the wrong call is recoverable.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead:** the same contest, materialised. Computing supersession over
the whole store on every query is fine for a lab and not fine for a store of millions, so the winner
per `(holder, subject)` is written down — a `superseded_by` column on the loser pointing at the
memo that beat it, updated when a memo is written rather than when one is read.

That pointer buys two things beyond speed. It makes the chain visible, so *"why did you send it
there?"* can be answered with the sequence rather than a shrug. And it makes the wrong guess above
recoverable, because nothing was destroyed.

**What degrades at scale:** the write path, not the read path, and this is the trade being made.
Materialising the winner turns each write into a read-modify-write over every memo sharing that key.
Under concurrency two sessions closing at the same moment for one customer race, and the loser of
the race can end up marked current — which is why the ordering key should come from the event's
timestamp in the session rather than from arrival at the store. The memo already knows which turn it
came from; use it.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Marking the loser `superseded` is right, deleting
it is not. Add a `superseded_by` pointer so the chain is walkable — the first time a customer asks why
we used an old address, 'we superseded it' is not an answer, and 'here is the sequence and the ticket
each one came from' is. Also: this recomputes the whole contest on read. That is fine now and it is
not fine at a million memos, so write the winner down."*

**The interview question:** *"Your store has three facts about a customer's plan, all accurate when
written. What do you return?"* The honest spoken answer: *"One, and the interesting part is that
expiry cannot help you. All three are inside their retention period, because a plan is true until it
changes — putting an expiry on it would be wrong. So you need a second, independent mechanism:
supersession, keyed on who the memo is about and what it is about, ignoring the kind. The ordering is
date first, then an authority number from the policy table, then arrival order, and the ordering
matters — if you compare authority before date, a correction from last year beats a genuine change
made this morning. I measured it on our desk: three plan facts over two years, supersession off gives
you three live answers with two of them wrong and nothing marking which. What I would change for
production is materialising the winner and keeping a pointer from the loser, because supersession is a
guess about intent and you want the wrong guesses to be recoverable."*

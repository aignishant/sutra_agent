# 3.1 — True on the day it was said

## One-line answer

Some memories go off, and the only way a store can know is if the date they stop being trustworthy
was written on them when they went in.

## The story

A carton of milk in the fridge has a date printed on the side.

Nobody checks the milk by tasting it. You pick it up, you turn it round, you read the date, and you
decide in under a second — and the decision is right almost every time even though you have learned
nothing about the milk itself.

What makes that work is not the fridge and not the milk. It is that somebody printed a date on it at
the moment it was filled, when they knew how long it had. By the time the carton is in your hand,
that knowledge is gone. You cannot reconstruct it. You can only read it.

Take the date off and the system collapses immediately. Now every carton needs a person to smell it,
every person smells differently, and someone eventually pours a glass for a guest because it seemed
fine to them.

The date is not information about milk. It is a decision, made once, by someone who had the context,
recorded where the person without the context will be standing.

## The idea in plain language

Some things a conversation produces are true permanently. Some are true *on a date*.

A customer's plan is true until they change it. The reason a decision was taken does not stop being
the reason. But a price, a delivery estimate, a queue length, a "the vendor is investigating" — every
one of those had a shelf life measured in days or weeks, and none of them says so.

Here is what makes stale facts more dangerous than missing ones. A missing fact produces a visible
gap: the agent says it does not know, somebody looks it up, life goes on. A stale fact produces a
**confident, specific, wrong answer**, and specificity is exactly what makes people believe it. "The
Business plan is 4800 a year" sounds far more authoritative than "I would need to check the current
price", and it is the sentence that gets repeated to a customer.

**Expiry** is the rule that says a memo stops being returned after a date. Three things about it are
worth being precise on.

**The clock starts when the memo is written**, not when it is read. The rule says thirty days; the
memo says the fifth of October. This matters: if you store the *rule* rather than the *date*, then
changing the rule next year silently re-dates every memo already written, which is a rewrite of
history disguised as a config change.

**Expiry is not deletion.** An expired memo is not returned by a retrieval. Whether the bytes are
still on disk is a completely separate question, and it is the subject of
[3.3](3.3-a-verdict-is-not-an-erasure.md).

**No expiry is a choice too.** `keep_for_days=None` is not the absence of a decision — it is the
decision that this kind is true until something replaces it. Two of the six kinds have it, and
section 6 measures what it costs.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because Sutra's support desk quotes numbers, and the first ticket it ever handles contains one:
*"Business is 4800 a year at the moment."*

Read the last three words of that sentence. The agent said them. The agent knew the number had a
shelf life, and said so out loud. Then it went into a memo as `Business plan is 4800 a year` — because
the hedge is not a fact and there is nowhere to put it — and the shelf life was lost at exactly the
moment somebody knew it.

That is the whole argument for a `keep_for_days` column: the knowledge about how long something lasts
exists **once**, when the memo is written, and if it is not recorded then it is gone.

You meet this again in [5.2](../05-failure-lab/5.2-the-price-that-changed-last-year.md), where that
exact memo is quoted back to a customer 380 days later, and again on Day 49, where an index that
scores by relevance will happily rank a stale memo first because it matches the query beautifully.

## The mechanism

The rule is one column, and it is resolved to a date the moment the memo is built.

```python
# days/day-48-memory-design/lab/policy.py, inside what_to_keep
def what_to_keep(candidates: list[Candidate], *, today: date) -> tuple[list[Memo], list[Refusal]]:
    for candidate in candidates:
        ...
        expires_on = (
            None if rule.keep_for_days is None else today + timedelta(days=rule.keep_for_days)
        )
```

**Line by line:**

- `rule.keep_for_days is None` and not a falsy test, because `0` is also falsy and `0` means the
  opposite: never store this at all. That candidate was already refused a few lines earlier
  ([2.2](../02-policy-as-data/2.2-every-verdict-names-its-row.md)), so by the time this line runs
  `0` cannot appear — but writing `if not rule.keep_for_days` here would still be wrong the day
  somebody reorders the function.
- `today + timedelta(days=...)` resolves the expiry **now**, at write time. The alternative — storing
  the rule name and evaluating it at read time — means the answer to "is this expired?" changes when
  the policy changes, retroactively, for memos written years ago.
- `today` is the parameter passed in, never `date.today()`. That is what makes the next four lines of
  output possible: the whole store can be asked what it thinks on any date, without a clock and
  without waiting.
- `None` is stored as an actual `None`, not as a far-future date like the year 9999. A sentinel date
  is a real date to every query that touches it, and eventually somebody writes `WHERE expires_on <
  ?` and gets a different answer than they expected.

And the verdict, which reads it back:

```python
elif memo.expires_on is not None and memo.expires_on < today:
    verdicts.append(Verdict(memo, "expired", f"expired on {memo.expires_on.isoformat()}"))
```

**Line by line:**

- `memo.expires_on is not None` first, so a permanent memo never reaches the comparison. Comparing
  `None < date` raises `TypeError: '<' not supported between instances of 'NoneType' and
  'datetime.date'`, and it would raise inside a loop over a store, taking every other verdict with it.
- `<` and not `<=`, so a memo expires at the *end* of its last day. A thirty-day quote recorded on the
  fifth of September is still live on the fifth of October and gone on the sixth. Either choice is
  defensible; what is not defensible is nobody knowing which one was made, which is why the reason
  string prints the actual date.
- The verdict carries `expired on <date>`, not just `expired`. When somebody asks why the desk stopped
  quoting a price, the answer is a date they can check against the day the policy was written.
- This is an `elif`, following the erasure check. The ordering is deliberate and
  [4.3](../04-privacy-and-erasure/4.3-erasure-is-an-obligation.md) explains why erasure has to come
  first.

Ask the same store the same question on four different dates:

```bash
cd days/day-48-memory-design/lab
uv run python expiry.py
cd -
```

**Line by line:**

- The script writes the memos **once**, on the fifth of September, and then calls `what_to_forget`
  four times with four different `today` values. Nothing is re-written between the rows.
- There is no ablation flag, because the four rows are the experiment: the only variable is the date.

Measured on 2026-09-05:

```text
recorded on 2026-09-05; 5 memos written.

asked on        live  expired  what expired
2026-09-05         4        0  -
2026-10-04         4        0  -
2026-10-06         3        1  quote/business_plan_price
2027-11-02         3        1  quote/business_plan_price

Nothing was written or deleted between these rows. Only the date moved.
A memo with no expiry date is a memo that will still be there in 2036.
```

Two things to read carefully.

**The first column starts at four, not five.** Five memos were written; four are live on day one. The
fifth was superseded the instant it was stored, because the conversation contained a correction —
which is [3.2](3.2-the-newer-fact-wins.md), and it is worth seeing here that expiry and supersession
are two independent reasons a memo stops being returned.

**The last two rows are identical.** Thirteen months apart, same answer. Once the quote is gone,
nothing else ever goes. The three survivors have no expiry date at all, so 2027 and 2037 look exactly
the same to this store — which is the second sentence of the summary line, and it is not a joke.

## When it breaks

The break is the row you did not give an expiry to, and it produces no output at all, which is why
the last line of that run says what it says.

Here is the version with an error message, because there is one, and it is the most common way this
goes wrong in code. A memo written before the `expires_on` field existed, loaded from an older store,
comes back with `None` where a date is expected — and if the `is not None` guard is missing:

```traceback
Traceback (most recent call last):
  File "policy.py", line 214, in what_to_forget
    elif memo.expires_on < today:
         ^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^
TypeError: '<' not supported between instances of 'NoneType' and 'datetime.date'
```

That one is easy. It is loud, it is a one-line fix, and it happens on the first permanent memo.

The dangerous break is the choice of number. Thirty days for a quote is not a fact — it is a
judgement about how often prices change, made by somebody, on a day, and it can be wrong in both
directions.

Too long, and you get [5.2](../05-failure-lab/5.2-the-price-that-changed-last-year.md): a confident
price quoted more than a year after it was true.

Too short, and you get [5.3](../05-failure-lab/5.3-the-rule-that-deleted-the-evidence.md): the record
of what was decided is gone when somebody disputes it. That one is worse, and it is the reason
`decision` is the only kind in the table with a long fixed number rather than `None` or a small one.

The break with no error text at all is the one where the number was chosen because it sounded
reasonable. `30` is a suspiciously round number. So is `90`. If the `why` column does not say what
the number is sized against — *how often does this actually change?* — then nobody can tell a
considered rule from a guess, and the two look identical in the table.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead:** the same column, with the expiry sized against something
measurable rather than something round. For a price, the right number is derived from how often
prices actually change, which is a question the billing system can answer. Writing `keep_for_days=30`
with a `why` that says *"prices are reviewed quarterly; a quote older than a month has usually been
superseded"* is a different artifact from writing `30` alone, even though the behaviour is identical.

The second production addition is that a live quote is returned **with its date attached**, not as a
bare number. `"Business plan is 4800 a year (quoted 5 September 2026)"` lets the model, and the human
reading the reply, do something sensible near the boundary. Expiry is a cliff; a visible date is a
slope.

**What degrades at scale:** the sweep, not the check. Deciding whether one memo is expired is a
comparison. Deciding it for a store of millions on every query means either an index on `expires_on`
or a scheduled job that removes them — and the moment there is a scheduled job, the question of what
"expired" means to the bytes becomes real, which is [3.3](3.3-a-verdict-is-not-an-erasure.md). Under
concurrency there is a second issue: a memo can expire *between* the retrieval and the reply, so the
check happens once, at retrieval, and the reply is built from that snapshot rather than from a store
that keeps moving.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Two of the six kinds have no expiry, which means
they are permanent, which means we are agreeing to hold them forever right now. I am fine with that
for `customer_fact` but I want the `why` to say it explicitly rather than leaving `None` to speak for
itself. And the thirty on `quote` — what is that sized against? If the answer is 'it felt about
right', say so in the column, so the next person knows they are allowed to change it."*

**The interview question:** *"How do you stop an agent giving stale information from memory?"* The
honest spoken answer: *"By writing the expiry date on the memo when it is stored, because that is the
only moment anybody knows what it should be. On our desk the agent literally said 'Business is 4800 a
year at the moment' — the hedge was in the sentence — and then the memo said 'Business plan is 4800 a
year', because a hedge is not a fact and there was nowhere to put it. So the shelf life has to become
a column. Two details I would defend: store the resolved date rather than the rule, because otherwise
changing the policy retroactively re-dates memos written years ago; and store `None` rather than a
sentinel date for things that never expire, because a year-9999 sentinel is a real date to every query
that touches it. The part that is genuinely hard is not the mechanism, it is the number. Thirty days
for a price is a guess unless the `why` column says what it is sized against."*

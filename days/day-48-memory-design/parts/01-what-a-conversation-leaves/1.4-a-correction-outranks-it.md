# 1.4 — A correction outranks what it corrects

## One-line answer

When a customer says "actually, no", they have not added a second fact — they have replaced the
first one, and a store that treats both as equally live will sometimes answer with the version the
customer already told you was wrong.

## The story

You get into a taxi and say the name of a road.

Two streets later you realise you have given the wrong one — it is the road with the same name on
the other side of town, the one you used to live on. So you lean forward and say: no, sorry, not
that one, the other one, near the hospital.

There are now two instructions in the car. Both were said by you. Both were said clearly. The second
one was said later and it exists *because* of the first one.

If the driver holds both and picks whichever comes to mind at the junction, you will sometimes
arrive at the wrong road, and you will have no way to argue about it — you did say that. What you
need is not for the driver to remember more carefully. It is for the second instruction to
**cancel** the first one, automatically, because that is what a correction is for.

The version of this that actually goes wrong is quieter. The driver says nothing, drives to the
right place, and you never learn whether the correction landed or whether you simply got lucky
because the second road was closer.

## The idea in plain language

A **correction** is a statement whose entire purpose is to replace an earlier statement about the
same thing.

That is a different act from adding information. If a customer tells you their plan on Monday and
their billing address on Tuesday, you have two facts about two subjects and both are true. If they
tell you their billing address on Monday and correct it four minutes later, you have **one** fact
and one obsolete draft of it.

So a correction carries something a plain fact does not: an implicit *"and forget the other one"*.
The question this part answers is how a store hears that clause, given that the customer did not say
it out loud and never will.

The naive answer is "keep the newer one". It is nearly right, and it fails in the exact case that
matters most. Both statements were made in the same conversation, on the same day, four minutes
apart. If the store's only ordering is a date, the two are tied — and a tie means both survive.

So the store needs one more thing: some kinds of statement **outrank** others about the same
subject. A correction outranks a customer fact. That ranking is not a heuristic buried in a
function; it is a number in the policy table, and section 2 is about why that matters.

Two definitions carried forward:

- Two memos are **about the same thing** when they share a `holder` and a `subject`. Not a kind —
  that is the mistake this part exists to catch.
- **Supersession** is the verdict that says: this memo is still stored, still unexpired, and no
  longer the answer, because something outranks it.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because the support desk's very first ticket contains one. Turn 0 gives a billing address; turn 2
corrects it. That is not a contrived example — it is the single most common shape in support, since
people volunteer information before they have thought about it.

And because Sutra's memory is read by a model, not by a person. A person handed two contradictory
notes asks which is current. A model handed two contradictory memos writes a fluent sentence using
one of them. Which one depends on the order they happened to arrive in the prompt, which
[Day 19](../../../day-19-context-engineering-selection/parts/03-selection/3.3-position-is-not-presence.md)
showed is not a thing you can rely on.

Section 3 builds the general machinery for this. This part exists first because the *decision* — that
corrections are their own kind and that they win — belongs with the taxonomy, and because getting it
wrong is invisible.

## The mechanism

Here is the keying almost everybody writes first. Read it before the correct one, because it looks
right.

```python
# days/day-48-memory-design/lab/correction.py
def forget_by_kind(memos: list[Memo], *, today: date) -> list[Verdict]:
    """The keying almost everyone writes first: a fact supersedes a fact, of the same kind."""
    newest: dict[tuple[str, str, str], date] = {}
    for memo in memos:
        if RULES[memo.kind].supersede:
            key = (memo.holder, memo.kind, memo.subject)
            newest[key] = max(newest.get(key, memo.recorded_on), memo.recorded_on)
    verdicts: list[Verdict] = []
    for memo in memos:
        key = (memo.holder, memo.kind, memo.subject)
        if RULES[memo.kind].supersede and memo.recorded_on < newest[key]:
            verdicts.append(Verdict(memo, "superseded", "an older memo of the same kind"))
        else:
            verdicts.append(Verdict(memo, "keep", "nothing of its own kind outranks it"))
    return verdicts
```

**Line by line:**

- `key = (memo.holder, memo.kind, memo.subject)` is the bug, and it is the whole part. Including
  `kind` in the key means a `correction` and a `customer_fact` land in **different buckets** and can
  never supersede each other. The code is correct about what it does; it is wrong about what a
  correction is.
- `newest[key] = max(...)` finds the latest date per bucket, which is the right idea applied to the
  wrong grouping.
- `memo.recorded_on < newest[key]` is a strict comparison, so two memos recorded on the *same day*
  are never ordered. Both are `keep`. This is the second half of the failure and it does not depend
  on the first: even with the right key, a same-day correction ties with the fact it corrects.
- The verdict strings say *"of the same kind"* deliberately. When this runs, the output explains its
  own bug to anyone who reads it carefully — which nobody does, because the run does not fail.

Now the version in `policy.py`:

```python
# days/day-48-memory-design/lab/policy.py
def _standing(memo: Memo, position: int) -> tuple[date, int, int]:
    """How strongly this memo speaks about its subject: recency, then authority, then arrival."""
    return (memo.recorded_on, RULES[memo.kind].authority, position)


best: dict[tuple[str, str], tuple[date, int, int]] = {}
for position, memo in enumerate(memos):
    if RULES[memo.kind].supersede:
        key = (memo.holder, memo.subject)
        standing = _standing(memo, position)
        if standing > best.get(key, (date.min, -1, -1)):
            best[key] = standing
```

**Line by line:**

- `key = (memo.holder, memo.subject)` — `kind` is gone. Everything that speaks about one subject for
  one holder now competes in one bucket, which is the only arrangement in which a correction can beat
  a fact.
- `_standing` returns a **tuple**, and Python compares tuples left to right. So date decides first,
  and only when dates are equal does authority get a say, and only when both are equal does arrival
  order break the tie. That ordering is deliberate: a fact recorded last year must not beat a
  correction from this morning just because corrections outrank facts.
- `RULES[memo.kind].authority` is read from the policy table rather than written here as an
  `if kind == "correction"`. That is the section 2 rule applied early: a reviewer changes the ranking
  by editing a number in a row, not by editing a function.
- `position` comes from `enumerate` over the store, which is stored in the order memos were recorded.
  It is the tiebreaker of last resort — two memos, same day, same authority — and it is the reason
  the same-day case in the naive version disappears.
- `best.get(key, (date.min, -1, -1))` uses `date.min` and `-1` as a floor that every real memo beats.
  Using `None` would work too and would need a second branch; the floor keeps the comparison to one
  line.
- The guard `if RULES[memo.kind].supersede` means a `decision` never enters this contest at all. Two
  decisions about one ticket are two decisions, not a decision and a stale draft — which is why that
  row's `supersede` is `False`.

keyed on: (holder, kind, subject)
  keep        customer_fact  Billing email is the one on the account.
  keep        correction     Billing goes to the accounts address; the other one is personal.

memos a retrieval about billing_email returns: 2
Two live answers to one question. The model picks whichever it reads first.
```

keyed on: (holder, subject) + authority
  superseded  customer_fact  Billing email is the one on the account.
  keep        correction     Billing goes to the accounts address; the other one is personal.

memos a retrieval about billing_email returns: 1
One live answer, and it is the correction.
```

Two against one, on a one-word change to a dictionary key.

## When it breaks

The first arm is the break, and its most important property is that **it does not look like one**.
Nothing raised. Both memos are stored correctly. Both are unexpired. Both are honestly labelled.
Every unit test you would naturally write — *the correction was stored*, *the fact was stored*, *both
are retrievable* — passes.

The failure surfaces later, in a sentence like this one, produced by a model handed both memos:

```text
Your invoices go to the address on your account. I can see three of them
bounced last month.
```

That is fluent, confident, cites nothing, and is exactly the thing the customer corrected in turn 2.
There is no traceback to search for, because nothing went wrong in any component.

The second break is subtler and it is why `authority` is a number rather than a boolean. Suppose a
correction is recorded, and then eight months later the customer changes their billing address for
ordinary reasons — a plain `customer_fact`, newer. Which wins?

The newer fact, because date is compared first. That is correct, and it is worth testing on purpose,
because a naive reading of "corrections always win" would give you a store that pins itself to a
correction from last year forever. **A correction outranks the thing it corrected. It does not
outrank the future.**

The third break is the one to be honest about: nothing in this machinery *detects* a correction. A
human or an extractor labelled that memo `correction`. If the label is wrong, the ranking is applied
to the wrong sentence with complete confidence, and the store is now wrong in a way that is harder to
find than if it had never ranked anything at all.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead:** the same comparison, with the superseded memo kept and
marked rather than deleted, plus a `superseded_by` pointer to the memo that beat it. That pointer is
what makes the store answerable when a customer asks *"why did you send it there?"* — you can show
the chain. Deleting the loser makes the store smaller and makes that question unanswerable.

**What degrades at scale:** the `subject` vocabulary. Supersession only works when two memos about
one thing agree on what to call it. `billing_email` and `billing_address` are the same subject with
two names, and the moment both exist, the two memos stop competing and both go live. That failure
scales with the number of people writing extractors. The defence is that `subject` comes from a
closed list, checked at write time, exactly as `kind` does.

Under concurrency there is a real ordering problem: two sessions for one customer closing at the
same moment produce memos whose `position` in the store depends on which transaction committed
first, and the same-day tiebreak is then a coin toss. The fix is to order by the event's timestamp
from the session rather than by arrival at the store — the memo already knows which turn it came
from.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Your supersession key includes the kind, so a
correction can never supersede a fact — which is the only case the feature exists for. Take `kind`
out of the key and put a rank on the row instead. And write the test with both memos recorded on the
same day, because that is the real case: people correct themselves in the same conversation, not next
Tuesday."*

**The interview question:** *"A user tells your agent something and then corrects it. What happens?"*
The honest spoken answer: *"It depends entirely on how supersession is keyed, and the obvious keying
is wrong. If you key on holder, kind and subject, a correction and the fact it corrects sit in
different buckets and both stay live — so a retrieval returns two contradictory answers and the model
picks one. I measured that on our desk: two live memos about one billing address, from one
conversation. The fix was two things. Key on holder and subject only, so different kinds compete,
and give each kind an authority number so a same-day correction beats the fact rather than tying with
it. The subtle part is the ordering: date first, then authority. Corrections beat what they
corrected, but they must not beat a genuine change made a year later, and if you compare authority
first they do."*

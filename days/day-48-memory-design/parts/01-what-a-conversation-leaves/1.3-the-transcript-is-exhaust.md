# 1.3 — The transcript is exhaust

## One-line answer

The raw turns of a conversation are what *produced* the memory, not the memory itself, and the right
place for them is the session store you already have rather than the memory store you are about to
fill.

## The story

A building goes up behind hoarding for most of a year. Every morning there is scaffolding: poles,
boards, a hoist, a temporary staircase, plastic sheeting.

Then one week it all comes down and there is a building.

Nobody stands in the road afterwards saying it was a shame about the scaffolding. It was never the
point. It was how you got up there to do the work, and once the work is done it is in the way — it
blocks the light, it takes up the pavement, and anybody arriving now would have to walk round it to
see the thing they came for.

It is also not *thrown away* in the sense that matters. It goes back to the yard. If a window has to
be replaced next year it comes out again. What changes is that it is no longer standing in front of
the building, presented to every passer-by as though it were part of it.

## The idea in plain language

A support conversation is scaffolding around a small number of durable facts.

Ten turns of dialogue produced five memos in [1.2](1.2-six-kinds-not-one-bucket.md). The other five
turns were how the customer and the agent *got to* those five things: a greeting, a holding
sentence, a question, a thank-you, a "let me check". Real, necessary, and finished.

The mistake is to think the choice is between keeping the transcript and losing it. It is not. Sutra
already has two stores, and they were built on two different days:

- The **session store** ([Day 47](../../../day-47-persistent-sessions/LESSON.md)) holds the
  conversation, in order, as it happened. It is the faithful record. It survives a restart because
  Day 47 put a database behind it.
- The **memory store** ([Day 46](../../../day-46-sessions-vs-memory/LESSON.md)) holds what a *later*
  conversation should be told. It is the useful record.

Promoting a transcript into the memory store does not preserve it — it was already preserved. What
it does is put the scaffolding in front of the building: every future retrieval now has to walk
round ten turns of dialogue to find three sentences.

There is a second reason, and it is the one that survives when somebody points out that storage is
cheap. A retrieval does not return "the store". It returns entries, and every returned entry is
pasted into the prompt and paid for on that turn and every turn after it
([Day 24](../../../day-24-token-accounting-and-budgets/parts/01-what-a-request-costs/1.2-charged-again-every-turn.md)).
Storage is cheap. **Context is not**, and a transcript is the single largest thing you could put in
it.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because Day 46's `add_session_to_memory(session)` does exactly the thing this part argues against,
and it does it in one line.

Read what the installed service actually does with a session:

```text
self._session_events[user_key][session.id] = [
    event
    for event in session.events
    if event.content and event.content.parts
]
```

Every event that has any content at all. No filter, no kind, no expiry. That is the default, it is
the thing one line of code buys you, and Sutra needs a written reason not to use it that way.

You meet the consequence on Day 49, where an embedding index is built over whatever this store
holds. Indexing scaffolding produces an index of scaffolding.

## The paper behind it

> **MemGPT: Towards LLMs as Operating Systems** · `arXiv:2310.08560` · 2023 ·
> `https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.08560`

It proposed treating an agent's memory as a **tiered store** — a small fast tier the model sees
directly and a large slow tier it pages things in from — with the agent itself deciding what gets
moved between them.

The paper is taught on Day 20, at
[`../../../day-20-context-engineering-compaction/papers/01-memgpt.md`](../../../day-20-context-engineering-compaction/papers/01-memgpt.md).
The half that matters here is the *eviction* half rather than the paging half: the argument that
what leaves the fast tier and in what form is a design decision the system makes deliberately, not
something that happens to it when a window fills up.

## The mechanism

Measure it. Two numbers and a ratio, no model needed.

```python
# days/day-48-memory-design/lab/exhaust.py
def main() -> None:
    raw = transcript()
    kept, refused = what_to_keep(CANDIDATES, today=date(2026, 9, 5))
    memo_text = "\n".join(memo.text for memo in kept)

    print(f"{'the transcript':<22}{len(TURNS):>7}{'-':>7}{len(raw):>8}{len(raw.split()):>7}")
    print(
        f"{'what was kept':<22}{'-':>7}{len(kept):>7}{len(memo_text):>8}{len(memo_text.split()):>7}"
    )
    print(f"\nthe kept form is {len(memo_text) / len(raw):.0%} of the transcript by characters.")
```

**Line by line:**

- `transcript()` joins every turn with `who: text`, which is the shape a session store actually
  holds. Measuring the bare message text without the speaker labels would flatter the transcript by
  a few percent and would not be what gets filed.
- `what_to_keep(...)` is the policy from section 2, called here rather than reimplemented, so this
  script cannot drift away from the rules the rest of the day teaches.
- `memo_text` joins the accepted memos the same way a retrieval would present them — newline
  separated, one memo per line. This is the fair comparison: what a later prompt would carry in each
  case.
- `len(raw.split())` is a word count, printed beside the character count because characters alone
  invite the objection *"but tokens are not characters"*. They are not, which is why
  [6.1](../06-the-price/6.1-charged-again-on-every-turn.md) runs the same two strings through the
  real tokeniser and gets a different ratio.
- The percentage is formatted `:.0%` rather than as a raw float, because the number that gets
  quoted in a design review is a percentage and rounding it here stops three people rounding it three
  ways later.

**Line by line:**

- One arm, no ablation flag: the two rows *are* the comparison. The ablation for this idea lives in

Measured on 2026-09-05:

```text
                        turns  items   chars  words
the transcript             10      -     698    110
what was kept               -      5     294     44

refused: 2
  - guess        guess: keep_for_days = 0
  - transcript   transcript: keep_for_days = 0

the kept form is 42% of the transcript by characters.
The transcript is not deleted. It stays in the session store; it is not promoted.
```

Two things in that output are worth pausing on.

**42% is not an impressive number, and it should not be.** A ten-turn conversation is short and
tidy. The compression is unremarkable here because the example is small — but the *ratio holds* as
conversations grow, and a forty-turn ticket still produces about five durable facts. The saving is
not 58%; it is 58% of a number that keeps growing while the memo count does not.

**The `transcript` kind is refused by name.** It is not filtered out by a length check or a heuristic
about pleasantries. It is a row in the policy that says `keep_for_days = 0`, with a written reason —
*"kept in the session store, never promoted to memory"*. The distinction matters: a heuristic is
something you tune, and a row is something you argue with.

## When it breaks

The obvious break is filing the transcript anyway, which produces no error and is covered in
[1.1](1.1-the-chat-nobody-can-leave.md).

The break worth teaching here is the opposite one, and it is the mistake made by people who have
understood this part too well: **throwing the scaffolding away instead of returning it to the yard.**

A team reads "the transcript is exhaust", decides the session store is redundant now that memos
exist, and stops persisting sessions. Then somebody disputes a memo. The memo says
`source: "ticket 4521 turn 7"` — and there is no turn 7 any more, because the conversation it names
was never kept.

What you get is a memo that cites evidence that does not exist:

```text
decision      ticket-4521           2029-09-04  ticket 4521 turn 7
    rule: What we did and why. Never replaced: a later decision is a second decision.
```

That `source` field is a promise. Every memo in this design points back at a turn in a conversation,
and the whole audit story of [5.3](../05-failure-lab/5.3-the-rule-that-deleted-the-evidence.md)
depends on being able to follow the pointer.

So the rule is precise, and both halves are load-bearing: **the transcript is not promoted to
memory, and it is not deleted either.** Its retention is the session store's business, on its own
schedule, and section 3's expiry rules apply to it there — not here.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead:** an extraction step between the two stores, with the memo
carrying a stable pointer back into the session store — session id and event id, not a turn number
that renumbers if anything is ever edited. `ticket 4521 turn 7` is fine for a lab and wrong for a
system, because turn numbers are positions and positions move.

**What degrades at scale:** the session store, not the memory store. Memos grow slowly, roughly with
the number of *decisions* a customer generates. Transcripts grow with the number of *messages*, which
is one to two orders of magnitude faster. That is why the two get different databases and different
retention periods in every real system: a session store commonly holds ninety days and a memo store
holds years, and neither number makes sense for the other. Under concurrency the session store is
also the hot one — it is written on every turn while the memory store is written once at the end.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"`add_session_to_memory` is not an extraction step,
it is a copy. If we call it, our memory store is a second copy of our session store with worse
indexing and no retention policy. Show me the function that turns a closed session into memos and
show me what it refuses."*

**The interview question:** *"You have a conversation store and a memory store. What is the
difference?"* The honest spoken answer: *"Faithfulness versus usefulness. The session store's job is
to record what was said, including the wrong things, because that is the evidence. The memory
store's job is to tell the next conversation what is true, which means it must not contain the wrong
things. On our desk a ten-turn ticket was 698 characters of transcript and 294 characters of memos —
five durable items — and the transcript was refused by name in the policy rather than filtered out by
a heuristic. The part people get wrong in both directions is that refusing to promote a transcript is
not the same as deleting it. Every memo cites the turn it came from, so if we deleted the
conversation we would have memos citing evidence that no longer exists, and the first time anybody
disputes one we would have nothing to show them."*

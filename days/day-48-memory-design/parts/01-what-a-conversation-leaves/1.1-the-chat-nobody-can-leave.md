# 1.1 — The chat nobody can leave

## One-line answer

Keeping everything is not the absence of a memory policy, it is a memory policy, and it is the one
policy nobody wrote down or agreed to.

## The story

Someone starts a group chat to organise a birthday party. Nine people, one weekend, a venue and a
cake.

The party happens. The chat does not stop. Two years later it is still there, and it is where the
building's plumbing, three school runs, four sets of holiday photographs and one long argument about
a shared electricity bill now live. Nobody left, because leaving looks rude.

Then you need the address of the flat where the party was, because you have to go back there.

You know it is in the chat. You scroll. You pass the photographs, the argument, the plumber's phone
number, the same joke three times. Twenty screens later you find something that says *"top floor,
the one with the blue door"* and you still do not have the number of the building. You give up and
ring someone.

The address is in there. It was always in there. Nothing was deleted and nothing was corrupted. The
chat kept a perfect record and the perfect record is why you could not use it.

Now notice the part that has nothing to do with scrolling. Somewhere in those two years, one person
posted a new phone number and, four screens later, corrected it. Both messages are still in the
chat. Neither is marked. If you go looking for that number today, the record is not merely hard to
search — it holds two answers to one question and says nothing about which one is current.

## The idea in plain language

A conversation and a memory are two different things, and the difference is not size.

A **conversation** is a record of what was said, in the order it was said, including everything
that turned out to be wrong, everything that was polite rather than informative, and everything
that was superseded ten seconds later. Its job is to be faithful.

A **memory** is what you want to be true and useful the *next* time. Its job is to be right.

Those two jobs pull in opposite directions. Faithfulness says keep the wrong answer, because it was
said. Usefulness says drop it, because repeating it would be a mistake. You cannot have one store
that does both jobs well, and the store that quietly tries is the one that ends up giving a
confident wrong answer from a message somebody corrected two years ago.

So there is a decision to be made about every single thing a conversation leaves behind: does it
go into the memory, in what form, and for how long? That decision is what this whole day is
about, and the first thing to understand is that **you cannot avoid making it**. If you never write
the decision down, you have still made one. Its content is "keep everything, forever, unranked,
with no way to tell the corrected from the current" — and it is the worst one available, because it
is the only one nobody reviewed.

Two words for the rest of the day:

- A **memo** is one thing you decided to remember: one fact, one decision, one correction. Small,
  addressed, with a date on it.
- A **retention policy** is the written rule that decides which things become memos and which do
  not.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because [Day 46](../../../day-46-sessions-vs-memory/LESSON.md) gave Sutra a memory service and
[Day 47](../../../day-47-persistent-sessions/LESSON.md) made its sessions survive a restart, and
between them they have armed exactly this trap. Filing a whole conversation is one line of code.
One-line powers get used without thought.

The support desk you have been building since Day 5 talks to the same customers repeatedly. That is
the entire reason it wants a memory. But a support conversation is *mostly* the wrong material: a
greeting, a "let me check that for you", a guess the agent floated and dropped, a number that was
true that morning, and — buried in it — three sentences that are worth keeping for years.

Day 49 puts a retrieval index over whatever this store holds. An index does not fix the store; it
finds things in it faster, including the wrong things. The decision has to be made here, at write
time, because nothing downstream can undo it.

## The mechanism

Two stores, one question, one matcher. The matcher is not invented for this lab: it is the rule
`InMemoryMemoryService` actually applies, copied out of the installed ADK so the numbers are real.

```python
# days/day-48-memory-design/lab/noise.py
import re


def words(text: str) -> set[str]:
    """Lowercase word set. This is ADK's `_extract_words_lower`, copied so the lab can run it."""
    return {word.lower() for word in re.findall(r"\w+", text, re.UNICODE)}


def hits(entries: list[str], query: str) -> list[str]:
    """Every entry sharing at least one word with the query - ADK's matching rule, exactly."""
    query_words = words(query)
    return [entry for entry in entries if query_words & words(entry)]
```

**Line by line:**

- `re.findall(r"\w+", text, re.UNICODE)` splits on anything that is not a word character, so
  punctuation and case disappear. It is copied verbatim from
  `.venv/Lib/site-packages/google/adk/memory/in_memory_memory_service.py`, where the function is
  called `_extract_words_lower`. Reading the installed source rather than guessing is the rule here
  (Principle 8), and in this case it changes the lesson: the matcher is far blunter than most people
  assume.
- `set(...)` and not a list, because the match test that follows is set intersection, and a word
  appearing four times in a message must not count four times.
- `query_words & words(entry)` is the whole matching rule: **any** shared word is a hit. Not all
  words, not a score, not a threshold. ADK's own line is
  `if any(query_word in words_in_event for query_word in words_in_query)`, which is the same test
  written as a loop.
- That means a query containing the word `the` matches every entry containing the word `the`. This
  is not a bug being reported; it is the behaviour of the service Day 46 wired in, and it is exactly
  why what you put in the store decides what comes out of it.
- The functions take and return plain lists of strings rather than ADK `MemoryEntry` objects,
  because the point being measured is about *content*, and dragging the framework in would make the
  script harder to read without changing a single number.

Now the two stores. The question is one a support agent actually asks.

```bash
cd days/day-48-memory-design/lab
uv run python noise.py
uv run python noise.py --policy
cd -
```

**Line by line:**

- `cd` into the lab first, because these scripts import each other (`noise.py` imports `policy.py`
  and `taxonomy.py`) and a sibling import needs the sibling's directory on the path.
- `uv run python` rather than a bare `python`, so the script runs against the environment
  `pyproject.toml` pins rather than whichever Python happens to be first on `PATH`.
- The bare run is the *keep everything* store: one entry per turn of the conversation, which is what
  `add_session_to_memory` files.
- `--policy` is the ablation. Same conversation, same question, same matcher — the only thing that
  changes is that the store holds what a written policy accepted instead of everything that was
  said. An ablation is the difference between showing that code ran and showing that this idea
  mattered.
- `cd -` returns you to the repository root, which is where every other command in this curriculum
  is run from.

Measured on 2026-09-05:

```text
store: everything
entries held: 10
question: "what is the billing email for this account"
entries returned: 9

of those, on the subject asked about: 3
characters handed to the model: 653
```

```text
store: policy
entries held: 5
question: "what is the billing email for this account"
entries returned: 5

of those, on the subject asked about: 3
characters handed to the model: 290
```

Read the third line of each block, then the last two.

**The same three useful entries came back both times.** The curated store did not know more. It knew
the same things. What changed is that the first store also handed the model *"One moment, let me
check that for you"*, *"And the invoices?"* and *"Great, thanks for your help!"* — 653 characters
against 290, for identical information.

That ratio is the cost of never deciding. It is paid on every turn, and section 6 puts it in tokens.

## When it breaks

The failure has no error message, which is why it survives so long.

Both runs above returned something. Both looked like they worked. A test that asserts *"searching
memory returns results"* passes against both stores, and that is very often the only test there is.

The break you can actually see is what happens when the noise starts to *outrank* the signal.
Watch the second block again: **five held, five returned.** The curated store returned everything it
had, because the query contained the words `is` and `the` and almost every English sentence contains
those too. That is the honest finding from reading the installed matcher: it has no stopword list,
no scoring and no cutoff.

So a curated store is not a store that filters well at read time. It is a store where returning
everything is affordable. The keyword matcher's weakness is not fixed by curation — it is *made
survivable* by it.

There is a second break, and it is worse because it produces a confident answer rather than a
messy one. In the keep-everything run, two of the nine returned entries are about the billing
address and **they disagree**: the customer's first message names one address, and a later message
corrects it. Both are in the store, neither is marked, and the model reading them has no way to tell
which is current. That is the subject of [1.4](1.4-a-correction-outranks-it.md).

## In production

**What a professional writes instead:** not a smarter search. A written policy, reviewed by someone
who is not an engineer, that decides at write time what enters the store. Search quality is bought
at write time and it cannot be bought back later. Every read-time trick — reranking, filtering,
scoring — is an attempt to undo a decision that was already made badly, and it costs a model call
per query to partly undo it.

**What degrades at scale:** the number of conversations per customer. One ticket produces a store
you can eyeball. Forty tickets over three years produce a store where the keyword matcher returns
dozens of entries for any common query, and every one of them is pasted into the prompt. The
context window is fixed; the store is not. Under concurrency it degrades a second way — two agents
filing the same customer's sessions at once produce duplicate entries with no way to tell a
duplicate from a genuine repetition, because nothing in a raw turn is a key.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"You have added a memory service and no retention
policy, so the policy is 'keep everything' and it is enforced by nobody. Before this merges, I want
the list of what we write down and the reason for each row, and I want it in a file our support lead
can read. If the answer is that we cannot enumerate what is worth keeping, then we have not thought
about it, and thinking about it is the work."*

**The interview question:** *"Your agent has a memory. What goes in it?"* The honest spoken answer:
*"The first thing I would say is that 'everything' is an answer to that question, not a way of
dodging it — and it is the answer you get by default if you never write one down. On our support
desk I measured it: the same customer question against a store holding every turn and a store
holding five curated memos returned the same three useful entries, but the raw store also handed the
model 653 characters instead of 290 to do it. The extra characters were greetings. And the raw store
held two contradictory billing addresses with nothing marking which was current, because the
customer had corrected themselves mid-conversation. That is the real cost — not the space, the
contradiction. So we decided what enters the store at write time, in a table with a reason per row,
and the search stayed exactly as dumb as it was."*

# 1.2 — Six kinds, not one bucket

## One-line answer

"What should the agent remember?" has no useful answer until you split the question by *kind*,
because a fact, a decision, a correction and a guess want four different lifetimes and only one of
them wants none.

## The story

A doctor writes two lines on the same afternoon, on the same page, in the same handwriting.

The first line says **allergic to penicillin**. The second says **seemed tired today**.

Thirty years later, in a different city, at three in the morning, one of those lines can stop
somebody being killed by a routine injection. The other one is about a Tuesday afternoon when
somebody had not slept well.

They cost the same to write. They take the same space on the page. They look identical to anything
that reads the page as text. And the difference between them is not how *important* they sounded at
the time — it is that one of them is still true and the other one stopped being true by Thursday.

Now imagine a filing system that treats both lines as "a note about the patient", keeps both
forever, and shows both to whoever is on duty. It has not been careless with the tired line. It has
been careless with the penicillin line, because it buried it.

## The idea in plain language

A conversation does not leave behind "information". It leaves behind several *different sorts* of
thing, and they behave differently over time.

Here are the six that a support conversation actually produces. The names are ours; what matters is
the behaviour in the right-hand column.

| Kind | What it is | How it behaves over time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **customer fact** | Something true about the customer: their plan, their billing address, their timezone. | Stays true until they change it. A newer one replaces it. |
| **correction** | The customer telling you that you had something wrong. | Stays true, and beats whatever it corrects. |
| **decision** | What was done and the reason it was done. | Never becomes false. A later decision is a second decision, not a replacement. |
| **quote** | A number that was true on a date: a price, a delivery estimate, a queue length. | Rots. Wrong within weeks, and confidently wrong. |
| **guess** | A hypothesis nobody confirmed: *"might be a proxy issue"*. | Should never have been written down at all. |
| **transcript** | The raw turns, in order. | Belongs in the session store, not in memory. |

Two of those six have a lifetime of **zero**, and that is the most useful thing in the table.

A **guess** is dangerous precisely because storage strips the hedging. *"It might be a proxy issue,
I'm not sure"* goes in. Three months later a search returns *"proxy issue"* with a date and a ticket
number attached, and it now reads like a finding. Nothing lied. The store simply has no column for
*"the person who said this did not believe it"*.

A **transcript** is not dangerous, it is just the wrong shape — which is [1.3](1.3-the-transcript-is-exhaust.md).

The other four are all worth keeping, and they want four different rules. That is the whole reason
the taxonomy exists: not to be tidy, but because you cannot write one retention rule that is right
for a billing address and right for a price quote at the same time.

**A note on the word "kind".** In academic writing about memory you will meet *episodic* (what
happened), *semantic* (what is generally true) and *working* (what is in front of you right now).
That split is real and it is worth knowing for a conversation with somebody who uses those words.
It is not the split that helps you here, because it groups by *where the knowledge came from* rather
than by *when it stops being true* — and when it stops being true is the only question a retention
policy can act on.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because from here on, every rule in Sutra's policy is written against one of these kinds, and a kind
with no row in the table is refused ([2.3](../02-policy-as-data/2.3-the-kind-with-no-rule.md)).

Concretely: section 3 gives `quote` an expiry date and gives `decision` a long one. Section 4 gives
every kind a redaction rule and a holder. Section 6 prices them separately, and finds that the two
kinds with no expiry are the ones you pay for forever. None of those rules can be written until the
kinds exist.

You will meet this again on Day 49, where the retrieval index has to decide what a "document" is.
A memo of a declared kind is a document. A conversation is not — it is six documents wearing one
coat.

## The mechanism

The extraction is done by hand in this lab, and that is a deliberate choice rather than a shortcut.
A model could propose candidates from a transcript. But the argument of this day is that the
*judging* must not depend on a model's mood, and the whole day runs on zero generations.

```python
# days/day-48-memory-design/lab/taxonomy.py
from policy import RULES, Candidate
from session import TICKET, TURNS

CANDIDATES: list[Candidate] = [
    Candidate(
        kind="customer_fact",
        holder="acct-88",
        subject="billing_email",
        text="Billing email is asha.k@example.com.",
        source=f"ticket {TICKET} turn 0",
    ),
    Candidate(
        kind="correction",
        holder="acct-88",
        subject="billing_email",
        text="Billing goes to accounts@example.com; the other address is personal.",
        source=f"ticket {TICKET} turn 2",
    ),
    Candidate(
        kind="guess",
        holder="acct-88",
        subject="plan",
        text="They might move to the Business plan next quarter.",
        source=f"ticket {TICKET} turn 4",
    ),
]
```

**Line by line:**

- `Candidate` is the record for something that *wants* to be remembered. It is deliberately not
  called `Memory`: nothing here has been accepted yet. Anybody may propose a candidate; only the
  policy decides.
- `kind` is the first field because it is the field everything else keys off. Every rule in section
  2 is looked up by this string.
- `holder` is who the memo is *about* — the account, not the person who typed. It looks like
  bookkeeping and it is the field that makes deletion on request possible at all
  ([4.2](../04-privacy-and-erasure/4.2-you-cannot-delete-what-you-cannot-address.md)).
- `subject` is what the memo is *about within* that holder: `billing_email`, `plan`,
  `business_plan_price`. Two memos with the same holder and subject are two answers to one question,
  which is how section 3 knows one of them has been superseded. Without this field, supersession has
  nothing to compare.
- `text` is the memo itself, written as a standalone sentence rather than a fragment of dialogue.
  *"Billing goes to the accounts address"* is readable cold; *"Actually no — the other one is
  personal"* is not, and a store full of dialogue fragments is a store nothing can use.
- `source` records where it came from: ticket and turn. This is the citation. A memo you cannot
  trace back to the sentence that produced it is a memo you cannot check when somebody disputes it.
- The two entries shown here are `customer_fact` and `correction` about the **same subject**, which
  is the case [1.4](1.4-a-correction-outranks-it.md) is entirely about.
- The `guess` is included on purpose. It is a real thing the agent said. Leaving it out of the
  candidate list would be cheating — the policy has to be given the chance to refuse it, and refusing
  it has to be visible.

Run it:

```bash
cd days/day-48-memory-design/lab
uv run python taxonomy.py
cd -
```

**Line by line:**

- No flags. This script is a classification, not an experiment: there is nothing to ablate, because
  the point is the table, not a difference between two runs.
- It prints the `keeps?` column by looking up each candidate's rule, so the answer comes from
  `policy.py` rather than from anything typed twice.

Measured on 2026-09-05:

```text
kind          subject               keeps?  text
customer_fact billing_email         yes     Billing email is asha.k@example.com.
correction    billing_email         yes     Billing goes to accounts@example.com; the other a...
customer_fact plan                  yes     Plan is Team, billed yearly.
guess         plan                  no      They might move to the Business plan next quarter.
quote         business_plan_price   yes     Business plan is 4800 a year.
decision      ticket-4521           yes     Re-sent three bounced invoices to the billing add...
transcript    ticket-4521           no      Hi, our invoices are not arriving. ... Great, tha...

```

Seven things a ten-turn conversation left behind. Five of them written down. Two refused before
anything else in the system ever sees them.

## When it breaks

The taxonomy breaks in the direction of laziness: everything gets labelled `customer_fact`, because
that is the row that keeps things forever and asks no questions.

Here is what that looks like when you run it. Take the guess and relabel it, then look at the table
again:

```text
kind          subject               keeps?  text
customer_fact plan                  yes     They might move to the Business plan next quarter.
customer_fact plan                  yes     Plan is Team, billed yearly.
```

Two `customer_fact` rows about `plan`, one of which is a guess wearing a fact's clothes. Because they
now share a holder and subject, section 3's supersession will pick one of them and silently discard
the other. If the guess arrived later, the guess wins.

Nothing raises. Nothing logs. The store is internally consistent and confidently wrong, and the only
evidence that anything happened is one word in one field that a human chose.

The second break is the opposite failure: too many kinds. A team that adds `sentiment`,
`escalation_risk`, `churn_signal` and `tone` ends up with a table nobody reads, which is the same
outcome as no table. The test is not "is this a distinct sort of thing" — almost everything is. The
test is **"does this want a different retention rule from every row already in the table?"** If it
does not, it is not a new kind.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead:** the same table, with two more columns — who approved the
row, and on what date. A retention taxonomy is a governance artifact as much as a technical one,
and the question *"who decided we keep billing addresses forever?"* is asked during audits, not
during code review.

The extraction step, which this lab does by hand, is where a real system spends a model call: an
extractor prompt that reads a closed conversation and proposes candidates with kinds. That is a
sensible use of a generation. What a professional does **not** do is let the extractor invent a
kind. It picks from the table or it returns nothing, exactly as a structured-output schema forces it
to ([Day 12](../../../day-12-structured-output/LESSON.md)).

**What degrades at scale:** the labels drift. Two extractor prompt versions, six months apart, put
the same sentence in different rows, and now the store holds memos whose retention depends on which
version of the prompt was live the day they were written. The fix is boring and works: the kind is
stored on the memo, the prompt version is stored beside it, and a change to the taxonomy triggers a
re-classification job rather than applying only to new memos.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Six kinds is fine. What I want to see is the
`guess` row, because that is the one that pays for the table. If we are not refusing anything, we
have written a taxonomy that describes our data instead of one that governs it — and describing is
free."*

**The interview question:** *"How would you decide what an agent stores in long-term memory?"* The
honest spoken answer: *"By kind, and the sorting question is 'when does this stop being true?'
rather than 'is this important?'. Importance is a judgement you make once and cannot check; expiry
is a property of the thing. On our support desk it came out as six kinds, and the useful part was
that two of them have a lifetime of zero. Raw transcripts stay in the session store and never get
promoted. Guesses never get written at all, because storage strips the hedging — 'might be a proxy
issue, not sure' goes in with a caveat and comes out three months later as a finding with a ticket
number attached. That is not the model being gullible. That is the store having no column for
doubt."*

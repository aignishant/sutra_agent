# 2.3 — The kind with no rule

## One-line answer

The most important row in a retention policy is the one that is not there: what happens to something
nobody wrote a rule for, and the only safe answer is that it is refused.

## The story

A lost-and-found counter has shelves, and each shelf has a label. Umbrellas. Keys. Bags. Phones.
Coats. Each label has a rule on it: how long the thing stays before it goes to a charity, who can
claim it, what proof they need.

Somebody hands in a sealed envelope with a name on it.

There is no shelf for that. The person behind the counter has a choice, and it will be made in about
four seconds, by whoever happens to be on shift, and it will not be written down.

They can put it in the bag shelf, because that is closest. Now the envelope inherits the bag rule —
kept for three months, handed over to anyone who describes it — and nobody has decided that is
appropriate for a sealed envelope with a name on it.

Or they can say: there is no shelf for this, I am not going to invent one, it goes in the manager's
drawer until somebody tells me what the rule is.

The second answer is more annoying. It requires a conversation. It also means that the day somebody
finally writes the envelope rule, they write it on purpose, rather than discovering by accident that
sealed envelopes have been treated as bags for two years.

## The idea in plain language

Every policy is a list of the cases somebody thought of. That is not a criticism — it is what a list
is. And the number of cases nobody thought of only ever goes up, because new code proposes new
things.

So the question is not whether an unlisted case will arrive. It is what your policy does when one
does, and there are exactly two answers:

- **Default keep.** No rule means no objection. Write it down. This feels helpful and it is what
  happens when nobody chooses.
- **Default refuse.** No rule means nobody has approved this. Do not write it down; report it.

They differ in one word and they produce opposite systems, and — this is what makes the argument hard
to win — **they behave identically on every case anybody has thought of**. Run both against the six
kinds in the table and they agree six times out of six. The difference only appears in the case
that, by definition, was not in the test suite.

The name for choosing the second one is **fail-safe defaults**: when a decision cannot be made from
the rules you have, the answer that costs least when it is wrong is the one that refuses.

Work out what each mistake costs here, because that is the whole argument.

**Default refuse, wrongly:** something useful is not remembered. Somebody notices within days,
because a thing they expected to be there is not. It is fixed by adding a row, which takes one line
and one conversation. The cost is paid by the person who wanted the memo, early, with an obvious
symptom.

**Default keep, wrongly:** something nobody approved is written down, permanently, with no expiry,
because there was no rule to give it one. It cannot be expired, because expiry comes from a row. It
was never redacted, because redaction comes from a row. Nobody knows it is there. The cost is paid
by whoever eventually has to explain what the company holds about a customer, months or years later.

Those two are not symmetric, and the asymmetry is the reason for the choice.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because Sutra's memory will be fed by more than one thing.

Today the candidates are typed out by hand. Later there is an extractor, and after that there is a
second extractor for a different kind of ticket, and at some point somebody adds a summariser that
proposes a memo per closed session. Each of those is a new source proposing new kinds, written by
somebody who has not read the retention table.

This is the same argument Day 40 made about tools arriving from a server you do not control, and it
is worth noticing that the *shape* is identical: a list of things you thought of, meeting something
you did not. Day 40's answer was an allowlist. This part's answer is the same answer, applied to
storage instead of capability.

Section 4 depends on it completely. Every obligation there — redact this, expire that, delete on
request — is expressed as a column in a row. A memo with no row has none of those properties, so a
default-keep policy is a machine for producing memories that are exempt from your privacy rules.

## The paper behind it

> **The protection of information in computer systems** · `doi:10.1109/PROC.1975.9939` · 1975 ·
> `https://doi.org/10.1109/PROC.1975.9939`

It gave the field the principle this part applies: **fail-safe defaults** — base access decisions on
permission rather than exclusion, so that a mistake in the rules refuses rather than permits.

The paper is taught on Day 40, at
[`../../../day-40-filtering-and-allowlists/papers/01-protection-of-information.md`](../../../day-40-filtering-and-allowlists/papers/01-protection-of-information.md).
It is written about access to information rather than retention of it, and the translation is exact:
a memo with no rule is a permission nobody granted.

## The mechanism

The choice is one method call, and it is easy to miss in review.

```python
# days/day-48-memory-design/lab/policy.py
rule = RULES.get(candidate.kind)
if rule is None:
    refused.append(Refusal(candidate, f"no rule for kind {candidate.kind!r}"))
    continue
```

**Line by line:**

- `RULES.get(...)` returns `None` for a kind with no row, rather than raising `KeyError`. Both are
  refusals in effect; the difference is that this one is a *reported* refusal that a batch can carry
  on past, and a `KeyError` is a crash that takes the other six candidates with it. Neither one
  silently keeps, which is the property that matters.
- `if rule is None` is checked **first**, before the `keep_for_days` test, because the second test
  cannot run without a rule.
- The refusal message quotes the kind with `!r`, so a kind that is an empty string or has trailing
  whitespace is visible in the log rather than reading as a mysterious blank.
- `continue` rather than a raise. This function's contract is that it judges a whole list and returns
  everything it knows ([2.2](2.2-every-verdict-names-its-row.md)); one unlisted kind must not stop
  six good candidates from being written.
- What is **not** here is the important part: there is no `else` branch giving the unlisted candidate
  a default rule. Adding `rule = rule or DEFAULT_RULE` would be one line, would look like
  robustness, and would flip the whole system to default-keep.

The permissive version, written out in the lab so both can be run:

```python
# days/day-48-memory-design/lab/unknown_kind.py
def keep_permissively(
    candidates: list[Candidate], *, today: date
) -> tuple[list[Memo], list[Refusal]]:
    """The default that feels helpful: no rule means no objection, so write it down forever."""
    kept: list[Memo] = []
    for candidate in candidates:
        rule = RULES.get(candidate.kind)
        if rule is not None and rule.keep_for_days == 0:
            continue
        days = rule.keep_for_days if rule else None
        text = redact(candidate.text)[0]
        kept.append(
            Memo(
                kind=candidate.kind,
                holder=candidate.holder,
                subject=candidate.subject,
                text=text,
                source=candidate.source,
                recorded_on=today,
                expires_on=None if days is None else today + timedelta(days=days),
            )
        )
    return kept, []
```

**Line by line:**

- `if rule is not None and rule.keep_for_days == 0` still honours the rules that exist. This is not a
  strawman that ignores the policy — it obeys every row and differs only on the case with no row.
- `days = rule.keep_for_days if rule else None` is where the harm happens, and it is one line that
  reads as sensible. An unlisted kind gets `None`, which means **no expiry**. The most permissive
  possible retention is handed out to the one category nobody reviewed.
- `redact(candidate.text)[0]` is called unconditionally, because there is no `redact` column to read
  without a rule. Here that happens to be the safe choice; note that it was made by the author of
  this function rather than by the policy, which is the deeper problem.
- It returns `[]` for refusals, and the empty list is honest: this function never refuses anything,
  so there is nothing to report. A caller reading a refusal count sees zero and concludes all is well.

Run both:

```bash

uv run python unknown_kind.py --permissive
cd -
```

**Line by line:**

- The three candidates are one known kind and two invented ones — `sentiment` and `internal_note` —
  which are exactly the sort of thing a second extractor starts proposing six months in.
- `--permissive` swaps which `what_to_keep` is called. Nothing else differs.

Measured on 2026-09-05:

```text
default for an unlisted kind: refuse
kept: 1
  customer_fact  in RETENTION      never expires Plan is Team, billed yearly.
refused: 2
  sentiment      no rule for kind 'sentiment'
  internal_note  no rule for kind 'internal_note'

memos in the store that no written rule covers: 0
```

```text
default for an unlisted kind: keep
kept: 3
  customer_fact  in RETENTION      never expires Plan is Team, billed yearly.
  sentiment      NOT IN RETENTION  never expires Customer sounded annoyed about the third bou
  internal_note  NOT IN RETENTION  never expires Flagged for the retention team; do not offer

refused: 0

memos in the store that no written rule covers: 2
Nobody reviewed these. Nobody can expire them. They have no row to argue with.
```

Read the `never expires` column on the two `NOT IN RETENTION` rows.

Nothing went wrong. No exception, no warning, no log line. Two memories now exist permanently, one of
which is a judgement about a customer's mood and the other is an internal instruction not to offer a
discount — and neither will ever appear in a review, because reviews walk the table and these have no
row in it.

## When it breaks

The refusing default breaks the way an allowlist breaks: something you wanted is missing, and it
tells you so immediately.

```text
refused: 2
  sentiment      no rule for kind 'sentiment'
```

That is a person's Tuesday afternoon. They added an extractor, ran it, and the memos are not there.
They read one line, add a row to `RETENTION` with a reason and a retention period, and it works. The
policy got reviewed because the system made somebody look at it.

The permissive default breaks in a way with no message, and the moment it becomes visible is
usually somebody else's problem. Here is the realistic version. A customer exercises their right to
know what is held about them. Somebody assembles the answer by walking the retention table, because
that is the document that says what the system stores. The `sentiment` memos are not in the table, so
they are not in the answer, and the answer is now incomplete in a way nobody has any way to notice.

There is a third break worth naming because it is an argument people make in good faith:
*"default-refuse will lose data during an incident."* It is a real cost. A misconfigured extractor
proposing a slightly-wrong kind name — `customer_facts` with an `s` — refuses everything, silently
from the extractor's point of view, and a day of memos is lost. The defence is not to change the
default; it is that refusals are counted ([2.2](2.2-every-verdict-names-its-row.md)), and a refusal
rate that jumps from two percent to a hundred is an alert. **The refusing default is safe *because*
its failures are loud, so the cost of taking the alerting away is that you have kept the annoyance
and thrown away the safety.**

## In production

**What a professional writes instead:** the same refusal, plus a named alert on the refusal rate per
kind, plus one more thing the lab does not have — a **quarantine**. Refused candidates for unlisted
kinds go to a table nobody reads at query time, with a short retention of their own, so that a person
adding a rule next week can see what they would have kept. That is not a default-keep in disguise:
the quarantine is not searched, not retrieved, and expires on its own.

**What degrades at scale:** the vocabulary drifts faster than the table. Once several teams propose
memos, kind names multiply — `customer_fact`, `customerFact`, `fact` — and each variant is refused
correctly and separately, so a real problem shows up as three small refusal streams rather than one
obvious one. The fix is that kind is a closed enumeration validated at the boundary, so a bad name
fails where it is typed rather than where it is stored.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"`RULES.get(kind)` returning `None` needs to refuse,
not fall through to a default rule. I know the fallback looks defensive, but the fallback's retention
is 'forever' and its redaction is 'whatever the caller felt like' — so the one category nobody
reviewed gets the most permissive treatment in the system. And add the refusal-rate alert in the same
PR, because a refusing default without alerting is just data loss with good intentions."*

**The interview question:** *"What does your policy do with a case it does not cover?"* The honest
spoken answer: *"Refuses it and says so. The reason is the asymmetry of the two mistakes. If we
wrongly refuse, someone notices in days because the thing they expected is missing, and it is fixed by
adding one row after one conversation. If we wrongly keep, we have written something permanent that
nobody approved — and crucially it has no expiry and no redaction rule, because both of those come
from the row it does not have. So the unreviewed category gets the most permissive treatment in the
whole system. I measured it on our desk: two invented kinds went into a permissive policy and came out
as memos that never expire, with a refusal count of zero, so nothing anywhere said it had happened.
The thing I would add in production is a quarantine and an alert on the refusal rate — a refusing
default without alerting is data loss with good intentions."*

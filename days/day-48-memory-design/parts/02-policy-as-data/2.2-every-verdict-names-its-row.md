# 2.2 — Every verdict names its row

## One-line answer

A decision to keep or drop something must come back carrying the rule that made it, because a
verdict without a citation cannot be checked, appealed or improved.

## The story

A parking ticket lands under your wiper.

The bad version says: **Violation. £60.** You look at it, you look at the car, you look at the space.
You are fairly sure you were allowed to park there. You have nothing to work with. The only thing you
can do is complain in general terms, and the only thing anyone can do in reply is repeat that you
were in violation.

The good version says: **Parked in a residents' bay, Mon–Fri 08:00–18:00, without a permit displayed.
Section 4b.** Now you can do something specific. You can point at the sign, which says the bay is
residents-only *until* six, and it was seven. Or you can find the permit in the glovebox and
photograph it on the dashboard. Or you can read section 4b and discover that you were, in fact,
wrong.

All three of those outcomes are better than the first ticket, including the one where you lose.

The second ticket is not more polite. It costs the same £60. What it does is put the *reason* in your
hands, and a reason in your hands is the only thing that lets a wrong decision get corrected — and
the only thing that lets a right one be accepted.

## The idea in plain language

Every time the policy looks at something, three things come out of it: the thing, what happened to
it, and **which row decided**.

That third part is what makes the system reviewable. Without it you have a store and an absence, and
an absence explains nothing. Somebody asks *"why isn't the Business plan price in memory?"* and the
answer is either a shrug or an engineer reading code.

So the design uses four small records, and the shape of them is the lesson:

- A **Candidate** is something that wants to be remembered. Anyone may propose one.
- A **Memo** is a candidate the policy accepted, with its expiry date already worked out.
- A **Refusal** is a candidate the policy rejected, carrying the reason a reviewer will be shown.
- A **Verdict** is what the policy later decided about a memo, and why.

Notice what is *not* in that list: there is no "silently dropped". A candidate either becomes a memo
or becomes a refusal, and both are returned. The function does not filter a list — it **splits**
one, and the second half is as much output as the first.

This is a small design decision with a large consequence. A filter throws away the thing it rejects
and the reason it rejected it in the same instant. A split keeps both, so the refusals can be logged,
counted, sampled and read. The first time somebody asks whether the policy is too aggressive, the
answer is a list rather than an opinion.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because Principle 10 says errors surface, escalate and are logged, and a refusal is not an error but
it obeys the same argument: **never make something disappear without saying so.**

Concretely, three things in later days depend on refusals being visible.

Section 5's failure lab is only teachable because every drop is printed. The whole of
[5.3](../05-failure-lab/5.3-the-rule-that-deleted-the-evidence.md) is somebody discovering that a
rule threw away the thing an audit needed, and that discovery is only possible if the throwing-away
left a trace.

Day 49's retrieval work needs to know what is *not* in the index and why, or every missing answer
looks like a retrieval bug.

And section 4's erasure story needs the same shape for a different reason: when a customer asks what
you hold about them, "here is what we kept and here is what we refused" is a much better answer than
"here is what we kept".

## The mechanism

The records are four lines each and carry no behaviour.

```python
# days/day-48-memory-design/lab/policy.py
@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Memo:
    """A candidate the policy accepted, with its expiry date already computed."""

    kind: str
    holder: str
    subject: str
    text: str
    source: str
    recorded_on: date
    expires_on: date | None


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Refusal:
    """A candidate the policy refused, and the reason a reviewer will be shown."""

    candidate: Candidate
    why: str
```

**Line by line:**

- `Memo` carries `expires_on` as a **date**, not as the number of days from its rule. The rule said
  thirty days; the memo says the fifth of October. Storing the resolved date means a later change to
  the rule does not silently re-date memos that were already written under the old one, which is the
  difference between a policy change and a rewrite of history.
- `recorded_on` is stored beside it even though `expires_on` could be derived from it, because
  supersession compares recording dates
  ([1.4](../01-what-a-conversation-leaves/1.4-a-correction-outranks-it.md)) and a memo with no expiry
  has no other date at all.
- `Refusal` holds the **whole candidate**, not a summary of it. A refusal you cannot re-examine is a
  refusal you cannot appeal, and the first question anybody asks about a rejected memo is what it
  actually said.
- `why: str` on the refusal is free text on purpose, because it names the rule *and* the value that
  triggered it — `"guess: keep_for_days = 0"` tells you both the row and the column.
- Both are `frozen=True`. A verdict that can be edited after the fact is not a record of a decision.

Now the function that splits:

```python
def what_to_keep(candidates: list[Candidate], *, today: date) -> tuple[list[Memo], list[Refusal]]:
    """Judge every candidate against RETENTION. Returns the memos kept and the refusals."""
    kept: list[Memo] = []
    refused: list[Refusal] = []
    for candidate in candidates:
        rule = RULES.get(candidate.kind)
        if rule is None:
            refused.append(Refusal(candidate, f"no rule for kind {candidate.kind!r}"))
            continue
        if rule.keep_for_days == 0:
            refused.append(Refusal(candidate, f"{rule.kind}: keep_for_days = 0"))
            continue
        text = candidate.text
        if rule.redact:
            text, _ = redact(text)
        expires_on = (
            None if rule.keep_for_days is None else today + timedelta(days=rule.keep_for_days)
        )
        kept.append(
            Memo(
                kind=rule.kind,
                holder=candidate.holder,
                subject=candidate.subject,
                text=text,
                source=candidate.source,
                recorded_on=today,
                expires_on=expires_on,
            )
        )
    return kept, refused
```

**Line by line:**

- The return type is `tuple[list[Memo], list[Refusal]]` — two lists, not one filtered list. That
  signature is the whole argument of this part expressed in a type: a caller cannot use this function
  without receiving the refusals, even if they then ignore them.
- `*, today: date` forces the date to be passed by keyword, and forces it to be passed *at all*.
  There is no `today = date.today()` default anywhere in this module. A policy that reads the clock
  internally cannot be tested against next year, and section 3 does nothing but ask this system what
  it thinks on four different dates.
- `RULES.get(...)` rather than `RULES[...]`, so an unknown kind becomes a refusal with a readable
  reason instead of a `KeyError` from inside a loop. Which of those two is correct is the entire
  subject of [2.3](2.3-the-kind-with-no-rule.md).
- `rule.keep_for_days == 0` is checked **before** anything is built, so a `guess` never becomes a
  `Memo` object at all, even briefly. There is no moment in this function where a refused candidate
  exists in the accepted shape.
- The comparison is `== 0` and not falsy, because `None` is also falsy and `None` means *the
  opposite* — keep forever. `if not rule.keep_for_days` would refuse every permanent memo, which is
  the single most likely bug in this file and it would pass a test suite that only used `quote`.
- `if rule.redact` reads the column rather than always redacting, so the behaviour is stated in the
  table where a reviewer can see it
  ([4.1](../04-privacy-and-erasure/4.1-redact-before-you-write.md)).
- `today + timedelta(days=rule.keep_for_days)` resolves the expiry once, here, at write time.
- `kind=rule.kind` rather than `candidate.kind` — they are equal, and taking it from the rule means
  the stored memo carries the kind the policy actually matched, not the string that was proposed.
- Nothing is logged and nothing is printed inside this function. It returns everything it knows and
  lets the caller decide what to do with it, which is why the same function can be used by a script
  that prints a table and by a service that emits structured logs.

**Line by line:**

- `verdicts.py` calls `what_to_keep` once and prints **both** returned lists, each line followed by
  the `why` of the rule that produced it. It is the audit trail, rendered.

Measured on 2026-09-05:

```text
KEPT
  customer_fact billing_email         no expiry   ticket 4521 turn 0
    rule: True until the customer changes it. A newer fact about the same subject replaces it.
  correction    billing_email         no expiry   ticket 4521 turn 2
    rule: The customer telling us we had it wrong. Outranks whatever it corrects.
  quote         business_plan_price   2026-10-05  ticket 4521 turn 5
    rule: A number that was true on a date. Prices move; a stale quote is worse than none.
  decision      ticket-4521           2029-09-04  ticket 4521 turn 7
    rule: What we did and why. Never replaced: a later decision is a second decision.

REFUSED
  guess         plan                  guess: keep_for_days = 0
    rule: A hypothesis nobody confirmed. Stored, it comes back later wearing a fact's clothes.
  transcript    ticket-4521           transcript: keep_for_days = 0
    rule: The raw turns. Kept in the session store, never promoted to memory.

5 kept, 2 refused, 7 proposed.
```

Every line names its rule. The two refusals are as loud as the five acceptances, and the last line
adds up: seven went in, seven are accounted for.

That last property is worth stating as a rule of its own. **Kept plus refused equals proposed,
always.** If those numbers ever fail to add up, something is being dropped by code that is not the
policy, and you have the split-policy bug from [2.1](2.1-a-rule-someone-else-can-read.md).

## When it breaks

The break is a filter written where a split belongs, and it happens because a filter is shorter:

```python
memos = [make_memo(c, today) for c in candidates if RULES.get(c.kind, ZERO).keep_for_days != 0]
```

That line is correct. It produces exactly the same five memos. It is also, from an operations point
of view, a hole: two candidates went in and vanished, and there is no object anywhere in the process
that remembers they existed.

The symptom arrives as a support question nobody can answer:

```text
"The desk keeps forgetting that this customer was quoted 4800 for the Business
plan. It was definitely said on ticket 4521. Where does it go?"
```

With the split, that is a one-command answer: run the policy over the candidates and read the
`REFUSED` block. With the filter, it is an afternoon of reading code, and the person asking is not
going to be given a satisfying answer either way.

The second break is subtler and shows up as a number that does not add up. If somebody adds an early
`continue` for a candidate that fails some other check — a blank `text`, say — without appending a
`Refusal`, the final line reads `4 kept, 2 refused, 7 proposed`. Seven went in and six came out.
That arithmetic is the cheapest possible test of this whole module, and it is worth an assertion.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead:** the same split, with the refusals emitted as structured log
lines at INFO rather than merely returned — one event per refusal, carrying the kind, the subject,
the rule that fired and the source, but **not the text**. Day 22's structured logging
([`3.1`](../../../day-22-structured-logging/LESSON.md)) is what makes that safe: a refusal is often
refused *because* of what it contains, and logging the content of a rejected memo puts it in a place
with a longer retention than the store you just kept it out of.

A counter goes beside it: refusals per kind, per day. A sudden change in that ratio is the earliest
signal that an upstream extractor has changed behaviour, and it is visible long before anybody
notices a wrong answer.

**What degrades at scale:** the refusal list itself. On one conversation it is two objects. On a
batch job over a year of tickets it is millions, held in memory because the signature says
`list[Refusal]`. That is the point at which the function becomes a generator yielding
`Memo | Refusal`, and the caller decides what to accumulate. The design does not change; only the
plumbing does.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"This returns the memos it kept and nothing about
what it dropped, so the first time someone asks why a fact is not in memory we will be reading code
to answer. Return the refusals too, with the rule that fired, and assert that kept plus refused
equals what we were given. And do not log the refused text — half of them are refused because of what
is in them."*

**The interview question:** *"How do you debug a system that decides what to store?"* The honest
spoken answer: *"By making sure the decision comes back carrying its reason, which means the function
splits rather than filters. A filter throws away the item and the reason at the same instant, so the
question 'why isn't this in memory?' has no cheap answer. Our policy returns two lists — kept and
refused — and each refusal carries the row that fired and the value that triggered it, so the answer
is a command rather than an afternoon. The check I would insist on is arithmetic: kept plus refused
must equal proposed. If that ever fails, something outside the policy is dropping things, and that is
the bug you cannot find by reading the policy."*

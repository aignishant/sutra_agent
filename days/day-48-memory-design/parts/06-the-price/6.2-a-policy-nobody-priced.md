# 6.2 — A policy nobody priced

## One-line answer

Put the three candidate retention policies in one table with a number beside each, and the argument
stops being about who is more confident.

## The story

You fill a trolley and then you look at the total.

Nothing in it was extravagant. Every item went in for a reason — that one was on offer, that one is
for the weekend, that one you were nearly out of. You made every decision carefully and separately,
and each one was defensible on its own.

The number at the till is a surprise anyway.

It is a surprise because the trolley never showed you a running total. You made forty small decisions
and never once saw what they added up to, so there was no moment at which you could have said *"that
is more than I meant to spend"* — because the information that would have prompted it did not exist
until it was too late to act on.

The shops that put a running total on the handle do not stop anybody buying anything. They just move
the number from the end to the middle, where it can still change a decision.

## The idea in plain language

Every earlier part of this day argued about one row at a time. Should a guess be stored. Should a
quote expire. Should a correction outrank a fact. Each of those is a good argument and each was made
in isolation.

A **policy** is what all those rows add up to, and until you total it, nobody has actually seen the
thing they are approving.

Totalling it changes the conversation in three ways.

**It makes disagreements specific.** *"We should keep more"* is unanswerable. *"Moving `guess` from
zero to ninety days adds 14 tokens per turn per customer"* is a proposal with a price, and the person
who wants it can decide whether it is worth that.

**It exposes which rows dominate.** Six rows do not contribute equally. In this policy the `decision`
row is the most expensive single kind by some distance, and that is worth knowing before anyone argues
about the cheap ones.

**It gives the "keep everything" default a number.** [1.1](../01-what-a-conversation-leaves/1.1-the-chat-nobody-can-leave.md)
argued that keeping everything is a policy nobody wrote down. This part gives it a column in the same
table as the others, so it competes on the same terms instead of being the invisible baseline.

The unit is the one from [6.1](6.1-charged-again-on-every-turn.md): tokens per turn, multiplied out to
tokens per conversation and tokens per customer per year. The last of those is the number a retention
argument is really about, because it is the one you multiply by your customer count.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because this is a design day, and design days fail by becoming opinion essays.

Everything in sections 1 to 5 is a judgement. Judgements are how you *arrive* at a policy and they are
not how you *defend* one. The defence is a table, and the table is what survives the meeting where
somebody who was not here asks why decisions are kept for three years.

Sutra needs it concretely too, because of the twenty-generations-a-day ceiling. Tokens are rationed
rather than billed ([Day 24](../../../day-24-token-accounting-and-budgets/parts/02-two-ceilings/2.3-denominated-in-quota-not-dollars.md)),
so memory that fills the window shortens the conversations the desk can have. A policy that spends 176
tokens per turn on one customer's memory is not expensive — it is *crowding*, and the thing it crowds
out is the ticket the desk is supposed to be reading.

And it is what makes the `TODO(me)` in the build brief answerable. The learner has to choose
`keep_for_days` for every row. Choosing without a price is guessing; this part is the tool that turns
it into a decision.

## The mechanism

Three policies over one conversation, priced with the same tokeniser.

```python
# days/day-48-memory-design/lab/defend.py
POLICIES: dict[str, tuple[Rule, ...]] = {
    "keep everything": tuple(
        Rule(r.kind, None, False, r.authority, r.redact, r.why) for r in RETENTION
    ),
    "this day's policy": RETENTION,
    "aggressive": tuple(
        Rule(
            r.kind,
            0 if r.keep_for_days != 1095 else 1095,
            r.supersede,
            r.authority,
            r.redact,
            r.why,
        )
        for r in RETENTION
    ),
}
```

**Line by line:**

- All three are built **from** `RETENTION` rather than typed out separately, so the comparison cannot
  drift from the policy the rest of the day teaches. Change a row and all three columns move.
- `"keep everything"` sets `keep_for_days=None` on every row and `supersede=False`. That is the
  unwritten default from [1.1](../01-what-a-conversation-leaves/1.1-the-chat-nobody-can-leave.md)
  expressed as an actual policy: nothing expires, nothing is superseded, nothing is refused.
- `"aggressive"` sets every row to zero except `decision`, which keeps its three years. It is the
  position somebody takes after reading a privacy checklist: store almost nothing, and keep only what
  an audit demands. It is not a strawman — it is a coherent and defensible policy, and it should be
  priced rather than dismissed.
- The rows are constructed **positionally** here, which is the one place in this day that happens.
  Inside a comprehension deriving six rows from six rows, keyword arguments would be six lines of
  noise; the trade is deliberate and it is why the ordering of `Rule`'s fields is worth keeping stable.

```python
def live_text(rules: tuple[Rule, ...]) -> tuple[int, str]:
    """Run one policy over the conversation and return the live memo count and their text."""
    import policy

    original = policy.RETENTION, policy.RULES
    policy.RETENTION = rules
    policy.RULES = {rule.kind: rule for rule in rules}
    try:
        kept, _ = what_to_keep(CANDIDATES, today=TODAY)
        live = [v.memo for v in what_to_forget(kept, today=TODAY) if v.action == "keep"]
    finally:
        policy.RETENTION, policy.RULES = original
    return len(live), "\n".join(memo.text for memo in live)
```

**Line by line:**

- The module's globals are swapped rather than the functions being parameterised, because
  `what_to_keep` and `what_to_forget` read `RULES` directly. This is a lab technique and it is not how
  the project module should work — production takes the rules as an argument, which is noted below.
- `import policy` inside the function gets the module object so its attributes can be rebound. Editing
  `RULES` imported by name would only rebind this file's copy, and the functions would carry on reading
  the module's.
- `try` / `finally` restores the originals even if a judgement raises. Without it one failure leaves
  every later comparison running against the wrong policy, and the numbers would be wrong rather than
  absent.
- Both `what_to_keep` **and** `what_to_forget` are run, so the count is *live* memos — after refusal,
  after expiry, after supersession. Pricing what was written rather than what survives would flatter
  every policy equally and mean nothing.
- The memos are joined with newlines, which is how a retrieval presents them, so the token count
  includes the separators an actual prompt would carry.


**Line by line:**

- `--offline` swaps the tokeniser for the character estimate, so the table can be produced with no API
  key at all. The shape of the comparison survives; the individual numbers move a little
  ([6.1](6.1-charged-again-on-every-turn.md)).

Measured on 2026-09-05, against `gemini-3.7-flash`:

```text
tokens counted by gemini-3.7-flash; one conversation, 12 turns

policy               memos  tok/turn  tok/convo  tok/yr/cust
keep everything          7       101       1212         4848
this day's policy        4        62        744         2976
aggressive               1        25        300         1200
(raw turns filed)       10       176       2112         8448

The last column is one customer, one year, if every memo is retrieved every turn.
Multiply by your customers. That is the number a retention argument is actually about.
```

Read the last column, and then read the bottom row.

**Filing the raw turns costs 8448 tokens per customer per year**, against 2976 for this day's policy.
That is 2.8 times, and it buys nothing — [5.1](../05-failure-lab/5.1-the-summary-that-lost-the-sentence.md)
showed the curated store answering more questions, not fewer.

**"Keep everything" is 4848 and it is not the worst row.** That is worth noticing, because keeping
every *memo* forever is much cheaper than filing every *turn*. The two mistakes are often talked about
as one thing and they differ by a factor of nearly two.

**"Aggressive" is 1200 and it holds one memo.** Which memo? The `decision` — the only kind it kept. So
the aggressive policy is the one from
[5.3](../05-failure-lab/5.3-the-rule-that-deleted-the-evidence.md) inverted: it keeps the audit trail
and throws away everything that would make the desk useful. It is cheap and it is a different product.

The policy this day arrived at sits between them, and now it sits between them **with a number**, which
is the entire point.

## When it breaks

The first break is treating the cheapest column as the best one.

Nothing in this table says what a policy is *worth*. The aggressive row costs a quarter of the
"keep everything" row and it also cannot answer three of the four questions from
[5.1](../05-failure-lab/5.1-the-summary-that-lost-the-sentence.md). A table of costs with no
corresponding table of what each policy can answer is exactly half an argument, and it is the half that
wins meetings, which is why it is dangerous.

The honest form is two columns side by side, and the day has both numbers:

```text
policy               tok/yr/cust   questions answered later
keep everything             4848   3 of 4
this day's policy           2976   3 of 4
aggressive                  1200   0 of 4
```

That second column is what stops the first one being used to argue for the aggressive policy, and it is
the pairing a reviewer should insist on.

The second break is scaling one conversation to a whole system by multiplication. This table measures
one ten-turn ticket for one customer. A customer with forty tickets over three years does not have
four times the memos, because supersession collapses repeated facts about the same subject — but they
do have far more `decision` memos, because every ticket produces one and decisions never supersede.
**The permanent kinds grow sub-linearly and the append-only kinds grow linearly**, and that is the
shape you actually have to model.

The third break is the measurement drifting from reality. This prices the memo text; the prompt carries
the rendered form with its wrapper ([6.1](6.1-charged-again-on-every-turn.md)). If the numbers in a
design document were produced by a script that measures something slightly different from what ships,
they will be quoted for years and be quietly wrong.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead:** the policy is a **parameter**, not a module global. Every
function takes the rules it is judging against, so comparing two policies is calling a function twice
rather than swapping module attributes. That is the honest criticism of `defend.py`'s technique, and it
is the one change worth making when this moves into `sutra/memory/policy.py` — the lab does it the
quick way because the quick way is legible in twelve lines.

Once the rules are a parameter, this table becomes a test rather than a script. A regression test that
asserts the current policy's tokens-per-turn stays under a stated ceiling turns a design decision into
something CI can defend, and the day somebody adds a row without thinking, the number moves and the
build says so.

**What degrades at scale:** the average stops describing anybody. This is one customer with one ticket.
Real stores have a long tail — a few customers with hundreds of memos, and they are the
long-tenured, frequently-escalating ones whose conversations matter most. So the number to put in the
design document is the ninety-fifth percentile rather than the mean, and the mean is the number that
gets used because it is easier to compute and looks better.

There is also a cost this table does not show at all: retrieval width. Every number here assumes every
memo is retrieved on every turn, which is the worst case and is what ADK's keyword matcher actually
does for a query with common words ([1.1](../01-what-a-conversation-leaves/1.1-the-chat-nobody-can-leave.md)).
Day 49's index returns a top `k` instead, and `k` becomes the dominant term — at which point the store
can grow without the per-turn cost growing, and the thing that degrades is relevance rather than price.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Good — now put the answered-questions column next to
the token column, because as it stands this table argues for the aggressive policy and the aggressive
policy cannot answer anything. And make the rules a parameter rather than swapping module globals in a
`finally` block. Once they are a parameter, this becomes a CI check with a ceiling, and the next person
who adds a row finds out from the build instead of from the quota."*

**The interview question:** *"How do you defend a retention policy to someone who thinks it keeps too
much?"* The honest spoken answer: *"With a table and two columns, because the argument is unwinnable
with one. On our support desk I priced three candidate policies against the same conversation with the
provider's token counter: filing the raw turns was 8448 tokens per customer per year, keeping every
memo forever was 4848, ours was 2976, and an aggressive privacy-first policy was 1200. That last number
is the one that catches people out, because it looks like the obvious winner — and it holds one memo,
the audit record, and cannot answer a single question the desk actually gets asked. So the cost column
on its own argues for the worst product, which is why I would never show it without the column beside
it saying what each policy can still answer. The other thing that surprised me was the gap between
filing every turn and keeping every memo forever. People talk about those as the same mistake and they
differ by nearly a factor of two — the expensive one is the transcript, not the permanence."*

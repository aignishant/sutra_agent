# 5.1 — 💥 The summary that lost the sentence you needed

## One-line answer

Compressing a conversation into a summary throws away exactly the detail a later question needed, and
the loss is invisible at the moment it happens because a good summary looks complete.

## The story

Somebody at home takes a phone call for you and tells you about it when you get in.

*"The plumber called. He'll come Tuesday."*

That is a good message. It has the who, the what and the when, and it is what you would write on a pad
yourself. You say thanks and get on with the evening.

On Tuesday nobody comes.

He said Tuesday **week**, because he had a job on. He also said he would need the stopcock uncovered
and to bring the old part down from the loft, and he said which of the two problems he was actually
coming for. All of that was in the call. None of it was wrong in the message you got — the message
was true, it was just the *shape* of a message rather than the content of the call.

And here is the part that makes it a systems problem rather than a domestic one. At the moment the
message was passed on, nobody could tell. It sounded complete. It answered the question that had been
asked. The gap only existed relative to questions that had not been asked yet, and those arrive later,
from a different direction, when the call is long over.

## The idea in plain language

There are two ways to make a conversation small enough to keep.

**Summarise it.** Write a short prose account of what happened. One paragraph replaces forty turns.
This is what compaction does ([Day 20](../../../day-20-context-engineering-compaction/LESSON.md)) and
it is the obvious thing to reach for.

**Extract from it.** Pull out the individual durable facts as separate memos, each addressed and dated,
and drop the rest. This is what sections 1 to 4 built.

Both make the conversation smaller. They are not variations on one technique, and the difference is
not how much they compress.

A **summary** is lossy in a way you cannot predict, because compression is a decision about what
matters made by somebody who does not know what will be asked. The summariser optimises for a
plausible reader with a general question. Real questions are specific and arrive from directions
nobody anticipated.

An **extraction** is lossy in a way you *declared*. The policy says which kinds survive and which do
not. When something is missing you can point at the row that dropped it. The loss is the same size or
larger — extraction throws away more of the conversation than a summary does — but it is **legible**,
and legible loss can be argued with and changed.

The trap is that a summary looks better at the moment you write it. It reads well. It covers the
narrative. An extraction looks like a pile of disconnected sentences with no story. Judged as prose,
the summary wins every time — and a store is not read as prose, it is queried.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because Day 20 gave Sutra compaction and taught the reader that summaries are lossy, and this is the
day that shows where that loss lands when the summary becomes a **memory** rather than a working
context.

The distinction matters. Compaction's loss is bounded by the conversation: what is dropped from the
context is still in the session store, and the conversation ends. A memory's loss is permanent and it
compounds, because the memory is what a *different* conversation, months later, will be told — and
by then nobody remembers there was a full version.

So the rule this day adds to Day 20's is precise: **compaction may summarise, memory must extract.**
They are different stores with different jobs and they are allowed different answers.

## The paper behind it

> **MemGPT: Towards LLMs as Operating Systems** · `arXiv:2310.08560` · 2023 ·
> `https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.08560`

It proposed a tiered memory in which the agent decides what to move out of its limited context and
into a larger store, and — the part that bites here — **can go back and page it in again** when a
later query needs it.

The paper is taught on Day 20, at
[`../../../day-20-context-engineering-compaction/papers/01-memgpt.md`](../../../day-20-context-engineering-compaction/papers/01-memgpt.md).
The half worth re-reading after this part is the recall path: the scheme only works because eviction
is reversible. A summary written over a conversation nobody kept is an eviction with no way back, and
that is the difference between the paper's design and the mistake this part is about.

## The mechanism

The summary is written by rule rather than by a model, and that is a deliberate choice rather than a
budget saving. A model writes a *better* sentence and loses the same things, because the loss is in
the compression rather than in the writer — and a fixed string makes the comparison reproducible.

```python
# days/day-48-memory-design/lab/lossy.py
SUMMARY = (
    "Ticket 4521: the customer's invoices were not arriving because the billing email "
    "had a typo. Resolved by re-sending the invoices and enabling bounce alerts."
)

LATER = [
    ("Which address should invoices go to?", "accounts"),
    ("What plan are they on and how is it billed?", "yearly"),
    ("Why were bounce alerts switched on for this account?", "cannot see bounces"),
    ("Was a price quoted for the Business plan?", "4800"),
]
```

**Line by line:**

- `SUMMARY` is a genuinely good summary. It names the ticket, the symptom, the root cause and the
  resolution. If you were grading summaries this would score well, and that is the point — the failure
  is not a bad summary.
- `LATER` holds the questions that actually arrive, each paired with a **needle**: the specific string
  that would have to be present for the store to answer at all. Pairing the question with its needle
  rather than asking a model to judge keeps the test deterministic and keeps the day at zero
  generations.
- The four questions are chosen to come from four different directions: a fact corrected mid-call, a
  fact never mentioned in the summary, a *reason* rather than an action, and a number said in passing.
  A summary loses each of those for a different reason.
- Question three is the one worth staring at. The summary says bounce alerts were enabled. It does not
  say **why**, and the why — *the customer cannot see bounces themselves* — is the part that tells a
  future agent whether to switch them off when the customer asks.

```python
def answerable(store: list[str], needle: str) -> bool:
    """Can anything in the store answer the question at all? Substring, case-insensitive."""
    return any(re.search(re.escape(needle), entry, re.I) for entry in store)
```

**Line by line:**

- `re.escape(needle)` treats the needle as literal text, so a needle containing a full stop or brackets
  does not silently become a pattern.
- `re.I` for case insensitivity, because the test is about whether the information survived rather than
  about capitalisation.
- `any(...)` over a generator, which stops at the first match rather than scanning the whole store.
- This is deliberately the **most generous possible** test. It does not ask whether the answer is well
  phrased or retrievable by the keyword matcher — only whether the words are present anywhere at all.
  A store that fails this test cannot answer the question by any means.

```bash
cd days/day-48-memory-design/lab
uv run python lossy.py
uv run python lossy.py --memos
cd -
```

**Line by line:**

- The bare arm is the summary store: one entry. `--memos` is the extraction store: five entries from
  the same conversation, produced by the policy.
- Both stores are built from the identical ten-turn ticket. Nothing else differs.

Measured on 2026-09-05:

```text
store: 1 summary  (156 chars, from 689 of transcript)
  - Ticket 4521: the customer's invoices were not arriving because the billing email had a typo. Resolved by re-sending the invoices and enabling bounce alerts.

questions that arrive later:
  [ NO] Which address should invoices go to?
  [ NO] What plan are they on and how is it billed?
  [ NO] Why were bounce alerts switched on for this account?
  [ NO] Was a price quoted for the Business plan?

answered: 0 of 4
```

```text
store: 5 memos  (290 chars, from 689 of transcript)
  - Billing email is [REDACTED-EMAIL]
  - Billing goes to [REDACTED-EMAIL]; the other address is personal.
  - Plan is Team, billed yearly.
  - Business plan is 4800 a year.
  - Re-sent three bounced invoices to the billing address and switched bounce alerts on, because the customer cannot see bounces themselves.

questions that arrive later:
  [YES] Which address should invoices go to?
  [ NO] What plan are they on and how is it billed?
  [YES] Why were bounce alerts switched on for this account?
  [YES] Was a price quoted for the Business plan?

answered: 3 of 4
```

**Zero out of four against three out of four**, for 156 characters against 290.

The summary is not a bad summary. It is 156 characters that correctly describe what happened, and it
cannot answer a single one of the four questions that turned up — including the one about the address,
which is the *subject of the ticket it summarises*. It says the billing email had a typo. It does not
say what the correct address is, because the correct address is a detail and a summary is not made of
details.

## When it breaks

The break is the run above, so this section is about the second failure in it, which is ours.

**The memos arm scores three out of four, not four.** Look at which question failed and why:

```text
  [ NO] Which address should invoices go to?
```

The memo that should answer it is right there:

```text
  - Billing goes to [REDACTED-EMAIL]; the other address is personal.
```

The needle was `accounts`, and the address `accounts@example.com` was redacted at write time by
[4.1](../04-privacy-and-erasure/4.1-redact-before-you-write.md). So the extraction store lost that
answer too — not to compression, but to a privacy rule.

That is worth being honest about rather than tidying away, and it is a real design finding: **redaction
is also lossy, and it is lossy in the same shape.** The difference is that this loss is declared. There
is a row in the table that says `redact=True`, with a reason, and somebody can look at it and decide
that a billing address is data the desk genuinely needs and should be stored under a different rule.
Nobody can have that conversation about a summary, because there is no row.

The second break is the seductive fix: *keep the summary as well as the memos*. It costs almost
nothing, it reads nicely, and now the store contains a prose account and five facts that overlap it. On
every retrieval that matches, the model gets both — and when they disagree, because the summary was
written before a correction or rounds a number, there is no rule saying which wins. Sections 3.2 and
1.4 built an ordering for competing memos. A summary is not a memo about a subject, so it competes
with all of them and is ranked against none.

The third break is measuring the wrong thing. A summariser is easy to evaluate on how good the summary
is, and that number will look fine here. The number that matters is the one in the output above:
**questions answered later**. Nobody measures it, because it requires the later questions, and the
later questions do not exist yet at the moment the summary is written.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead:** extraction for the memory store, and a summary only as a
**pointer** — a short prose entry whose job is to help somebody find the ticket, carrying the session
id, never treated as a source of facts. Kept in a different field from memos so nothing can retrieve it
as though it were one.

The extraction step is where a real system spends a model call, and it is a good place to spend one:
one call per closed conversation, producing candidates with kinds, which the policy then judges. That
is a completely different cost shape from summarising on every compaction, and the output is checkable
because each candidate points at the turn it came from.

**What degrades at scale:** the summary gets *shorter* as conversations get longer, which is exactly
backwards. A summariser given a fixed output length and a growing input compresses harder, so the
longest and most complicated tickets — the ones most likely to matter later — are the ones summarised
most aggressively. Extraction has the opposite property: a forty-turn ticket produces more memos than a
ten-turn one, because it contained more decisions.

There is a compounding failure worth naming: summarising summaries. A system that compacts, stores the
compaction, and later compacts *that* loses detail geometrically, and after three rounds the memory is
a paraphrase of a paraphrase with no way back to the turns.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"We are storing the compaction summary as the memory
entry. That is two different jobs with two different loss profiles sharing one field. A summary is
optimised for a reader with a general question and our retrievals are specific — the ticket about a
billing address does not contain the billing address. Extract facts for the memory store. If you want
to keep the summary, put it in its own field and make sure nothing retrieves it as a fact."*

**The interview question:** *"You need to fit a long conversation into memory. Summarise or extract?"*
The honest spoken answer: *"Extract, and the reason is not compression ratio — extraction actually
throws away more of the conversation. It is that the loss is declared. With a policy, when something is
missing you can point at the row that dropped it and argue about the row. With a summary, the loss was
decided by a compressor that did not know what would be asked, and it is invisible until somebody asks.
I measured it on our desk: a genuinely good 156-character summary of a ten-turn ticket answered zero of
four questions that arrived later, and 290 characters of extracted memos answered three. The summary
failed on the address, which was the subject of the ticket it summarised — it said the email had a typo
and never said what the right one was, because that is a detail and summaries are not made of details.
The fourth one my extraction missed too, and it is worth admitting why: redaction had removed the
address. Redaction is lossy in the same shape. The difference is there is a row in a table saying so."*

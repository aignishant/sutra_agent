# 5.3 — 💥 The rule that deleted the evidence

## One-line answer

Forgetting is a feature right up to the moment somebody disputes what you did, and a retention number
chosen for tidiness rather than for a reason will eventually delete the only record of a decision.

## The story

A shop keeps its till receipts in a box under the counter and clears the box out at the end of every
month.

This is sensible. The box fills up, the receipts are worthless after a few weeks, and nobody has ever
regretted throwing away a month-old receipt for two bags of screws. It has worked for years and
nobody has ever had to think about it.

In February somebody comes in with a heater bought in November. It has stopped working, they are
within warranty, and they want it repaired. The manufacturer will honour it — they just need the date
of sale and the serial number, which are on the receipt.

The customer lost their copy. That is what always happens; it is the entire reason the shop's copy
matters.

The shop's copy went into the box in November and out of it on the last day of the month, along with
four hundred receipts for screws and light bulbs. The rule did exactly what it was written to do. It
was applied consistently, on schedule, to every receipt equally — and that consistency is precisely
the problem, because a receipt for a heater under warranty and a receipt for two bags of screws are
not the same kind of thing and were never treated differently.

Nobody made a mistake in February. The mistake was made in whichever year somebody decided that one
rule, one interval, was fine for everything in the box.

## The idea in plain language

Sections 3 and 4 argued for forgetting: expiry, supersession, erasure. This part is the other edge of
that argument, and it is the reason retention numbers are not a matter of taste.

Every retention rule has **two** costs, and most policies only ever count one of them.

**The cost of keeping** is the one everybody sees. Storage, context, stale answers, privacy exposure.
It is visible, it is measurable, and it shows up on a bill.

**The cost of forgetting** is invisible until a specific event happens, and then it is total. It is
not a degraded answer; there is simply no answer, and no way to get one. Nobody can look harder.

The asymmetry matters. Keeping something you did not need costs a small amount continuously. Deleting
something you did need costs nothing at all, right up until it costs everything, and you cannot know
in advance which record that will be.

This is why `decision` is the odd row in the table. Every other kind is justified by what a *future
conversation* needs. `decision` is justified by what a **dispute** needs, and disputes are rare,
delayed and adversarial — which is exactly the combination that a retention period chosen for
convenience will get wrong.

And it produces a rule about how retention numbers are chosen. **A retention period must be sized
against the event that would need the record, not against how big the store is getting.** Storage is
never the real reason to forget a decision; storage is the reason somebody *says*. If the honest
reason is a privacy obligation or a legal limit, that is a good reason and it goes in the `why`. If
the honest reason is that the number looked tidy, the `why` column will expose it.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because Sutra's support desk records decisions about other people's money — invoices re-sent, alerts
switched on, refunds agreed — and those are exactly the things that get questioned later.

The `decision` row's retention is `1095` days. That is three years, and it is the one number in the
table that is not sized against how quickly something goes stale. A decision does not go stale at all:
the reason you did something in 2026 is still the reason in 2029. It is sized against **how long
somebody might come back and ask**.

That number is a judgement and it should be argued with. It is in a row precisely so that a support
lead can read it and say three years is too short, or too long, and the conversation happens against a
number rather than against a codebase.

This also closes a loop opened in
[1.3](../01-what-a-conversation-leaves/1.3-the-transcript-is-exhaust.md). Every memo cites the turn it
came from. That citation is only worth anything while the thing it cites still exists, so a decision's
retention and the session store's retention are related numbers — and if the session store keeps
ninety days while decisions keep three years, then for most of a decision's life its evidence is
already gone.

## The mechanism

The same decision memo, under two retention numbers, asked after a dispute arrives.

```python
# days/day-48-memory-design/lab/shredded.py
QUESTIONS = [
    "Did we agree to re-send the invoices, or did the customer send them themselves?",
    "Who turned bounce alerts on, and what reason was given at the time?",
]


def build(days: int) -> list[Memo]:
    """The same decision memo, under two different retention numbers."""
    return [
        Memo(
            kind="decision",
            holder="acct-88",
            subject="ticket-4521",
            text=(
                "Re-sent three bounced invoices and switched bounce alerts on, because the "
                "customer cannot see bounces themselves."
            ),
            source="ticket 4521 turn 7",
            recorded_on=RECORDED,
            expires_on=RECORDED + timedelta(days=days),
        )
    ]
```

**Line by line:**

- The two questions are both about **what was done and why**, which is what a `decision` memo is for.
  Neither is a question about a fact — a fact could be looked up again, and that is the difference
  between the kinds.
- The second question asks for a *reason*, and the reason is the half that is unrecoverable. You could
  reconstruct that alerts were switched on by looking at the account settings. You cannot reconstruct
  why, from anywhere, ever, once the memo is gone.
- `days` is a parameter rather than read from `RULES`, because this demo's whole subject is what
  happens when the number is different. The default arm passes `RULES["decision"].keep_for_days` so the
  correct arm still comes from the table.
- `expires_on` is always set here, never `None`. A `decision` does have a finite retention in this
  policy — the argument is about how long, not about whether.
- `text` carries the reason in the same sentence as the action, deliberately. Splitting them into two
  memos would let a retention rule delete the reason and keep the action, which is the worst of both.

**Line by line:**

- The bare arm uses the policy's real number, `1095`.
- `--tidy` uses `30`, the same number the `quote` row uses. That is the realistic mistake: somebody
  applies one comfortable interval to everything, exactly as the shop applied one interval to every
  receipt.
- The dispute is dated 2027-02-14, about five months after the decision. That is not a stretch; five
  months is a normal interval for a billing dispute to surface.

Measured on 2026-09-05:

```text
decision retention: 1095 days
recorded 2026-09-05, dispute opened 2027-02-14
  keep       current, unexpired, not erased

what the audit can answer:
  [YES] Did we agree to re-send the invoices, or did the customer send them themselves?
  [YES] Who turned bounce alerts on, and what reason was given at the time?

Three years is not a guess. It is the window disputes actually arrive in.
```

```text
decision retention: 30 days
recorded 2026-09-05, dispute opened 2027-02-14
  expired    expired on 2026-10-05

what the audit can answer:
  [ NO] Did we agree to re-send the invoices, or did the customer send them themselves?
  [ NO] Who turned bounce alerts on, and what reason was given at the time?

The policy worked exactly as written. That is the problem with the number in it.
Storage was never the reason to forget a decision. Nobody wrote down a reason.
```

The verdict line in the second block is the whole part: **`expired on 2026-10-05`**. Not corrupted, not
lost, not a bug. The policy read its row, did the arithmetic and applied the rule, four months before
anybody needed the record.

Everything worked. That is what makes it the most dangerous of the three failures in this section —
the other two are things going wrong, and this one is a thing going right, at a number nobody defended.

## When it breaks

There is no traceback. The error text arrives as a question with no answer behind it:

```text
The customer says they never authorised the bounce alerts and wants them removed
and the last two months refunded. What did we tell them at the time?
```

Somebody searches. The store returns nothing, because there is nothing — no row, no tombstone, no log
line. It is indistinguishable from a decision that was never recorded at all, which means the second
question in the room is whether anybody was writing these down in the first place.

The second break is the one that looks like a fix: **keeping everything forever to be safe.** That
trades one total failure for a slow one. Now the store holds every guess and every stale quote for a
decade, and the costs of section 6 apply to all of it. It also creates an obligation — a store you
keep is a store you must be able to search, redact and erase from, and doing that over ten years of
everything is a different job from doing it over three years of decisions.

The third break is more common than either and has no output at all: **the number that nobody chose.**
A `keep_for_days` value copied from the row above it, because it was there. It might be right. Nobody
can tell, because the `why` says something like *"standard retention"*, which is not a reason, it is a
description of the number being unremarkable. The check is to read the `why` column and ask what event
the number is sized against. If the sentence does not name one, the number is a guess in a table
pretending to be a policy.

The fourth is the mismatch mentioned above, and it is worth checking on a real system: a three-year
retention on decisions and a ninety-day retention on sessions means that after ninety days every memo
cites evidence that no longer exists. The decision survives and is unverifiable, which is a strange
middle state — you can say what you decided and cannot show the conversation it came from.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead:** different classes of record in different stores with different
rules, and an explicit `legal_hold` flag that suspends expiry entirely for a holder under dispute.
Once a complaint is open, nothing about that customer is deleted by any rule until the hold is lifted —
including by an erasure request, which is where the obligation from
[4.3](../04-privacy-and-erasure/4.3-erasure-is-an-obligation.md) meets a competing obligation and a
human has to decide. That tension is real and this day does not resolve it; what it insists on is that
the tension is visible in the table rather than discovered during a dispute.

The second thing a professional adds is a **deletion log**, which is the tombstone idea from 4.3 applied
to expiry. Not the content — the fact that a record of a given kind, for a given holder, was removed on
a given date by a given rule. That turns "we have no record" into "we had a record and this rule removed
it on this date", which is a far better sentence in a dispute and costs almost nothing to keep.

**What degrades at scale:** the pressure to shorten. As a store grows, retention periods come under
review, and the argument in the room is always about cost per gigabyte because that is the number
somebody can produce. The cost of forgetting has no number, so it loses. The defence is that the `why`
column names the event, so the conversation becomes "are disputes still arriving up to three years
later?" — which is a question the business can answer from its own data.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Thirty days on `decision` matches the `quote` row and
I do not think that is a decision, I think it is a copy. What is it sized against? Quotes expire because
prices move. Decisions do not go stale — they get disputed, and disputes arrive months later. Put the
event in the `why` and pick the number from it, and add a legal-hold flag before we need one rather than
during."*

**The interview question:** *"When has forgetting hurt you?"* The honest spoken answer: *"A retention
rule that deleted a decision record four months before a dispute arrived. Nothing failed — the policy
read its row, did the arithmetic, and applied the rule exactly as written, which is what makes it the
worst kind of incident to review. The number had been copied from the row above it. The real lesson was
that every retention rule has two costs and we were only counting one. Keeping something you did not
need costs a little, continuously, and it shows up on a bill. Deleting something you did need costs
nothing until it costs everything, and there is no partial answer — nobody can look harder. So the rule
I would defend now is that a retention period is sized against the event that would need the record,
not against how big the store is getting, and the reason goes in a column so the next person can argue
with it. Storage is never the honest reason to forget a decision. And I would keep a deletion log,
because 'we had a record and this rule removed it on this date' is a very different sentence in a
dispute from 'we have no record'."*

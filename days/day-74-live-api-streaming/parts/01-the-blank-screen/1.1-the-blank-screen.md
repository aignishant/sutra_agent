# 1.1 — The blank screen

## One-line answer

**Streaming** changes *when the reader is first served* and nothing else: the same words are produced
at the same rate and the turn ends at the same moment, but the share of the answer that must exist
before anything can appear on screen falls from **100% to 13%** — which is a claim about the reader's
attention, not about the machine's speed.

## The story

Four of you sit down to eat. One orders soup, one orders a salad, two order the fish.

The soup is ready almost at once. The salad follows it. But this kitchen has a rule: no plate leaves
until the whole table's order is done. So the soup goes under a heat lamp, the salad goes into the
cold well, and everyone sits in front of an empty placemat watching the door to the kitchen.

The fish finishes. Four plates arrive together. The soup has a skin on it, the salad has gone limp,
and the two people who ordered the fish are the only ones eating something that is any good.

Now change one rule and nothing else. Same kitchen, same cooks, same order, same stove. Each plate
goes out when it is ready. The soup arrives while it is hot. The salad arrives second. The fish
arrives last — at the *exact same moment* it would have arrived under the old rule, because nobody
cooked it any quicker.

The table is finished eating at the same time in both restaurants. Nothing was sped up. But one of
those two meals is unpleasant and the other is normal, and the entire difference is *when finished
work was allowed to leave the kitchen*.

## The idea in plain language

Three words first, because the rest of the day leans on all three.

A **token** is the unit a language model actually produces. It is not a word and not a letter — it is
a fragment of text from the model's fixed vocabulary, often a whole common word, often part of a
longer one, often a word plus its leading space. Day 2 introduced this, and it matters here for a
reason that will show up on screen in a moment.

An **event** is one object the ADK runtime hands your code while a turn is running. Your loop reads
events, not text. One turn can be one event or many, and today's whole subject is that difference.

**Streaming** is the kitchen rule. It means the model's output is delivered in pieces as those pieces
are produced, instead of being held until the turn is complete and delivered once. That is the entire
definition. Notice what is *not* in it: nothing about speed, nothing about the model, nothing about
how long the answer takes.

Here is the sentence people say and get wrong: *"streaming makes it faster."* It does not. The model
generates the same tokens in the same order at the same rate whether or not anybody asked for a
stream. The last token of the answer lands at the same instant either way. Adding a stream does not
add a cook to the kitchen.

What changes is **how much of the answer must exist before any of it can be shown**. Without
streaming, that is the whole answer — there is nothing to show until the turn is over, because
nothing has left the kitchen. With streaming, it is the first piece. And a reader who is being served
the first piece is in a completely different situation from a reader looking at a blank screen, even
when the two of them will finish reading at the same moment.

That is why the honest way to describe streaming is a sentence about the reader. The engineering
claim is small and checkable; the "faster" claim is large and false.

## Why Sutra needs it

The desk answers support questions, and Day 73 built the half of Phase 11 that nobody watches — a
nightly job whose only audience is a file read the next morning. Today is the opposite end of the
same phase: somebody is sitting in front of the desk, waiting, and the design question is what is on
their screen before the answer exists.

It matters for Sutra specifically because the desk's answers are not one line. They quote a document,
they explain a cause, they sometimes call a tool first. A reader staring at nothing while all of that
happens has no way to tell a working system from a broken one, and the two look identical: a blank
area of screen. The moment the first fragment appears, the ambiguity is gone.

It is also the cheapest architectural decision on this day and the one with the longest tail. The
switch is a single field (part [1.2](1.2-one-field-on-runconfig.md)), but turning it on changes how
many events your loop receives, what each of them contains, what your code must do with the last one,
and what happens when the reader closes the tab. None of that is a display concern.

## The mechanism

The lab replaces only the model. The runner, the agent, the event loop and `RunConfig` are the real
ADK ones; `_fake.py` is a `BaseLlm` subclass that answers from a script, so the day costs no quota.
Its script is the thing to look at first:

```python
# The answer the desk gives, split the way a model emits it: not on word boundaries, because a token
# is not a word - Day 2 part 1.1's subject, and the reason a half-written word appears on screen.
CHUNKS = [
    "The invoice ",
    "PDF is blank ",
    "because the billing page ",
    "renders it before the fonts ",
    "have loaded.",
]
ANSWER = "".join(CHUNKS)
```

**Line by line:**

- `CHUNKS` is the answer already cut into the pieces the model would emit. The script is a list rather
  than a sentence because the point of the file is *how* the answer arrives, not what it says.
- The splits are deliberately not at word boundaries. `"The invoice "` ends after a trailing space,
  but `"PDF is blank "` and `"because the billing page "` each carry several words, and a real model's
  boundaries fall wherever its vocabulary happens to fall — mid-word as often as not. This is the
  token point from Day 2 made visible: a piece is a piece of *text*, not a piece of *language*.
- That is why a streaming interface sometimes shows you half a word for an instant. It is not a bug in
  the renderer. The renderer was handed half a word, because a token is not a word.
- `ANSWER = "".join(CHUNKS)` defines the complete answer as exactly the concatenation of the pieces —
  no separator, no reassembly rule. The chunks already contain their own spacing, so joining is the
  whole of the reassembly. That single line is what makes the two runs below comparable: both deliver
  precisely these 90 characters.

The measurement itself is one line in `modes.py`:

```python
    # How much of the answer had to arrive before anything could go on screen. In SSE that is the
    # first partial; with no streaming it is the whole turn, because there is nothing before it.
    before_render = len(seen[0][2]) if seen else 0
```

**Line by line:**

- `seen` is the list of events that carried text, in arrival order, built by the runner in `_fake.py`.
  Each row is `(index, partial, text)`.
- `seen[0]` is therefore the **first** event with text in it — the first thing that could possibly be
  put on a screen. `[2]` is that event's text.
- `len(...)` of that text is the whole metric: the number of characters that had to exist before the
  reader stops looking at nothing. It is a count of characters and not a count of seconds, and that is
  a deliberate limitation the next section is explicit about.
- `if seen else 0` guards a turn that produced no text at all — a tool-only turn, or a cancelled one.
  Without it a turn with an empty event list crashes the report instead of reporting zero, and
  Principle 10 wants the run to say what happened rather than to fall over describing it.

Now run the same turn twice, changing one field and nothing else:

```bash
cd days/day-74-live-api-streaming/lab
uv run python modes.py
```

**Line by line:**

- `cd .../lab` matters because `modes.py` does `import _fake` as a sibling module. Run it from the
  repository root and Python will not find `_fake`.
- `uv run` executes inside the project's pinned environment, so the `google-adk` version being
  measured is the one in `uv.lock` rather than whatever is on the system path.
- No flag means the default branch of `modes.py`, which is `StreamingMode.SSE` — streaming on.

Measured on 2026-09-06 (this is the summary block; the run also prints each event, shown in part
[1.3](1.3-one-event-or-six.md)):

```text
streaming_mode: SSE
the model was asked to stream: [True]

  events carrying text        6
    of which partial          5
    of which final            1
  characters in the answer    90
  chars before first render   12  (13% of the answer)
```

Then the ablation — the same script with streaming turned off:

```bash
uv run python modes.py --none
```

**Line by line:**

- `--none` is the switch that turns the idea off. `modes.py` reads it and selects `StreamingMode.NONE`
  instead of `StreamingMode.SSE`. Nothing else in the process changes: same agent, same instruction,
  same question, same scripted answer.
- An ablation is the only way this measurement means anything. A single run showing "12 characters
  before the first render" proves nothing on its own; the pair proves that the 12 came from the field
  and not from the model.

Measured on 2026-09-06, head of the run again:

```text
streaming_mode: NONE
the model was asked to stream: [False]

  events carrying text        1
    of which partial          0
    of which final            1
  characters in the answer    90
  chars before first render   90  (100% of the answer)
```

Line the two up:

| | `StreamingMode.SSE` | `StreamingMode.NONE` |
| --- | --- | --- |
| events carrying text | 6 (5 partial, 1 final) | 1 (0 partial, 1 final) |
| characters in the answer | 90 | 90 |
| characters before the first render | 12 — **13%** of the answer | 90 — **100%** of the answer |

Read the middle row first, because it is the one that carries the argument. **The answer is the same
90 characters in both runs.** Not similar, not equivalent — the same string, from the same script.
Nothing was produced sooner, nothing was skipped, and no work was moved.

The row that changes is the last one. With streaming off, the reader must wait for 100% of the answer
before the screen can change, because there is only one event and it arrives when the turn is over.
With streaming on, 13% is enough — `"The invoice "`, twelve characters, and the placemat is no longer
empty.

That is the kitchen rule, measured. The fish still finishes when the fish finishes. The soup stopped
sitting under the lamp.

Why 6 events rather than 5, and why the last one is not the last chunk, is a genuine trap and it has
its own parts: the counting is part [1.3](1.3-one-event-or-six.md), and what that final event
actually contains is part [2.1](../02-the-final-chunk/2.1-the-answer-twice.md). Do not write code
against these numbers until you have read both.

## When it breaks

**The first thing that breaks is this lab's own claim, and the honest thing is to say so before
anybody catches it.** `modes.py` **cannot measure latency and does not pretend to.** Its own docstring
is explicit:

> The lab cannot measure latency, and does not pretend to: this model answers instantly, so both runs
> finish at once. What it measures is the thing latency is spent on - how much of the turn a reader
> has to wait for before the screen can change.

The scripted model in `_fake.py` yields its five chunks in a loop with nothing in between, so the
streaming run and the non-streaming run finish at the same moment on a wall clock. If you point a
stopwatch at these two commands you will learn nothing at all. What the pair measures is the
*denominator* — how much of the turn must be complete before the screen can change — which is the
thing that real latency is spent on. A count of characters is a weaker claim than a count of seconds,
and it is the claim the lab can actually support (Principle 10).

The claim about time belongs to the paper at the end of the day, whose demo simulates a model emitting
tokens at a stated rate. That demo reports the **total completion time identical at 25.20s with
streaming on and off** — the total does not move — while the number of turns falling outside the
paper's goal band for a first response goes from **3 of 4 to 0 of 4**. Same total, different
experience, which is the same finding as the table above with seconds on it instead of characters.
Read [the paper](../../papers/01-response-time.md) last, as the hub says.

**The second thing that breaks is the word people reach for.** Somebody will describe this feature as
making the assistant "faster", and once that word is in the room the wrong decisions follow it. A team
that believes streaming buys speed will reach for it to fix a slow model, will be baffled when the
completion time does not move, and will not budget for the things streaming actually costs: a
connection held open for the length of the turn, a renderer that can accept partial text, a rule for
the final event, and a decision about what happens when the reader leaves mid-answer. All four are
later parts of this day. None of them is a price worth paying for a benefit you have described wrongly
to yourself.

**The third is measuring the wrong end.** It is tempting to check that streaming works by watching the
words appear, and words appearing is not evidence of anything except that a loop ran. The number to
look at is `chars before first render` against `characters in the answer`, with the ablation next to
it. That is why `modes.py` prints both and why `--none` exists.

## In production

**What changes at scale.** In this lab the two modes differ by 78 characters. In a real system the gap
is the size of a whole answer — an assistant reply that runs to several paragraphs, or one that calls
a tool, reads a document and *then* starts writing. The share of the answer that must exist before the
screen changes is still 100% without streaming, and 100% of a long answer is a reader who has decided
the system is broken and pressed the button again. Duplicate requests from impatient readers are a
real and measurable load, and they arrive precisely on the slowest turns, which are the ones already
under pressure.

**What a professional adds around it.** Three things, none of which is the streaming flag itself.
First, the mode is a **parameter**, not a constant: the same agent is called by a person at a keyboard
and by Day 73's unattended nightly job, and the nightly job has no screen to serve — the hub's build
brief makes this a `TODO(me)` for exactly that reason. Second, a rule for the final event that lives
in **one** place in the codebase, because the alternative is the same subtle bug written slightly
differently in every caller (part [2.1](../02-the-final-chunk/2.1-the-answer-twice.md)). Third, a
first-token measurement in the telemetry that is separate from the total, because they are different
numbers that move for different reasons, and a dashboard that only records the total cannot see the
thing streaming was bought to change.

**What does not change.** The token count, the cost, the model, and the time the last character lands.
If a proposal claims streaming improved any of those, something else changed too, and finding out what
is the actual work.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Take 'faster' out of the description — it makes a
claim we can't support and it will end up in a status update. What this changes is time-to-first-token,
and the total is unchanged; say that instead, because it's true and it's checkable. Two follow-ups.
The mode has to be an argument, not a constant — the nightly job calls this same function and it has
no screen, so streaming there is pure overhead. And show me the number: first-token and total as
separate metrics. If we can't see them apart, we can't tell whether this did anything."*

**The interview question:** *"Does streaming make your assistant faster?"* An honest answer: *"No, and
the fact that it doesn't is the interesting part. The model produces the same tokens at the same rate
and the last one arrives at the same moment, so total completion time is unchanged — I ran the same
turn with the mode on and off and the answer was the same ninety characters both times. What changes
is how much of the answer has to exist before the reader can be shown anything: without streaming
that's the whole answer, a hundred per cent, because there's a single event at the end of the turn;
with it, it's the first chunk — twelve characters out of ninety, about thirteen per cent. So it's a
claim about time-to-first-token and about attention, not about throughput. It's worth being precise
because streaming isn't free: you hold a connection open for the turn, your renderer has to cope with
partial text, and you need a rule for the final event, which in ADK carries the whole turn's content
again — concatenate everything you're handed and you print the answer twice. And you have to decide
what happens when the reader closes the tab half-way through, because the default probably isn't what
you'd choose."*

## Check yourself

```bash
cd days/day-74-live-api-streaming/lab
uv run python modes.py
uv run python modes.py --none
```

**Line by line:**

- Run both, in this order, and read only two lines of each: `characters in the answer` and
  `chars before first render`.
- The first number must be identical across the two runs. If it is not, something other than the mode
  changed, and the comparison is void.
- The second number is the whole finding. Predict both values before you press enter; the prediction
  is the exercise, not the output.

Two things to do with that:

1. Take any interface you use that shows an answer appearing piece by piece, and explain to somebody
   what it is doing **without using the word "faster"**. If the explanation gets hard, that is the
   point — you are being forced to talk about when the reader is served instead of about speed, and
   that is the only accurate version.
2. Open `_fake.py` and find the chunk boundary that falls in an awkward place. Say out loud why a real
   model's boundaries would be worse, not better, and what that means for a renderer that trims
   trailing whitespace on every update.

**Out loud, without scrolling up:** both runs deliver 90 characters and both finish at the same
moment. So what, precisely, did turning streaming on buy — and which number in the output is the
evidence?

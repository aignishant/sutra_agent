# 4.2 — The free-quota check

## One-line answer

A free-quota check has **three** possible outcomes rather than two — *free*, *not free*, and *could
not determine* — and this one returned all three at once: text streaming is **free**, because the
lab measured that a streamed turn costs the same **one** model request as an unstreamed one;
bidirectional voice is 🅿️ **parked**, because it needs something this curriculum will not pay for;
and whether the Live API is on a free tier at all is **recorded as unverified**, because the pages
that would answer it do not put the answer in anything a script can read — and writing that third
line down honestly is worth more than a confident guess at the first.

## The story

You decide to join a gym. There is one at the end of the road you walk past constantly, so you go and
have a proper look at the front of it, and there is no price anywhere. Big photographs of equipment,
opening times, a laminated sheet about the class timetable, and nothing at all about money. You get
your phone out and find the website. Membership, plans, *find out more* — and every path ends at the
same box: **call for details**.

So you are standing on the pavement holding a decision you cannot make. You could sign up anyway and
find out what it costs when the first payment leaves your account. You could give up on the whole
idea. Or you could do the third thing, which does not feel like doing anything: get out the notes app
and type *"gym at the end of the road — price not published anywhere, website says call. Ask at the
desk."*

The third thing turns out to be the useful one, and it takes a while to see why. It is not that the
note answers the question. It is that the note stops you from *inventing* an answer. A week later,
when a friend asks whether that gym is any good, you do not say "I think it's about the usual" — you
say "I don't know, they don't publish it." And when you finally have a spare afternoon in that part
of town, the note tells you exactly what to do: walk in and ask at the desk. The unknown has a name,
a reason and a next step, which is a completely different object from an unknown you have quietly
papered over.

Here is the part that matters most, and it is the part people skip. While you were standing there,
you also worked out something you *could* answer without any price at all: the gym is a bus ride away
at the wrong end of the day, and you were never realistically going to go. That decision did not need
the number. Knowing which of your decisions depend on the missing fact — and which do not — is what
lets you get on with your afternoon instead of standing on the pavement.

This part is that pavement. One thing was measured, one thing was decided without the missing number,
and one thing was written down as *not known* with the exact question to ask at the desk.

## The idea in plain language

Three terms first, because the rest of this page leans on all of them.

A **free tier** is the amount of a paid service a provider lets you use for nothing, with no billing
account attached — usually capped, usually per model, and usually revocable. Addendum 02
(`docs/02_ADDENDUM_ZERO_BUDGET_MODELS.md`) is the rule that this curriculum uses only what is inside
somebody's free tier, and it wins over the master plan on anything to do with paying.

**RPD** is *requests per day*: how many separate calls a provider will accept from you in a
twenty-four-hour window before it starts refusing. Its sibling is **RPM**, requests per minute.
Addendum 02 makes these the currency the day's budget is denominated in — the §6 table in
[`../../LESSON.md`](../../LESSON.md) counts requests, not money, and Day 70 — the quota router — is
the day that turned that counting into code that picks a provider by what is left.

**Rendered by JavaScript** describes a web page whose visible content is not in the file the server
sends. The server sends a mostly empty skeleton plus a program; your browser runs the program, the
program fetches the real content and writes it into the page, and only then do you see a table. This
matters here for one blunt reason: a command-line tool that fetches a page gets the skeleton and does
not run the program. What you see in a browser and what a script receives can be entirely different
documents at the same address.

Now the idea. When you check whether you can afford a feature, the instinct is to expect a yes or a
no. In practice a check has a third outcome, and it is the most common one: **you could not find
out**. That is not a failure of the check. It is a *result*, and it has to be written down as one,
because the alternative is that the gap gets filled in by whatever sounded plausible on the day — and
a plausible wrong answer about cost is indistinguishable from a real one until the bill arrives.

The second half of the idea is the pavement observation. Once you have separated the outcomes, you
can look at each pending decision and ask whether it actually depends on the unknown. Sometimes it
does and you are stuck until you go and ask. Sometimes — as here — the decision comes out the same
way whichever answer the unknown turns out to have, and then you can make it now, say why it is safe
to make it now, and leave the unknown genuinely open instead of pretending you resolved it.

## Why Sutra needs it

ADK-53 is the free-quota check, and this is the page where it gets answered — including the half of
it that could not be.

Part [4.1](4.1-what-a-live-session-is-made-of.md) did the honest first half: it read off the
installed package what a bidirectional session *requires*, without deciding anything about money.
That order matters, because a budget decision about a feature you cannot describe is not a decision,
it is a mood. This page does the second half, and it owes the curriculum three things rather than
one: what is affordable, what is not, and what remains unknown with the exact way to find out.

There is a deeper reason, and it outlives this day. This repository's whole method is dated rows in
ledgers — `docs/PACKAGES.md`, `docs/PAPERS.md`, the §8 *Verify before you code* table in every day's
hub. Principle 7 says never invent a version number; §17.4.1 says never invent a citation. Both are
the same instruction: **an unverified fact must look unverified in the record.** A free-tier claim is
exactly this kind of fact, and it is more dangerous than most, because nothing fails when you get it
wrong. A wrong version pin dies on the next `uv sync`. A wrong sentence about somebody's free tier
sits in a document being quietly believed until somebody builds on it.

## The mechanism

The check has three findings. Take the one that was actually measured first, because it is the good
news and it is the one holding up today's build.

### Finding one: streaming is free, and the lab proves it

The worry that makes people hesitate about turning streaming on is a reasonable one. Six events came
back instead of one — part [1.3](../01-the-blank-screen/1.3-one-event-or-six.md) measured that and
pastes the full run — so it is natural to wonder whether six events means six requests, which under
an RPD cap would mean streaming costs six times as much as not streaming. If that were true, every
budget in this curriculum would need rewriting.

`lab/modes.py` answers it directly, because `_fake.py` records the `stream` flag it was handed on
**every** call, and `modes.py` prints the whole list:

```python
    print(f"the model was asked to stream: {_fake.StreamingLlm.streamed}")
```

**Line by line:**

- `_fake.StreamingLlm.streamed` is a **class attribute**, not an instance one — a list that lives on
  the class itself, so every model object made from it appends to the same list. That is deliberate:
  if the runtime had constructed a second model, or called the same one twice, the entries would pile
  up in one place where they cannot be missed.
- Each entry is the value of the `stream` argument the runtime passed for one call. So the list is
  not a summary anyone wrote — it is a **call log**, and its *length* is the number of times the
  model was asked anything.
- Printing the list rather than a count is the point. A count of `1` would be a claim; `[True]` is
  the log itself, and you can see there is nothing else in it.
- `_fake.reset()` empties it at the start of each run, so a run's list only ever describes that run.

Run both modes:

```bash
cd days/day-74-live-api-streaming/lab
uv run python modes.py
uv run python modes.py --none
```

**Line by line:**

- `cd` into the lab first — these are plain scripts rather than installed modules.
- `uv run` executes them in this project's own environment, against the pinned `google-adk`, so the
  runtime doing the calling is the one the repository actually uses.
- The first run is `StreamingMode.SSE`; `--none` is the **ablation**, the same turn with streaming
  switched off. Two runs, one difference, so anything that differs between them is caused by that
  difference and nothing else.
- Neither command contacts a provider. `_fake.py` is a real `BaseLlm` subclass answering from a
  script, so this measures the real runner and spends nothing.

One line out of each run is the whole finding:

```text
the model was asked to stream: [True]
```

```text
the model was asked to stream: [False]
```

**One entry in each list. One call in each run.** The streaming run produced six events out of that
single request; the non-streaming run produced one event out of its single request. Same one request
either way.

That is the budget fact of this part, and its consequence is a relief: **Addendum 02's
request-per-day accounting is unaffected by turning streaming on.** Whatever RPD figure you are
working against, it counts the same before and after. You are not buying more of anything — you are
receiving the same answer in pieces instead of in a lump, and the pieces come down the same one
request. Part [1.1](../01-the-blank-screen/1.1-the-blank-screen.md) made the same point about time:
streaming changes *when the reader is served*, never how much work happens. This is that argument
restated in the only unit Addendum 02 recognises.

### Finding two: what a live session needs, and what it costs to find out

Part [4.1](4.1-what-a-live-session-is-made-of.md) established the requirement off the installed
`google-adk==2.7.1`, and its output is pasted there in full, so it is only cited here: three
streaming modes exist; `Runner.run_live` requires a `live_request_queue` and nothing else; and
**eleven** `RunConfig` fields exist only for live sessions, every one of them about audio,
transcription, session lifetime, or how to behave when both sides can speak.

`livecheck.py` ends by stating what the day does with that, and this is the verdict in the lab's own
words:

```python
    print("  What this day does with that:")
    print("    BIDI needs a model that holds an audio session open. Addendum 02 allows nothing")
    print("    that requires a billing account, so bidi voice is PARKED here; Day 75 revisits it.")
    print("    SSE carries text over one ordinary HTTP response and needs nothing extra, so it")
    print("    is what this day builds. Speech is the browser's job: it already has speech")
    print("    synthesis and recognition built in, they cost nothing, and they run on the")
    print("    listener's own machine.")
```

**Line by line:**

- The verdict is **printed by the script**, not only written in this document. That is on purpose: a
  decision that lives only in prose gets read once, while a decision the tooling states every time it
  runs gets re-read by whoever next runs it — including on the day the answer changes.
- `PARKED` is written in plain capitals rather than with the 🅿️ marker, because this is terminal
  output and an emoji in a console is a portability problem rather than a signal. The marker belongs
  in the documents.
- The second block is the affordable half, and it names *why* it is affordable in mechanism terms —
  "one ordinary HTTP response" — rather than asserting that it is free. Part
  [3.1](../03-the-wire/3.1-what-sse-actually-is.md) is where that claim about the wire was
  established.
- The last three lines are the fallback plan, and they are a **plan**, not a verified fact. See below.

🅿️ **Bidirectional voice is parked.** Not deferred pending a price check — parked, now, with the
reason written down.

### Why row two is safe even though row three is open

This is the pavement observation, and it is the genuinely useful thing to take from this page.

Suppose the unknown resolved the friendly way tomorrow — suppose the Live API turned out to be
squarely inside somebody's free tier. **Row two would not change.** A bidirectional voice session is
a connection held open per user for as long as that user is in it, which part
[4.1](4.1-what-a-live-session-is-made-of.md) showed is an *occupancy* cost rather than a
per-request one, and it brings a set of audio concerns — capture, encoding, interruption while the
model is speaking, a session that survives a dropped connection — that this curriculum has no way to
test at zero cost. The word "free" on somebody's pricing page does not supply a microphone, a second
machine to be interrupted from, or a way to exercise any of it without a human sitting there talking.

So the parking decision is **robust to the unknown**: it comes out the same whichever way row three
lands. That is precisely why row three was safe to leave open rather than guessed at. When you find
yourself missing a fact, the first thing to test is not how to get it — it is which of your pending
decisions actually turn on it. Frequently the answer is none of them, and then the honest move is to
decide the decidable things, say out loud that you decided them without the missing fact, and leave
the gap visible for whoever needs it later. That is a much stronger position than a guess, and it is
available far more often than people use it.

### The fallback, which is a plan and not yet a fact

If voice is parked, speech does not have to be. The day's plan is to move it to the listener's own
machine: **browsers have speech synthesis and speech recognition built in**, so they cost nothing,
need no account, need no key, and run locally rather than as a request against anybody's quota. That
is the intention `livecheck.py` prints, and it is what Day 75 — Live API II, the voice loop — is
expected to build against.

Be clear about the status of that paragraph. **Nothing on this page has verified it against a
specification or against a browser.** No document was read, no capability was exercised. It is the
day's plan, recorded as a plan, and it gets exactly the same treatment as row three of the table
rather than a softer one just because it is convenient.

## When it breaks

**A page that renders its tables in JavaScript is a page a script cannot read — and that is now the
normal case.** This is not an unlucky page. Provider documentation is overwhelmingly built this way,
and it is getting more so, so the old habit of *fetch it and grep for the number* has quietly stopped
being a reliable way to verify anything about quota. Be honest about what that costs, because it is
not nothing: verification becomes a **manual step with a date on it**. Somebody has to open a
browser, read a table with their own eyes, and write down what it said and when.

That cost is exactly why this repository keeps dated rows rather than live lookups. If a fetch could
reliably answer these questions, a script could re-check them and a stale row would be a nuisance
rather than a design. It cannot, so the record has to carry its own date and its own provenance and
be re-read by a person. Every §8 *Verify before you code* table in this curriculum is that trade
being paid, on purpose.

**A free roster moves, so everything here is true of one date.** In December 2025 a provider's free
model list changed under this curriculum's feet, which is the precedent Addendum 02 was written
around. Nothing above is a standing fact about the world. It is a measurement of one pinned package
and two URLs on 2026-09-06. The correct response to reading this page at any later date is not to
trust it — it is to **re-run the check** and write down your own result. That is Principle 7, and it
applies to the row that says *verified free* every bit as much as to the row that says *unknown*.

**The failure this whole part exists to prevent.** It would have been easy — and it would have read
better — to end finding three with a clean sentence: *"the Live API is available on the free tier."*
Or the opposite: *"the Live API is not on the free tier."* Either one would have looked like the
result of a check. Neither would have been one. And a wrong quota claim does not fail on the next
`uv sync` or the next test run; it sits in a document being believed for months, gets quoted into a
plan, and surfaces as a surprise at the worst possible moment. This is the identical argument §17.4.1
makes about citations: a wrong version pin fails loudly, a plausible-looking identifier attached to
the wrong title fails silently for years. A plausible-looking quota figure is in the second category.

**And there is a subtler version of the same failure: hedging into an implied answer.** "Bidi voice
is parked because it is unlikely to be free" would be a sentence that never technically claims
anything and leaves every reader with a claim in their head. The parking is because of occupancy and
untestable audio, which the page can support. The free-tier question is open, which the page says.
Keeping those two apart in the writing is the work.

## In production

**A team keeps this kind of fact in a table in the repository, not in a call at runtime.** The
shape that works is one row per claim with three columns: **what is claimed**, **the URL it was read
from**, and **the date somebody read it**. It lives in version control next to the code that depends
on it, so the claim moves through review like any other change and `git log` says when the team's
belief changed.

The instinct to instead fetch the limits at startup and adapt is wrong for three separate reasons,
and it is worth being able to say all three. The pages do not serve the numbers to a script, which is
this part's whole finding. A runtime that asks a provider what it is allowed to do has made the
provider's availability a dependency of its own start-up. And most importantly, quota is a
**planning** input rather than a runtime one — you want it visible while somebody is deciding what to
build, which is months before any process starts.

This is a pattern the curriculum has already used twice. Day 73's part 5.1 does the pricing exercise
for ambient agents — working out what a scheduled job costs before letting it loose on a schedule —
and Day 69's provider-terms table records what each provider says it does with your data, as dated
rows rather than as a lookup. This page is the third instance and the first one where the honest
result was a blank with a reason in it. A table that has never once recorded "not established" is a
table somebody has been guessing into.

**Every unknown gets an owner and a next action, or it is not recorded, it is buried.** "We don't
know" on its own decays into "nobody knows and nobody is finding out". The row that works names the
check — *open these two URLs in a browser and write down the limits and the date* — so that resolving
it is a small, obviously-doable thing rather than a research project somebody has to scope.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Thank you for the third row. I've reviewed a lot
of these and it's almost always two rows, which means the hard case got rounded to whichever
answer suited the plan. Two things. First, I want the 'we couldn't determine this' row to have a name
against it and a date to check by, otherwise it'll still be blank next quarter — the check is one
person opening two pages, so it should not survive a week. Second, the paragraph I actually want kept
is the one explaining why parking bidi doesn't depend on the unknown. That's the reasoning I'd want
repeated in every design doc we write: say which decisions turn on the missing fact and which don't,
because if none of them do we can stop waiting. And keep the streaming measurement in the repository
as a runnable script rather than a number in prose — 'one request either way' is the sentence
everything downstream is standing on, and I want it to be re-runnable when we bump the pin."*

**The interview question:** *"How do you decide whether you can afford a feature?"* An answer that
shows the work: *"I try to separate three things, because the usual mistake is collapsing them into
two. What it costs, what I can verify it costs, and what I can't. On the streaming work I did
recently, the affordability question was whether streaming multiplied my request count, since we
budget in requests per day rather than money. So I measured it: I ran the same turn with streaming on
and off against a scripted model, and logged the stream flag on every call the runtime made. One
entry in the log either way. Six events came out of one request, so the request accounting didn't
change at all and I could just build it. The voice feature went the other way. I read the runner's
signature off the installed package first — before thinking about cost, because a budget decision
about something you can't describe isn't a decision — and it needs a connection held open per user
plus a stack of audio config that has no equivalent in a normal turn. So I parked it. Then the honest
bit: I could not establish whether that API is on a free tier at all. I fetched the provider's docs
and got a 200 and the right page, but the limit tables are rendered by JavaScript and simply aren't
in the HTML a script receives, so the fetch answered nothing. I wrote that down as unverified with
the exact check for a human to run, rather than filling it in with something plausible — a wrong cost
claim doesn't fail loudly, it just gets believed. And I checked whether the parking decision actually
depended on that unknown. It didn't: held-open connections and untestable audio park it either way.
So I could decide the thing that was decidable and leave the gap genuinely open, which I think is a
better position than a guess that reads as confident."*

## Check yourself

```bash
cd days/day-74-live-api-streaming/lab
uv run python modes.py | grep "asked to stream"
uv run python modes.py --none | grep "asked to stream"
uv run python livecheck.py | tail -n 8
```

**Line by line:**

- `cd` into the lab first, then `uv run` so the pinned environment is the one being exercised.
- `grep "asked to stream"` pulls out the single line that carries the budget finding and drops the
  rest, which parts [1.3](../01-the-blank-screen/1.3-one-event-or-six.md) and
  [4.1](4.1-what-a-live-session-is-made-of.md) already paste in full. Run the two commands one after
  the other and read the two lines as a pair: `[True]` and `[False]`, one entry each.
- `tail -n 8` shows the last eight lines of the live check, which is the printed verdict — parked,
  and why. Reading the decision out of the tool rather than out of a document is the habit worth
  building.

Two exercises.

**One — the check this page could not do.** Open <https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/live-api>
and <https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/rate-limits> in a **browser**, where the tables actually
render, and write these four lines somewhere you will find them again:

```text
Live API free tier offered?  ____________________
limits stated (if any)       ____________________
URL read                     ____________________
date read                    ____________________
```

Then do the thing that proves the point: fetch the same URL with a command-line tool and search the
response for the numbers you just read with your own eyes. When they are not there, you have
reproduced this part's finding yourself, and you will never again treat a successful fetch as a
successful verification.

**Two — the robustness test, applied to your own work.** Take a decision you are currently putting
off because you are missing a fact. Write the decision down, then write the missing fact, then write
what you would do **if the fact turned out to be the best possible answer** and what you would do
**if it were the worst**. If both branches end in the same action, you were never blocked — make the
decision now and record that you made it without the fact. If they differ, write down the one
specific question that separates them and where you would ask it. Either way you end the exercise
with something you did not have at the start.

**Out loud, without scrolling up:** *this page says text streaming is free and bidirectional voice is
parked — but it also says it could not find out whether the Live API is free at all. Why did that
missing fact not change the parking decision, and what would you have had to write if it had?*

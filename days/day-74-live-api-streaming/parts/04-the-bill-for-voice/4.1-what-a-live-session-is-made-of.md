# 4.1 — What a live session is made of

## One-line answer

`BIDI` is not a third setting of the streaming dial — it is a different **shape of program**:
`run_async` takes one message and hands you back events, while `run_live` takes no message at all and
requires a `live_request_queue` you keep pushing into while events come back the other way — and the
clearest statement of what that feature *is* comes from counting the `RunConfig` fields that exist
only for it, which on the installed `google-adk==2.7.1` is **eleven**, every one of them naming an
audio or session-control concern that a request-and-reply turn simply does not have.

## The story

You are learning to drive, and the first arrangement is a supermarket car park on a Sunday with an
uncle who has never taught anybody anything.

It settles into a routine within the first few attempts. He stands out in front of the car where you
can see him. You drive at him, badly, and stall. He walks round to your side. You wind the window
down. You ask the question you have been holding onto for the whole attempt — *when am I meant to
lift the clutch, exactly?* — and he answers it. You wind the window back up. He walks back round to
the front, and you try again.

It works, sort of. But look at what the arrangement forbids. While you are driving, nobody can say
anything. Whatever he notices about your hands, your mirrors, the fact that you are in third gear at
walking pace — he has to hold onto until you stop, and by the time the window is down you have both
lost half of it. And whatever you wanted to ask has to be saved up into one question, because you
only get the window down once per attempt. One thing goes across, then one thing comes back, then
the channel closes.

The second arrangement is a booked lesson, and the thing that makes it different is not that the
instructor is better at explaining. It is the seat. He is sitting beside you with the engine running,
and there is a second set of pedals under his feet. He says "mirror" while you are mid-turn. You say
"there's a cyclist" while he is mid-sentence. He touches the brake without asking you first and
without waiting for you to finish your sentence, because there is a child by the parked van and the
conversation is not the point.

Nobody sped anything up. Winding that window down twice as fast would not have turned the car park
into a lesson. The lesson is a different **arrangement**: both people are in the channel at the same
time, neither has to wait for a turn, and both of them can act while the other one is still going.

Everything below is that difference, written in a function signature.

## The idea in plain language

Part [1.2](../01-the-blank-screen/1.2-one-field-on-runconfig.md) established the enum. `StreamingMode`
has three members — `NONE`, `SSE`, `BIDI` — and they sit in the same Python enumeration, which makes
them look like three settings of one dial. `NONE` off, `SSE` on, `BIDI` on harder. That reading is
natural, it is what the shared enum invites, and it is wrong in an expensive way.

`NONE` and `SSE` really are two settings of one dial. Same call, same arguments, same return type;
the only difference is whether the answer arrives as one lump or as a run of chunks. Part
[1.2](../01-the-blank-screen/1.2-one-field-on-runconfig.md) traced it to a single expression inside
ADK's flow, `stream=run_config.streaming_mode == StreamingMode.SSE`, which is about as small as a
feature switch gets.

`BIDI` is somewhere else entirely. **Bidirectional** means *both directions at once*: material
travels from you to the model and from the model to you over the same open connection, and neither
side has to wait for the other to finish. That is the instructor's seat rather than the wound-down
window, and it is not something a boolean flag on a request can express, because there is no longer a
single request to put the flag on.

You can see the whole argument in two signatures. A **signature** is the list of parameters a
function accepts, with their names and their defaults — the shape of the call, written out. Read off
`google-adk==2.7.1` on 2026-09-06:

```text
Runner.run_async(self, *, user_id, session_id, invocation_id=None, new_message=None,
                 state_delta=None, run_config=None, yield_user_message=False)

Runner.run_live(self, *, user_id=None, session_id=None, live_request_queue,
                run_config=None, session=None)
```

Look at what is and is not there.

On `run_async`, `new_message` is the thing you are asking about, and it is **optional** — it has a
default of `None`. On `run_live` there is no message parameter at all. Not optional: absent. In its
place sits `live_request_queue`, which is the one argument with no default in the whole signature.

A **queue** here is an object you hand to the runner and then keep putting things into while it is
running. You do not call the runner again for each thing; the runner is already going, reading from
one end of the queue while you push into the other. That is the seat and the second set of pedals: a
channel that stays open, with something happening at both ends of it.

So the shape of the calling program changes. With `run_async` your code has one moment where it says
what it wants and then a loop where it receives. With `run_live` your code needs two things happening
at once — something pushing audio or text into the queue, and something consuming events out of the
runner — and the moment you write that down you have discovered you now own concurrency you did not
own before. That is not a bigger version of the first program. It is a second program.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because ADK-53 is the free-quota check, and a quota check written in the wrong order is worthless. If
you decide first whether you can afford something and only then look at what it is, you have made a
budget decision about a thing you cannot describe. The honest order is the other way round: establish
what the feature requires, in detail, with the evidence on the page — then decide whether this
curriculum can pay for it.

This part does the first half. Part [4.2](4.2-the-free-quota-check.md) does the second, and it is
the one that carries the verdict, including the fact it could not verify.

There is a second reason, and it survives this day. Every framework you adopt will have a feature
that shares a name or an enum with something you already use, and that shared name will quietly tell
you the two things are variants of each other. Sometimes they are. Sometimes, as here, one of them is
a different program wearing the same label, and the cheapest way to find out is to read the
signatures side by side before you plan any work around it.

## The mechanism

The whole check is `lab/livecheck.py`. It contacts nothing, spends nothing, and needs no key. It
imports three names from the installed package, looks at them, and prints what it found.

Two blocks in it are doing the real work.

The first asks `run_live` what it insists on:

```python
    sig = inspect.signature(Runner.run_live)
    required = [
        name
        for name, param in sig.parameters.items()
        if name != "self" and param.default is inspect.Parameter.empty
    ]
```

**Line by line:**

- `inspect.signature(Runner.run_live)` is the standard library asking a function object to describe
  itself — its parameters, in order, with their defaults. It reads the object that was imported from
  the installed package, so it cannot disagree with the code that would actually run.
- `sig.parameters.items()` walks those parameters as `(name, parameter)` pairs.
- `name != "self"` drops the first one. `Runner.run_live` is being looked up on the **class** rather
  than on an instance, so the method is *unbound* and `self` is still an ordinary parameter in the
  list. On a real call it is filled in by the instance you called through, so counting it as
  something the caller must supply would be wrong.
- `param.default is inspect.Parameter.empty` is how you ask *is this argument required?* This is the
  line worth slowing down on. You might expect a missing default to show up as `None`, and it cannot,
  because `None` is a perfectly good default that plenty of parameters really have — `new_message`
  on `run_async` is exactly that. If "no default" and "default of `None`" were both spelled `None`,
  the two would be indistinguishable. So `inspect` uses a **sentinel**: a unique object that exists
  only to mean *nothing here*, and that can never be confused with a real value because no real
  value is that object. `inspect.Parameter.empty` is that object, and `is` — identity, not equality —
  is the right way to test for it.
- What comes back is a list of the arguments the caller has no choice about. Print it and the
  argument for this whole part is one line long.

The second block asks `RunConfig` which live-only fields it actually has:

```python
    present = [name for name in LIVE_ONLY if name in RunConfig.model_fields]
```

**Line by line:**

- `LIVE_ONLY` is a **hand-written tuple at the top of the lab script**. Be clear about what that
  means: it is the lab's editorial judgement about which `RunConfig` fields belong to live sessions
  and to nothing else. Nobody handed it over; somebody read the field list and grouped it. It could
  be one name too long or one name too short.
- `RunConfig.model_fields` is the other half, and it is not editorial at all. `RunConfig` is a
  Pydantic model, and `model_fields` is the mapping of the fields that model actually declares —
  read off the class in your `.venv`, on the version you have pinned.
- The comprehension crosses one against the other. So the script does not trust the hand-written list
  and does not merely dump the class either: it reports **which of the names it expected are really
  there**. The list is the claim; the check against `model_fields` is the verified part.
- The script then computes the complement and prints a `not present in this version:` line if any
  expected name is missing — which is the whole point of writing it this way rather than writing the
  number `11` into a document.

Run it:

```bash
cd days/day-74-live-api-streaming/lab
uv run python livecheck.py
```

**Line by line:**

- `cd` into the lab first, because `livecheck.py` is a plain script rather than an installed module.
- `uv run` executes it inside this project's own environment, so the version it reports is the
  version the repository pins rather than whatever Python is first on your `PATH`.
- It takes no arguments and opens no connection. Everything it prints comes off the package on disk.

Two findings.

**One required argument, and it is the queue.** Everything else on `run_live` has a default —
including `user_id` and `session_id`, which are required on `run_async`. The single thing you cannot
omit is the channel. That is the signature agreeing with the story: the arrangement is the seat, not
the sentence you say once through the window.

**Eleven fields exist for this and nothing else.** That number is the useful one, because it is
independent of anybody's marketing. A feature that were genuinely "`SSE` but more so" would need no
new configuration at all — the same call, the same knobs. Eleven new knobs is the framework telling
you what kind of thing you are turning on.

Group them and the shape is obvious:

| Group | Fields | What the group is about |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sound in and out | `speech_config`, `response_modalities`, `realtime_input_config`, `explicit_vad_signal` | how audio goes in, what comes back, and how the incoming side is signalled |
| Text alongside the sound | `input_audio_transcription`, `output_audio_transcription` | a written version of each direction of the conversation |
| The session as an object | `session_resumption`, `save_live_audio`, `save_live_blob` | something with a lifetime, and something that can be kept |
| Conduct in a conversation | `enable_affective_dialog`, `proactivity` | how the model carries itself when nobody is taking strict turns |

**These names are evidence of the shape of the feature, not a manual for it.** This page has not read
the documentation for a single one of them and is not going to describe what any individual field
does beyond what its own name plainly says — inventing behaviour for an API you have not read is
exactly what Principle 8 forbids, and it is the most tempting kind of invention because a plausible
sentence about `proactivity` would be very easy to write. The finding here is the *census*, not the
manual: every one of the eleven names is about audio, transcription, session lifetime, or how to
behave when both parties can speak. Not one of them would mean anything to a request that is answered
and finished.

🅿️ **Bidi voice is parked on this day.** `run_live` needs a model that will hold an audio session
open, and Addendum 02 does not permit anything requiring a billing account. Part
[4.2](4.2-the-free-quota-check.md) is where that verdict is actually made, with what could and could
not be checked; Day 75 — Live API II, the voice loop — is where it is spent.

## When it breaks

**A field list is not a feature.** This part now knows what a live session *involves*. It does not
know how to run one, and the distance between those two is larger than a page of names suggests.
Nothing here establishes what you put on the queue, in what encoding, at what rate; how the runner
signals that the model has started speaking or stopped; what an event carrying audio looks like on
the way out; or what closing the queue does to the session. Four unanswered questions, from a check
that took one short script. That is a healthy ratio for a survey and a terrible one for a plan.

**Nothing on this page has opened a live session.** Everything above is read off signatures and class
fields with `inspect` and Pydantic, on a package sitting on disk, with no network call at any point.
That is exactly as far as the check goes, and it goes that far honestly — but a signature tells you
what a call will *accept*, never what it will *do*. Treat this as a map drawn from the index of an
atlas. It is genuinely useful for deciding where to go, and it is not the same thing as having been
there. When Day 75 revisits the voice loop, the first honest sentence in it should be about which of
these eleven names turned out to matter.

**The number `11` has a version and a date attached to it.** It is `google-adk==2.7.1` on 2026-09-06,
and nothing more. This is Principle 7 in its least dramatic form: a count in prose looks like a fact
about the world and is actually a measurement of one pin on one day. That is precisely why
`livecheck.py` prints a `not present in this version:` line when a name in `LIVE_ONLY` is missing
from `RunConfig.model_fields` — the script is built to disagree with this page. When the pin moves,
the correct response is to run it again and write down the new version, the new count and the new
date, not to trust the paragraph you are reading.

## In production

**A held-open connection is a per-user cost, not a per-request cost.** This is the change that
reaches your capacity planning before it reaches your code. A request-and-reply turn — everything
this day has built up to part [3.3](../03-the-wire/3.3-what-sits-in-between.md) — occupies resources
while it is being answered and then lets go. Ten thousand people who each ask one thing occasionally
are nothing like ten thousand connections, because their turns do not overlap. A live session
overlaps by definition: it is open for as long as the person is in it, whether or not anybody is
speaking. So concurrency stops being a throughput question — how many turns can we answer — and
becomes an occupancy question: how many seats do we have, and what happens to the person who arrives
when they are all taken. Every layer between you and the user has an opinion about that number, and
part [3.3](../03-the-wire/3.3-what-sits-in-between.md) is the reminder that those layers exist and do
not consult you.

**Interruption is normal traffic, not an error.** In a turn-based system, input arriving while the
model is producing output is a bug in your client, and most handlers are written to make it
impossible. In a live session it is the single most ordinary thing a person does: they hear enough,
they know it is wrong, and they start talking. The instructor says "mirror" and you say "there's a
cyclist" over the top of it, and neither of you has done anything unusual. A system that treats that
as a fault will feel broken to every user who is not being careful, which is all of them. Somewhere
in your design there has to be an answer to *what happens to the output already in flight when new
input arrives*, and "we never thought about it" produces the model talking over a person who has
stopped listening.

**A session has a lifetime, so it has to survive things.** This is where one of the eleven field
names stops being a curiosity: `session_resumption` exists because connections drop. A turn that
fails can be retried — you still have the question, and part
[1.2](../01-the-blank-screen/1.2-one-field-on-runconfig.md)'s whole call is one message you still
hold. A live session that drops has taken the accumulated state of a conversation with it, and the
person on the other end did not experience a failed request; they experienced being cut off
mid-sentence. That the framework has a named field for the problem is the framework confirming the
problem is routine.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"I'm glad this came out of the installed package
and not a feature page, and I want the script kept so it re-runs when we bump the pin. But be careful
how you present it internally, because 'we looked at bidi' will get heard as 'we can do voice.' What
we have is a census of eleven config fields and one required argument. Before anyone puts this on a
roadmap I want three answers we do not have: how many concurrent sessions a box holds, what the
handler does when a user talks over the model, and what a dropped connection does to a
half-finished conversation. That last one is why there's a resumption field in the list, and it's a
design decision rather than a config value. And the queue is the part I'd prototype first — it means
we own concurrency in the request path, and we should find out what that costs us on something small
before it's in front of users."*

**The interview question:** *"What's the difference between server-sent streaming and a bidirectional
live session?"* An answer that shows the work: *"They look like two settings of one dial, because in
ADK they're members of the same `StreamingMode` enum — `NONE`, `SSE`, `BIDI`. But `SSE` is the same
request-and-reply call with a flag on it: one expression in the flow turns the config field into
`stream=True`, and you get chunks instead of a lump. `BIDI` is a different shape of program. I
compared the two runner signatures on the version we have installed: `run_async` takes an optional
`new_message`, and `run_live` has no message parameter at all — its one argument with no default is
`live_request_queue`. So the caller isn't handing over a question any more, it's holding a channel
open and pushing into it while events come back the other way, which means the calling program now
has two things happening at once. The other thing I did was count the `RunConfig` fields that only
exist for live sessions: eleven on `google-adk==2.7.1`, and every one is about audio, transcription,
session lifetime, or how to behave when both sides can speak. Nothing a finished turn ever needs.
That count is what convinced me it wasn't a variant. In production the costs that change are
occupancy rather than throughput — a connection per user instead of per request — interruption
becoming normal traffic rather than an error, and resumption, because a dropped live session loses
conversational state that a retried request wouldn't. I should say plainly: I read this off the
installed package's signatures; I have not run a live session. Everything about how it actually
behaves is still unverified for me."*

## Check yourself

```bash
cd days/day-74-live-api-streaming/lab
uv run python livecheck.py
uv run python -c "import inspect; from google.adk.runners import Runner; print(inspect.signature(Runner.run_async)); print(inspect.signature(Runner.run_live))"
```

**Line by line:**

- The first command re-runs the whole check and prints the version, the required argument and the
  count together, which is the record you want.
- The second prints the two signatures next to each other with nothing else on screen. Read them as a
  pair. `new_message=None` on one, and no message parameter at all on the other — that is this part's
  entire argument in two lines of terminal output.

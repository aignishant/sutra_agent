# 6.2 — 🅿️ What Phase 9 takes over

## One-line answer

Containment stops a run; it does not put the work back, and the three things this day deliberately
does not build — a run deadline, bulkheads between workloads, and resumption — are the boundary
between "it stopped" and "nothing was lost".

## The story

The building has a big red button by the door of the machine room. Press it and everything stops.

It is the right thing to have and it is not the whole story. What everyone who has actually pressed
one knows is that the interesting part starts afterwards: which machines were mid-cycle, what is
still in them, what has to be thrown away, what can be restarted from where, and who has to be told.
The button is one afternoon's work to install. The procedure for the hour after it is pressed is a
document somebody maintains for years.

## The idea in plain language

Everything in this day answers one question: **how do you make it stop?**

There is a second question, and this day does not answer it: **what happens to the work?**

They are genuinely different questions and it is worth being precise about the line between them,
because a reader who has just built four brakes will reasonably feel that containment is handled.

| Question | Answered by | Where |
| --- | --- | --- |
| how does it stop? | brakes, guards, the kill | today |
| what state is it in when it stops? | checkpoints | Day 61 |
| how does it start again correctly? | resume, replay, idempotency | Day 60 |
| who decides whether it should? | approval gates | Days 63–64 |
| does all of that actually hold? | the Phase 9 gate | Day 65 |

[2.5](../02-where-a-brake-goes/2.5-the-kill-switch-and-what-it-leaves.md) is where the two questions
touch, and it stopped deliberately short: it measured that killing at `draft` and killing at `review`
leave **identical state** and differ by a model call, and then said that what to do about that is
Phase 9's. This part is the map of what that means.

🅿️ **Everything below is parked**: you learn what it is and why it is next, and you do not build it
today.

## Why Sutra needs it

Phase 8's gate is *triage graph v1 end-to-end* and section 5 audited it. Phase 9's gate is *kill it
mid-run; it resumes; a human approved the write* — and the first three words of that sentence are
this day's, while the rest are not.

That overlap is deliberate and it is worth reading as a design statement about the curriculum:
Principle 13 says blast radius before capability, so the ability to *stop* an autonomous run is
required in the same phase that introduces autonomous runs. The ability to *resume* one is not
required for safety; it is required for usefulness, and it gets its own phase because it is harder.

## The mechanism

Three things this day identified and did not build.

**One: a run deadline.** [5.2](../05-the-gate/5.2-criterion-2-every-shape-has-a-brake.md)'s table has
an empty column, and [1.5](../01-four-runaways/1.5-the-quiet-runaway-the-fuse-cannot-see.md) measured
what fills it: two hundred and one iterations under a fuse of four. The distinction that matters, and
which is easy to get wrong:

```python
FunctionNode(func=fetch_page, name="fetch_page", timeout=5.0)
```

**Line by line:**

- `timeout=5.0` is a real field on `FunctionNode`, and `NodeTimeoutError` is a real export of
  `google.adk.workflow` — both verified on the installed `google-adk==2.7.1` on 2026-09-05 with
  `python -c "from google.adk.workflow import FunctionNode, NodeTimeoutError; print(list(FunctionNode.model_fields))"`.
- And it is **not a run deadline.** It bounds one execution of one node. A node that returns in
  milliseconds, two hundred times, never approaches it. A run deadline is a different mechanism at a
  different altitude, and the reason it is parked rather than built is that ADK does not offer one on
  `RunConfig` in 2.7.1 — so it would have to be a wrapper around the run, which is a design decision
  this day should surface rather than make in passing.

**Two: bulkheads.** All four brakes in this day protect the system from **one run**. None of them
protects one workload from another. The nightly batch and the interactive desk share a quota, so a
batch that spends everything at one in the morning leaves the desk with nothing at nine — and every
brake fired correctly on every run. A bulkhead is a reserved allocation per workload, and the reason
it is not today's is that it requires a shared, per-workload ledger, which is Day 70's Quota-Router
territory.

**Three: resumption**, which is the largest of the three and is Days 60 and 61 in their entirety. The
shape of what is missing is exactly the finding from
[2.5](../02-where-a-brake-goes/2.5-the-kill-switch-and-what-it-leaves.md):

```text
kill point: inside the 'draft' station
    stations visited: 4  ['intake', 'classify', 'research', 'draft']
    model calls spent and not recoverable: 2

kill point: inside the 'review' station
    stations visited: 5  ['intake', 'classify', 'research', 'draft', 'review']
    model calls spent and not recoverable: 3
```

Identical state, different work done. A resume that reads state alone re-runs the review and pays a
fourth call. That is the problem Day 60 opens with, and the vocabulary it introduces — replay,
idempotency, the event log as the source of truth — is the vocabulary for solving it.

## When it breaks

The way this boundary breaks is a team believing they have durability because they have containment.

The symptom is a system that stops beautifully and loses work quietly. Every runaway is caught, every
brake fires, every refusal is well-structured — and every stopped run is a ticket that a human has to
notice, find, and start again by hand. The containment is real; the operational cost has just been
moved onto people, and it is invisible in every dashboard because a contained run is not an error.

The measurable version of that: today's day has a `stopped_by` field
([2.1](../02-where-a-brake-goes/2.1-a-guard-is-only-a-guard-if-it-is-outside.md)) and no `resume_from`
field. Everything the system knows about a stopped run is *why*; nothing is *where*. Those two are
what a resume needs together, and having only the first is what containment-without-durability looks
like in the data.

There is a second break worth naming, because it is a real risk of teaching containment first. A
brake that fires and loses work is expensive, so there is pressure to make the brakes looser — which
is [3.4](../03-brakes-that-do-not-hold/3.4-the-brake-loosened-for-one-ticket.md)'s failure arriving
for an operational reason rather than a quality one. **Durability is what makes strict containment
affordable**, and a team that builds the brakes first will feel that pressure for as long as it takes
them to build the resume.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead.** They build the three in a specific order and it is not the
order they are listed above. Deadlines first, because they are the cheapest and cover the shape
nothing else does. Then resumption, because it is what makes everything else affordable. Bulkheads
last, because they only matter once there is more than one workload, and until then they are a
generalisation of a problem you do not have.

They also keep the two vocabularies separate in their data. A run that was **contained** and a run
that **crashed** are different rows with different follow-ups, and a run that was contained and
**resumed successfully** is a third. Collapsing those three into "failed" is how a team ends up
unable to answer whether their containment is working.

**What degrades at scale.** The cost of a stopped run is linear in volume and the cost of a human
restarting it is not — human attention is the resource that does not scale, so a system that stops
correctly and resumes manually hits a wall at whatever volume exceeds one person's afternoon.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic.** Contained runs cluster. The tickets that
trip brakes are the hard ones, and they arrive together, so the manual restart queue goes from empty
to forty in an hour — at exactly the moment the humans are dealing with whatever caused it.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"We have four brakes and no way to resume. That is
the right order to build them in, but let us be honest in the runbook that every contained run is
currently a manual restart, and let us add a `resume_from` marker now even though nothing reads it
yet — because the alternative is discovering in Phase 9 that we have three months of stopped runs we
cannot place."*

**The interview question:** *"You have containment. What have you still not got?"* — *"Recovery.
Stopping and resuming are different problems and we deliberately built the first. What that leaves is
a system where every contained run is a ticket somebody restarts by hand, which is fine at our volume
and is a wall at ten times it. Concretely, our runs record why they stopped and not where, and I
measured why that matters: killing our graph at two different stations leaves byte-identical state
and differs by a spent model call, so state alone cannot tell a resume what has already been done.
The other two gaps are a whole-run deadline — a per-node timeout is not one, because a fast node that
runs two hundred times never trips it — and bulkheads between workloads, since our nightly batch and
our interactive path currently share one quota with no reservation."*

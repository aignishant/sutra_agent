# 5.2 — What a real public repository adds

## One-line answer

Nine things stand between this repository and a door that can safely open, and they are not equally
urgent: three are the licence, the ledger and the lint, they take about an afternoon between them, and
everything else on the list is easier once they are done.

## The story

The second-hand phone, sold still signed in.

Somebody sells a phone. They wipe the photos, delete the messages, clear the browser — carefully, and
in the order that occurs to them. The buyer turns it on and finds the mail app still logged in.

The seller was not careless about privacy; they were careless about *order*. There is one action —
sign out of the account, then factory reset — that makes every other action unnecessary, and a dozen
smaller actions that feel like progress and do not compose into safety. Deleting photos one by one is
work. It is not the work.

The list matters less than which item is first.

## The idea in plain language

Everything this day measured is a finding. A list of findings is not a plan, and the difference is
ordering — Day 83 part 5.3 made the same argument at a phase boundary and it applies here with a
harder deadline, because after the door opens some of these stop being fixable.

Nine items, in the order to do them.

**1. Add `LICENSE`.** Part [1.2](../01-the-stranger/1.2-the-five-files-before-the-code.md). Without
it the repository is visible and legally unusable, and the README's own licence sentence points at a
file that is not there. A candidate sits in `lab/candidates/LICENSE.candidate`; choosing is a
decision, adding is a minute.

**2. Backfill `docs/PROGRESS.md`.** Part [4.3](../04-nothing-extra/4.3-the-ledger-a-stranger-reads.md).
Forty-three rows, recoverable from `git log` today and harder every week. It un-refuses `./m brief`,
un-blocks `./m trace`, and makes the README's headline claim true again.

**3. Fix the one lint error.** Part
[2.2](../02-running-the-readme/2.2-the-setup-block-that-fails.md). `./m check` is the gate the README
promises is green, and it is red over a single import-order error that ruff will fix automatically.

**4. Fix the three rotted links.** Part [3.2](../03-the-docs-pass/3.2-four-kinds-of-broken-link.md).
The only group in the fifty that is a writing task.

**5. Fix the wiki generator.** Part
[3.3](../03-the-docs-pass/3.3-the-index-that-regenerates-broken.md). Twenty-two links, one cause:
rewrite relative links when copying a part's answer into `docs/wiki/`. Then re-run `./m trace`, which
repairs the other seven by itself.

**6. Make the two placeholders obviously fake.** Part
[4.1](../04-nothing-extra/4.1-the-scan-before-the-door-opens.md). They are not secrets and they will
fire the scan forever, which is how a scan stops being read.

**7. Add `SECURITY.md` and `CONTRIBUTING.md`.** Part
[1.2](../01-the-stranger/1.2-the-five-files-before-the-code.md). One paragraph each. A candidate for
the first is in `lab/candidates/`.

**8. State the machine precondition in Setup.** Part
[2.2](../02-running-the-readme/2.2-the-setup-block-that-fails.md). One sentence, so `os error 5` is
met with an explanation rather than a mystery.

**9. Put the checks in `./m check`.** Everything in this day is a script that exits non-zero. Until
one of them runs on every commit, all nine of these come back.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because items 1 and 2 decay and the rest do not. A licence added after publication does not cover the
copies already made; a ledger row gets harder to reconstruct every week the log grows. Everything
else on this list costs the same next month as it does today, which is the only ordering principle
that survives contact with a real week.

And because item 9 is what stops this day being a one-off audit. Ninety-three days of teaching say
that a finding which is not a check is a thing you have to be lucky enough to notice twice.

## The mechanism

The nine, with cost and what each unblocks:

| # | Item | Where | Roughly | Unblocks |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | add `LICENSE` | repo root | a minute plus a decision | anybody using it at all |
| 2 | backfill the ledger | `docs/PROGRESS.md` | an afternoon | `./m brief`, `./m trace`, `./m status` |
| 3 | fix the lint | `tests/test_persona.py` | one command | `./m check`, the README's promise |
| 4 | fix three rotted links | three day parts | half an hour | three dead ends |
| 5 | fix the wiki generator | `scripts/wiki.py` | an hour | twenty-two links, permanently |
| 6 | de-fang the placeholders | two day parts | ten minutes | the scan staying meaningful |
| 7 | `SECURITY.md`, `CONTRIBUTING.md` | repo root | half an hour | a private report channel |
| 8 | the machine precondition | `README.md` | one sentence | every future `os error 5` |
| 9 | checks in `./m check` | `m`, `scripts/` | an hour | all of the above, permanently |

**Items 1, 2 and 3 are about an afternoon between them** and they are the three that are either
decaying or contradicting a written promise. Items 4 to 8 are tidy-up. Item 9 is the one that decides
whether this list is ever needed again.

- `tail -12` for the verdict; each check's findings print above it.

Measured on 2026-09-07:

  FAIL              2  the README describes what exists
  FAIL              3  every relative link resolves
  FAIL              4  broken links have an owner
  FAIL              5  nothing secret-shaped is tracked
  FAIL              6  the ledger matches the day folders

Check 7 will stay red until the reader builds the product, which is by design — part
[5.1](5.1-a-demo-that-fails-honestly.md) is why. The other six are the nine items above, and they can
all be green.

## When it breaks

The list breaks by being worked top to bottom as written rather than by decay, and the failure has a
recognisable shape:

```text
1. the list is triaged by severity, so the secret scan goes first
2. the two placeholders are fixed - ten minutes, and nothing was at risk
3. the licence is "obvious" and left for the pull request that flips it public
4. the repository goes public in that pull request
5. copies are taken during the window before the licence lands
6. the ledger is still forty-three rows behind, and now strangers are reading it
```

Steps 3 and 4 are the phone. Every action taken was real work; the one that had to be first was not,
and the cost of that lands after the reversible window closes.

There is no error text for any of it, and no check in this day catches it either — ordering is a
judgement, and the only thing that enforces a judgement is somebody having written it down.

## In production

**What a professional does.** Sorts a pre-launch list by **what stops being fixable**, not by
severity, and does that part first. The rest is ordinary backlog and can be argued about. The
practical form is a short blocking list — three or four items — with everything else explicitly
deferred in writing, because a list of nine equal-looking items gets worked in the order they were
found.

**What degrades at scale.** Findings accumulate faster than they are fixed once a repository has an
audience, because every reader generates some. A repository that goes public without item 9 spends
the following months rediscovering this list one issue at a time, from strangers, which is the most
expensive way to receive it.

**The failure that only shows with real traffic.** Public attention is not evenly distributed. A
repository that gets noticed gets all of its attention in a few days, and whatever state it is in
during those days is the impression that persists — in forks, in links, in people's memory of it.
Fixing item 1 the week after is correct and does not reach anybody who already looked.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Licence, ledger, lint — this week, before the flip.
Everything else can land after, and put item 9 on the same pull request as the flip so we do not
audit this by hand again."*

**The interview question:** *"You have a pre-launch list of nine things and time for three. Which
three?"* The answer that shows experience picks by irreversibility rather than by severity, says so
explicitly, and then names what it is consciously deferring — because the interviewer is listening for
whether the deferral was a decision or an omission.

# 6.2 — Writing down what green means

## One-line answer

A suite's boundary has to live inside the suite, as a test that passes on purpose and says why,
because a docstring is read once by the person who wrote it and a test runs in front of everyone
forever.

## The story

The sticker on the bike lock says it is approved for bikes worth up to fifty pounds.

That is a strange thing to print on a product, and it is the most useful sentence on the packaging.
The lock is a real lock. It is made of real metal and it will really stop somebody walking off with
your bike on a casual afternoon. The sticker is not an apology for the lock; it is a statement of what
the lock was tested against, printed by the people who did the testing.

The person who buys it for a fifty-pound bike is well served. The person who buys it for a
thousand-pound bike is also well served, if they read the sticker — because they will buy a second
lock as well, and chain the frame to something solid, and not leave it out overnight.

The one who is not served is the person who bought a lock, saw that it locked, and reasoned no
further. And the sticker is the only thing standing between them and that.

## The idea in plain language

[1.2](../01-testing-prose/1.2-shape-and-sense.md) drew the line: this suite checks **shape** and never
**sense**. That was a paragraph in a lesson, and paragraphs in lessons are not read by the person who
runs the suite in eight months.

So the boundary is written twice, in the two places it will actually be met.

**In the module docstring**, which is what somebody opening `tests/test_skills.py` reads first — three
lanes, named, so the file's own structure says what it covers.

**As a test**, and this is the part that survives. A test that passes deliberately, holding a skill
that is perfectly shaped and genuinely bad, with the reason in its docstring. It appears in every
run's collected list. It shows up when somebody greps for a skill's name. It cannot be deleted without
somebody reading why it exists, because deleting a passing test requires opening it.

The general form is worth naming, because it applies far past this file: **a passing test can be
documentation, and it is the only kind of documentation that is executed.** A comment saying *"note:
we do not check routing quality here"* decays into a comment about a version of the code that no
longer exists. A test named `test_a_skill_can_be_shaped_right_and_still_be_wrong`, holding the actual
mush description, stays true because it runs.

What is on the other side of the line has to be named too, or the boundary reads as a shrug rather
than a plan:

| Not covered here | Covered by | When it runs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| does the description still route? | Day 28's routing gate, `lab/route.py` and `lab/routing_gate.py` | before merging a description change ([5.2](../05-versioning/5.2-the-bump-rule.md)'s minor obligation) |
| is a line an attack? | Day 29's five-pass audit | when a skill is sourced, and after a major bump |
| are the steps right for the business? | a person who knows the business | at review |
| does the agent actually behave? | evalsets, from Phase 12 | on a schedule, spending quota |

Each row has an owner and a trigger. A gap with an owner is a decision; a gap without one is a hole.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because Sutra has a documented case of a change that passes every check in this suite and should not
be merged.

[Day 29, 6.1](../../../day-29-sourcing-and-auditing-skills/parts/06-in-production/6.1-run-the-pack-against-the-routing-gate.md)
took a four-skill pack that was clean under a full security audit — zero findings — and watched it drop
Sutra's worst routing margin from one to zero. Every one of those four skills has a valid name, a
1024-character-compliant description, no dead pointers, and could carry a version and a digest
tomorrow. Run today's suite over that pack and it is silent.

That pack is the fifty-pound sticker made real. If the only sentence anybody remembers about
`tests/test_skills.py` is *"the skills tests are green"*, the pack merges and routing degrades. If the
suite says out loud what it did not look at, the reviewer knows there is a second gate to run.

## The paper behind it

> **Hints on Test Data Selection: Help for the Practicing Programmer**, `doi:10.1109/C-M.1978.218136`,
> 1978. `https://doi.org/10.1109/C-M.1978.218136`

The claim: a suite is worth judging by whether it can tell the real program from a slightly broken one,
which is a statement about what a suite *would notice* rather than about what it *executed* — the same
distinction between coverage and meaning that this part draws between shape and sense.

Read it after the parts: [Hints on Test Data Selection](../../papers/01-hints-on-test-data-selection.md).

## The mechanism

The docstring first. It is the top of `tests/test_skills.py` and it is three sentences:

```python
"""The skills shelf under test - three lanes, zero model calls.

The shelf lane: every folder in skills/, enumerated live, breaks no rule.
The checker lane: the checks run against skills built in memory, so each rule can be
proved to fire without a broken folder ever existing on disk.
The boundary lane: one test that states, in code, what these checks cannot know.
"""
```

**Line by line:**

- *Three lanes* is the file's map, and naming the lanes is what makes
  [4.3](../04-skills-built-in-code/4.3-what-memory-cannot-test.md)'s split visible to somebody adding a
  test — they have to decide which lane theirs belongs in, which is the decision that was going to be
  made badly by default.
- *zero model calls* is in the first line because it is the property most likely to be broken by a
  well-meaning addition. Somebody wanting to check routing quality reaches for a model, and the first
  line of the file is the argument against.
- The boundary lane is described as *what these checks cannot know*, not *what we did not have time
  for*. The distinction matters: one is a design decision with an owner, the other is a backlog item,
  and only the first is worth writing down.

Then the test itself:

```python
def test_a_skill_can_be_shaped_right_and_still_be_wrong() -> None:
    """The boundary, pinned in code: these checks read shape, never sense.

    This description passes every mechanical rule and is routing mush - it would take
    work from every other skill on the shelf. Nothing in skill_checks.py can tell.
    """
    mush = a_skill(description="Handles stuff. Use when something comes up.", version="1.0")
    assert check_description(mush) == []
    assert check_version(mush) == []
```

**Line by line:**

- The test name is a sentence, and it is the only part of this file some people will ever read, because
  it appears in `pytest -v` output and in every failure list. It states the claim rather than naming a
  function.
- The docstring is written for the person who is about to delete the test. It says what the test is
  for, gives the specific reason the object inside it is bad, and names the module that cannot tell.
- `"Handles stuff. Use when something comes up."` is chosen with care. It contains *"Use when"*, so it
  satisfies `check_description`
  ([3.2](../03-the-suite/3.2-a-failure-must-name-the-file.md)) — the rule is a substring search, which
  is exactly the shape check it was designed to be. And it is genuinely terrible: every word in it is
  generic, so on Day 28's routing measurement it takes weight from every skill on the shelf
  ([Day 28, 2.2](../../../day-28-progressive-disclosure-design/parts/02-descriptions-as-routing/2.2-coverage-orthogonality-specificity.md)).
  A less realistic string would make the test a joke; this one is a description somebody would actually
  write on a Friday.
- `version="1.0"` is there so the object is clean under `check_version` too. The claim being pinned is
  *passes every mechanical rule*, so the object has to pass all of them, not just the one the test
  would otherwise touch.
- Two assertions, both asserting **emptiness**. It is the inverse of every other test in the file, and
  that inversion is the whole design: this test fails on the day somebody teaches a shape checker to
  have an opinion about sense, which is the change
  [1.2](../01-testing-prose/1.2-shape-and-sense.md) warns about and the change nobody would otherwise
  notice.
- `a_skill(...)` is [4.1](../04-skills-built-in-code/4.1-a-skill-without-a-folder.md)'s factory, so
  everything not named here is known-good. The test is about the description and the version and
  nothing else.

## When it breaks

**A test that documents nothing.** Written without the docstring and named
`test_check_description_passes`, it is indistinguishable from an ordinary happy-path test. Somebody
consolidating tests merges it into the parametrized description case and the boundary disappears with
no diff anybody would question. The name and the docstring are not decoration here; they are the
mechanism.

**The boundary that becomes a claim.** Six months on, somebody adds a rule that penalises generic words
in a description, meaning well. This test goes red:

```text
E       AssertionError: assert [Finding(skill='probe', rule='vague-description', ...)] == []
```

That is the test working. It is asking the right question at the right moment: *is this rule true or
false, or is it a judgement?* If it is a judgement it does not belong in a gate
([1.3](../01-testing-prose/1.3-the-rules-worth-a-test.md)), and if the team decides it does belong,
then this test's claim has changed and the test should be rewritten deliberately — not silenced.

**The boundary written only in a lesson.** This is the failure this part exists to prevent, and it
leaves no error text at all. The suite is correct, the docstrings are correct, and eighteen months
later somebody in a design review says *"skills are covered, we have tests"*, and nobody in the room
has read
[1.2](../01-testing-prose/1.2-shape-and-sense.md).

## In production

**What a professional writes instead** of a coverage percentage is a sentence about what the suite
would notice. Coverage says which lines ran
([Day 23, 6.1](../../../day-23-testing-tools-and-callbacks/parts/06-in-production/6.1-coverage-is-a-map-not-a-score.md)),
and a suite can execute every line of `skill_checks.py` while asserting nothing about whether the
checks fire — that is exactly the gap the 1978 paper measures by breaking the program and seeing
whether anything complains. The professional artefact is a short *what this suite does and does not
cover* section in the module docstring, backed by tests that demonstrate both halves.

**What degrades at scale** is the number of readers who have any context. In a team of three,
everybody who runs the suite helped write it. At thirty, most people meet it as a red or green tick in
a pull request, and the tick carries whatever meaning they assume. Written boundaries are how a claim
survives that transition, and they have to be in the artefact rather than in a wiki, because the wiki
is not in the pull request.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"the pack passes the skills suite, and the skills
suite has never had an opinion about routing. Run the routing gate before you merge this — that is
what the boundary test in `test_skills.py` is there to remind you of, and it is why we keep a test
that passes on purpose."*

**The interview question:** *"your tests are green. What does that actually tell me?"* The honest spoken
answer: *"For our skills suite, that every folder on the shelf loads, is shaped to the spec, has no
dead pointers, and carries a version whose digest still matches the folder. It tells you nothing about
whether the descriptions route, whether the steps are right, or whether a line is an attack — those
have separate gates with separate triggers. We write that boundary in two places: the module docstring
names the three lanes, and there is a test that passes on purpose, holding a description that is
perfectly valid and would wreck our routing, with the reason in its docstring. A passing test is the
only documentation that gets executed, and it fails the day somebody teaches the shape checker to have
an opinion about quality — which is exactly the moment we want a conversation."*

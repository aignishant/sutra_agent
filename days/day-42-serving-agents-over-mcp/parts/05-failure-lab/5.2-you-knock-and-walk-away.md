# 5.2 — You knock and walk away

## One-line answer

A caller sets its timeout while looking at tools that read a dictionary, a served agent needs several
model round-trips, and when the caller gives up **the server keeps working and finishes** — spending
your quota on an answer nobody will ever receive.

## The story

You knock on the neighbour's door about the parcel that was left with them.

Nothing happens. You knock again. Still nothing, so you decide they are out, and you walk back down
the stairs.

They were not out. They were in the back room and they heard the second knock, and they got up, and
they went to look for the parcel, which took a moment because it was behind the door of the spare
room. Then they opened the front door onto an empty landing.

Nobody did anything wrong. You waited about as long as anyone waits at a door. They came as fast as
anyone comes. And the parcel was found, and carried to the door, and the effort was entirely real —
it just landed on nobody, and neither of you knows what the other did.

## The idea in plain language

A **timeout** is the caller's decision about how long to wait. Day 38's
[1.1](../../../day-38-failure-and-migration-lab/parts/01-the-clock/1.1-the-only-clock-is-yours.md)
made the point that matters here: the clock belongs to the caller, and the server does not get told
about it.

Now put a served agent behind it. Day 34's tools answer in one hop — a dictionary read, or a SQLite
query. A caller looking at that server and choosing a timeout picks something small, because
something small is generous for a database read.

A served agent's reply comes after **one model round-trip per decision**, and
[4.1](../04-what-a-call-costs/4.1-the-kettle-and-the-car.md) counted three for an ordinary desk
answer. Free-tier Flash is not instantaneous. Three of them in sequence, plus the tool work in
between, is a different order of duration entirely — and the caller chose their number without ever
being told that.

When the caller's timeout fires, three things happen, and only the first is obvious:

1. The caller raises an error and stops waiting.
2. **The server does not know.** Nothing cancels it. There is no signal, because a timeout is a
   decision made entirely on the caller's side.
3. The agent runs to completion, spends every generation the answer needed, writes the result to a
   session nobody will read, and hands the reply to a connection that has gone.

That is the third outcome from [3.2](../03-tool-or-peer/3.2-a-note-handed-over-and-money-sent.md),
seen from the server's side for once: work definitely happened, and the caller definitely does not
know it.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because Sutra's callers are going to set that number, and Sutra cannot see it or influence it except
through the description.

`sutra-mcp` and `sutra_mcp/agent_server.py` are separate endpoints —
[1.4](../01-the-back-office/1.4-the-counter-by-the-side-door.md) — which means a caller *can* choose
two different timeouts. Whether they do depends entirely on whether they know they should, and the
only channel for telling them is the sentence *"calls are slow"* from
[4.3](../04-what-a-call-costs/4.3-the-notice-on-the-ration-shop-board.md).

And this is the failure that costs the most per occurrence. A quota refusal at least costs nothing
extra. An abandoned call spends the full price of an answer and delivers nothing, and then — because
the caller thinks the call failed — it very often spends it again.

## The mechanism

`lab/slow.py` puts the mismatch in front of you. The sleeps stand in for generations, so the script
costs nothing:

```python
# One leg of the loop: ask the model, read a tool result back. A free-tier Flash answer
# is not instant, and three of them in a row is an ordinary desk answer.
LEG_SECONDS = 1.2
LEGS = 3

# What the caller wrote down when it was pointing at Day 34's dictionary-reading tools.
IMPATIENT_TIMEOUT = timedelta(seconds=2)
PATIENT_TIMEOUT = timedelta(seconds=10)

legs_completed = 0


class ThreeRoundTrips(BaseAgent):
    """A desk answer that needs three legs of the loop before it has anything to say."""

    async def _run_async_impl(self, ctx: InvocationContext) -> AsyncGenerator[Event, None]:
        global legs_completed
        for leg in range(1, LEGS + 1):
            await asyncio.sleep(LEG_SECONDS)
            legs_completed = leg
            yield Event(
                author=self.name,
                invocation_id=ctx.invocation_id,
                partial=True,
                content=types.Content(role="model", parts=[types.Part(text=f"leg {leg} done")]),
            )
        yield Event(
            author=self.name,
            invocation_id=ctx.invocation_id,
            content=types.Content(
                role="model", parts=[types.Part(text="KB-104 explains the logout loop.")]
            ),
        )
```

**Line by line:**

- `LEG_SECONDS = 1.2` is a stand-in, and the comment says so. The number is not a measurement of
  Gemini; it is a shape — a leg that is not instant, three of them, against a caller expecting one
  database read. Sleeping rather than generating is what keeps this part at zero cost.
- `LEGS = 3` matches [4.1](../04-what-a-call-costs/4.1-the-kettle-and-the-car.md)'s count for the
  desk's real answer: decide, read the ticket back, read the article back.
- `IMPATIENT_TIMEOUT = timedelta(seconds=2)` is the caller's number, and the comment names where it
  came from. Two is not careless — it is generous for the tools the caller was looking at when they
  wrote it down.
- `timedelta` rather than a float, because that is what `read_timeout_seconds` on the SDK's client
  session takes. Passing a bare number raises, and the type is the SDK telling you this is a
  duration rather than a count.
- `partial=True` on the first three events is what makes them non-final, so they follow
  [2.2](../02-what-crosses-the-counter/2.2-the-interpreter-who-relays-the-last-sentence.md)'s
  narration path instead of the reply path. The final event has no `partial`, so
  `is_final_response()` is true for it and only it.
- `legs_completed` is a module global updated after each sleep. It is the whole measurement: it says
  how much work the server had finished at the moment the caller stopped listening.
- Nothing in `_run_async_impl` checks whether anyone is still there, and **there is nothing it could
  check**. The agent is not handed the MCP context, and even if it were, an MCP request carries no
  deadline.

The caller's side, where the number lives:

```python
async with create_connected_server_and_client_session(
    server, read_timeout_seconds=timeout
) as client:
    try:
        result = await client.call_tool("sutra_desk", {"request": "why am I logged out?"})
        print(f"caller sees       : {result.content[0].text}")
    except Exception as exc:
        print(f"caller sees       : {type(exc).__module__}.{type(exc).__name__}: {exc}")
```

**Line by line:**

- `read_timeout_seconds=timeout` is the parameter, and it belongs to the **client session**, not to
  the call. One number covers every request on this connection, so a caller cannot be patient with
  your agent and impatient with a lookup on the same connection — which is another reason
  [1.4](../01-the-back-office/1.4-the-counter-by-the-side-door.md) put the two servers on two
  endpoints.
- `except Exception` rather than the specific class, deliberately, so the script *prints* the fully
  qualified name instead of the document asserting it. The output below is the answer to "what
  exception is this", measured rather than remembered.
- `result.content[0].text` on the success path assumes a non-empty content list whose first block is
  text. Day 34's
  [3.3](../../../day-34-building-sutra-mcp-tools/parts/03-the-two-calls/3.3-one-call-one-result.md)
  flagged that assumption; it holds here because the agent's final event is a single text part.

## When it breaks

The impatient arm, measured on 2026-09-05:

```text
arm: default
caller timeout    : 2.0s
work the agent has: 3 legs at 1.2s each
caller sees       : mcp.shared.exceptions.McpError: Timed out while waiting for response to ClientRequest. Waited 2.0 seconds.
legs the server completed before the caller was told: 1
```

Two things in that output. The error the caller gets is `mcp.shared.exceptions.McpError`, and its
message is about **the caller's own waiting** — *"Timed out while waiting for response"*. It says
nothing about the server, because the caller knows nothing about the server. And the last line is the
server's side of the same moment: one leg done, two to go, still running.

The ablation is one number on the caller's side, with the agent and its work untouched:

```text
arm: --patient
caller timeout    : 10.0s
work the agent has: 3 legs at 1.2s each
caller sees       : KB-104 explains the logout loop.
legs the server completed before the caller was told: 3
```

Same server, same agent, same three legs. The only thing that changed is a number in somebody else's
code, and it is the difference between an answer and an error.

💡 **The insight:** **the caller's timeout is a fact about the caller, and the cost of getting it
wrong lands on you.** They see an error and retry. You see two full answers produced and one delivered
— or, on the free tier, six generations spent to deliver three. The place to fix it is not a longer
timeout on their side, which you cannot set; it is either making the work short enough that a normal
timeout covers it, or making the long work into something a caller can walk away from and come back
to, which is a task handle and is Day 36's whole subject.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead:** for anything that might exceed an ordinary timeout, the tool
returns a **handle** immediately and the work continues in the background, exactly as Day 36's
[3.1](../../../day-36-long-jobs-and-tasks/parts/03-the-handle/3.1-a-name-the-server-mints.md)
teaches. The caller gets a short reply, polls when it likes, and a timeout on the poll costs nothing
because the poll is cheap. That converts the abandoned-work problem into a storage problem, which is
a much better problem.

Failing that: a server-side budget. If the agent has not finished within a duration the server
chose, stop it, and return a partial answer or a refusal. That is worse for the caller and much
better for the quota, because at least the spending is bounded by a number you own.

**What degrades at scale:** duration variance. A model's response time is not constant, so the same
question is inside the caller's timeout on a quiet morning and outside it at four in the afternoon.
That produces intermittent failure — the hardest kind to diagnose and the kind most likely to be
"fixed" by adding a retry. Day 44's *tail at scale* is the paper for exactly this.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic:** the retry storm. A caller whose timeout is
slightly too short fails, retries, fails again, retries again. Each attempt runs the full agent to
completion on your side. Three attempts is three full answers produced and zero delivered, and on a
twenty-generation day that is nine generations for one question the caller never got.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"our served agent needs three model round-trips and
the client library's default timeout was chosen for one-hop tools. When it fires we keep running and
finish, so we pay for an answer that goes nowhere and they retry. Either return a task handle for
anything multi-hop, or put a server-side budget on the run so we stop spending when we know nobody is
listening. And say 'calls are slow' in the description — it is the only way they will find out before
they are surprised."*

**The interview question:** *"what happens when a client times out on a call to your agent?"* The
honest answer: *"On their side, an `McpError` saying they stopped waiting. On mine, nothing — the
request carries no deadline and nothing cancels the run, so the agent finishes, spends every model
call the answer needed, and writes the reply to a connection that has gone. I have measured that: one
leg done when the caller gave up and three legs done a moment later. So the cost of their wrong number
lands on my quota, and they retry, which doubles it. The real fix is not a longer timeout, because I
do not own that number. It is either a task handle so long work is something they can come back to,
or a server-side budget so the spending stops when nobody is listening."*

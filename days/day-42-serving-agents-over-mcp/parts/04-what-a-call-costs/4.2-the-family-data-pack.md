# 4.2 — The family data pack

## One-line answer

Forty calls a day at three generations each is a hundred and twenty generations against a ceiling of
twenty, so a served desk on the free tier answers **six** callers and refuses thirty-four — which
means the honest answer to "should Sutra serve its agent" is *not this agent, and not to everyone*.

## The story

The pack is one number for the whole house. Everybody's phone draws from it and nobody's phone shows
whose share is whose.

For the first ten days it is invisible. Then somebody starts watching videos in the evening, and on
the eighteenth the number runs out. Now the consequences arrive for people who had nothing to do with
it. Your mother cannot open the banking app. Your brother cannot send the photograph the office asked
for. The person who used it is not the person who feels it, and none of them can tell, from their own
phone, that anything is wrong until the thing they are doing simply stops.

The argument that follows is never really about videos. It is about the fact that there was one
number, four people, and no way for any of them to see it.

## The idea in plain language

You have a per-day ceiling and you are about to let strangers draw on it. Three numbers decide
whether that works:

| Number | Sutra's value | Where it comes from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| generations available per day | 20 | measured off a live 429, `docs/PACKAGES.md` |
| generations per served call | 1 + tool round-trips | [4.1](4.1-the-kettle-and-the-car.md) |
| calls per day the callers will make | whatever they feel like | **not under your control** |

The third row is the one that changes the shape of the problem. Before today, the number of calls per
day was decided by you, running things on purpose. After today it is decided by other people's
models, and a model does not get bored, does not consider whether the question is worth asking, and
will happily call a tool eleven times refining a phrasing.

The multiplication is the whole part:

```text
generations demanded = calls per day x generations per call
```

Forty support questions a day is not an ambitious support desk. Three generations each is what the
desk actually costs. That is a hundred and twenty against twenty.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because this is the arithmetic that turns [3.3](../03-tool-or-peer/3.3-the-guillotine-and-the-press.md)'s
verdict from a preference into a constraint.

The security argument already said: serve something narrow. The cost argument says the same thing in
a different currency, and it says it harder. A narrow answerer with one tool round-trip costs two
generations, so twenty a day buys ten calls. That is still small. But it is *ten calls of something
useful* rather than *six calls of something that also cannot be secured*, and the difference between
ten and six matters when the number is this small.

It also decides something you cannot decide later: **whether the served endpoint gets a limit in
code**. A server that will be exhausted by six calls needs one. Adding it after the first outage is
an outage you have already had.

## The mechanism

The second half of `lab/price.py` runs the multiplication for a day of real traffic:

```python
# What a support desk actually receives in a day, once three teams have the server.
CALLS_PER_DAY = 40

typical = generations_per_call(2, tools_only=tools_only)
demand = CALLS_PER_DAY * typical
print(f"a day of real traffic: {CALLS_PER_DAY} calls at {typical} generations each")
print(f"generations demanded : {demand}")
print(f"generations available: {DAILY_GENERATIONS}")
if demand > DAILY_GENERATIONS:
    served = DAILY_GENERATIONS // typical
    print(f"calls answered       : {served}")
    print(f"calls answered with 429: {CALLS_PER_DAY - served}")
```

**Line by line:**

- `CALLS_PER_DAY = 40` is a modest guess and the comment says where it comes from. Three teams, each
  asking the desk a dozen or so questions in a working day, is a *small* success. The argument gets
  stronger, not weaker, if you think forty is too low.
- `generations_per_call(2, ...)` uses two round-trips rather than three, so the typical call is priced
  at three generations rather than four. This is deliberately the kinder number:
  [4.1](4.1-the-kettle-and-the-car.md) said the desk's real answer is three round-trips.
- `demand > DAILY_GENERATIONS` is the branch that matters and it is a comparison of two integers.
  There is no modelling, no distribution, no simulation. The whole finding is one multiplication and
  one comparison, which is exactly why it is believable.
- `DAILY_GENERATIONS // typical` is integer division, so it floors. Nineteen generations with a
  three-generation call answers six calls and has one left, which buys nothing. Floor is the honest
  operator here: a partial call is a 429 in the middle of somebody's answer.
- `CALLS_PER_DAY - served` is the refusal count, printed as its own line rather than left for the
  reader to subtract. A number somebody has to compute is a number nobody reads.
- The whole script imports nothing beyond `sys`. It costs zero generations to run, which is the point
  of doing the arithmetic before the experiment rather than after.

Run it and read the last four lines:

```text
a day of real traffic: 40 calls at 3 generations each
generations demanded : 120
generations available: 20
calls answered       : 6
calls answered with 429: 34
```

And the ablation — identical traffic, Day 34's plain tools behind it:

```text
a day of real traffic: 40 calls at 0 generations each
generations demanded : 0
generations available: 20
calls answered       : 40
calls answered with 429: 0
```

**Forty out of forty against six out of forty**, from the same traffic, on the same free tier, with
the only difference being what sits behind the tool name.

## When it breaks

The break is which six get answered, and it is worse than the number suggests.

🎬 **The scene:** the server has been up since morning. Three teams have it mounted. It is early
afternoon.

😬 **The naive fix:** nothing — there is no limit, because six calls sounded like a problem for
later.

💥 **Why it fails:** the six calls that got answered were the six that arrived first, which is not the
same as the six that mattered. There is no priority, no reservation and no notion of a caller's fair
share, because `to_mcp_server` discards caller identity at the door
([1.3](../01-the-back-office/1.3-the-hall-that-came-with-its-own-chairs.md)) and there is nothing to
apportion by. And it is not thirty-four callers who get a clean refusal — some of them get a 429 in
the middle of a multi-turn answer, after the agent has already spent two generations, so the quota
was consumed *and* nothing useful was produced.

Then there is you. You go to run the next day's work and get:

```text
429 RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED. {'error': {'code': 429, 'message': 'You exceeded your current quota,
please check your plan and billing details.', 'status': 'RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED', ...}}
```

Nothing in that message says a stranger's agent spent it. Nothing in your own repository changed.
Day 24's
[2.2](../../../day-24-token-accounting-and-budgets/parts/02-two-ceilings/2.2-reading-the-ceiling-off-a-refusal.md)
taught you to read the ceiling off a refusal; what it cannot tell you is who else was drawing on it.

💡 **The insight:** **a shared quota with no accounting is a shared quota with no owner.** The fix is
not a bigger number — there is no bigger number on a free tier. It is a limit that refuses cheaply,
before a generation is spent, and a counter that says who spent what. Both live on your side, because
there is nowhere else for them to live.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead:** a token bucket in front of the runner, checked before the
first generation, with the refusal returned as a normal result carrying a `retry after` hint rather
than as an exception. That converts "we ran out and everything broke" into "we are at capacity and
said so", which is a completely different conversation with a caller. The bucket is per connection
where there is no better key, and per authenticated caller where there is.

**What degrades at scale:** the fairness. One bucket for the whole server means the loudest caller
sets everyone else's experience, and the loudest caller is usually a retry loop rather than a person.
Day 44 hardens Sutra's client against slow servers; the mirror of that on the server side is a limit
that treats a retrying client as one caller rather than as three.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic:** the burst. A daily ceiling is not the only
ceiling — there is a per-minute one too, and Day 24's
[2.1](../../../day-24-token-accounting-and-budgets/parts/02-two-ceilings/2.1-two-ceilings-one-clears.md)
established that the per-minute one clears and the per-day one does not. Two callers asking at the
same moment can trip the per-minute limit while the daily budget is barely touched, which produces a
refusal that looks like exhaustion and is not, and a retry policy tuned for the wrong one makes it
worse.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"the arithmetic says this endpoint is exhausted by
six calls. That is not a reason not to ship it, but it is a reason to ship a limit with it. Put a
counter in front of the runner, refuse with a sentence and a retry hint instead of letting the
provider's 429 escape, and reserve some of the daily budget for our own work — because the first
symptom of this going wrong is our own build failing for a reason that is not in our repository."*

**The interview question:** *"you are on a free tier with twenty model calls a day and someone wants
to call your agent over MCP. What do you tell them?"* The honest answer: *"I tell them the number,
because they cannot see it. Forty calls a day at three generations each is a hundred and twenty
against twenty, so we answer six and refuse thirty-four, and the six are whoever was first rather
than whoever mattered. So three things: serve a narrower agent so a call costs two instead of four, put
a counter in front of the runner that refuses cheaply with a retry hint rather than letting the
provider's 429 escape mid-answer, and reserve part of the budget for our own work. And I would put
the number in the tool description, because a caller's model has no other way to learn that this tool
is not free to hammer."*

# 3.2 — A note handed over, and money sent

## One-line answer

Four things separate a call inside your process from a call across a boundary — how long it takes,
what memory it can touch, what happens when it half-fails, and what happens when two arrive at once —
and no amount of identical-looking syntax makes any of the four go away.

## The story

You owe a friend two thousand rupees and you are standing next to him, so you take the notes out of
your wallet and put them in his hand.

Watch how few states that has. Before, the money is yours. After, it is his. There is no in-between,
nothing to check afterwards, and no version of the afternoon where you both believe you are holding
it. If your wallet is empty you find out instantly, standing there, and nothing has moved.

Now he is in another city, and you send it through the app.

The screen says *Processing*. That word does not exist in the wallet version. It sits there for a
while and then the app loses its connection and shows you a red exclamation mark and the sentence
*Could not confirm*. And now you are in a state that has no equivalent in the first story at all:
**the money may or may not have gone, and there is no way to know from where you are standing.** If
you send it again you may send it twice. If you do not, you may not have paid him. Either way, the
only thing that resolves it is asking somebody else — the bank, the app, your friend.

You typed the same amount into the same phone for the same person. The two are not the same act.

## The idea in plain language

This is the oldest argument in distributed systems and it is still the one people get wrong, because
**the syntax lies**. In Python, `answer = desk.ask(question)` and `answer = remote_desk.ask(question)`
look identical. One of them cannot fail in the middle. The other can, routinely.

Four differences, and they are not degrees of the same thing:

**Latency.** An in-process call costs nanoseconds. A call over a network costs milliseconds at best,
and a call to a served agent costs however long a model takes to think — which is not a fixed number
and depends on somebody else's load.

**Memory access.** An in-process call can be handed a reference to an object and mutate it. Nothing
crosses a boundary by reference. Everything is copied, serialised, and re-created on the other side,
which is why [2.2](../02-what-crosses-the-counter/2.2-the-interpreter-who-relays-the-last-sentence.md)
had to talk about base64 and dropped parts at all.

**Partial failure.** A local call has two outcomes: it returns, or it raises. A remote call has
**three**: it returned, it definitely did not happen, and *you do not know*. That third outcome has no
local equivalent, and it is the one that produces the wrong answer rather than the error.

**Concurrency.** A local call runs when you call it, in your thread. A remote service is being called
by other people at the same time, and it has one quota, one connection pool and one rate limit
shared between all of you.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because this day is that argument, performed. You take an agent that has only ever been called in
Sutra's own process and put a process boundary in front of it, in one line, with no change to the
agent at all.

That one line is a promise of transparency, and the promise is false in exactly four ways, each of
which gets its own part later today:

| The difference | Where you meet it today |
| --- | --- |
| Latency | [5.2](../05-failure-lab/5.2-you-knock-and-walk-away.md) — a caller's timeout against three model round-trips |
| Memory access | [2.2](../02-what-crosses-the-counter/2.2-the-interpreter-who-relays-the-last-sentence.md) — parts that do not survive conversion, and [2.3](../02-what-crosses-the-counter/2.3-the-table-you-may-leave-your-books-on.md) — a session the caller cannot see |

| Concurrency | [4.2](../04-what-a-call-costs/4.2-the-family-data-pack.md) — forty callers, one daily quota |

And it decides the day's central question. Section 3.1 gave you *use* versus *talk to*. The reason
that choice is not cosmetic is that the four differences bite differently in each shape: a tool call
has no way to express "I do not know whether it happened", and a peer's task, with a status you can
poll, exists precisely to give that third outcome somewhere to live.

## The paper behind it

> **A note on distributed computing** · `doi:10.1007/3-540-62852-5_6` · 1997 ·
> `https://doi.org/10.1007/3-540-62852-5_6`

The claim: a local object call and a remote object call differ **in kind, not in degree**, because of
latency, memory access, partial failure and concurrency — so a programming model that hides the
difference behind identical syntax produces systems that work in development and fall over on a real
network.

Read it after the parts: [`../../papers/01-a-note-on-distributed-computing.md`](../../papers/01-a-note-on-distributed-computing.md).

## The mechanism

The mechanism is the third outcome, and the smallest way to see it is a function called two ways.

```python
def call_local(part: str, count: int) -> int:
    """Reserve in this process. Raises ValueError, or returns. There is no third case."""
    import warehouse

    return warehouse.reserve(part, count)


def call_remote(part: str, count: int) -> int:
    """Reserve in the other process. Raises CallFailed, ValueError, or returns."""
    with socket.create_connection(("127.0.0.1", PORT), timeout=5) as sock:
        sock.sendall(f"reserve {part} {count}\n".encode("utf-8"))
        reply = sock.recv(4096).decode("utf-8").strip()
    if not reply:
        raise CallFailed("connection closed before a reply arrived")
    status, _, rest = reply.partition(" ")
    if status == "err":
        raise ValueError(rest)
    return int(rest)
```

**Line by line:**

- The two signatures are identical: `(str, int) -> int`. A type checker sees no difference, an
  autocomplete popup sees no difference, and a caller reading the call site sees no difference. That
  identical appearance is the thing the paper is warning about.
- `call_local` has two exits, and the docstring says so. It returns, or `reserve` raises
  `ValueError`. There is no code path on which the shelf moved and the caller does not know.
- `call_remote` has **three**. `CallFailed` is the new one, and it is not an error the remote function
  raised — it is the absence of an answer. The work may have been done.
- `if not reply: raise CallFailed(...)` is where the third outcome is manufactured. `recv` returning
  an empty bytes object means the peer closed the connection. It does not mean the request was not
  processed, and nothing in the socket API distinguishes the two.
- `timeout=5` is on the connection, not on the work. It bounds how long you wait; it says nothing
  about what happened at the other end when you stop waiting. Part
  [5.2](../05-failure-lab/5.2-you-knock-and-walk-away.md) is that distinction with a served agent in
  the middle.
- `import warehouse` inside the function rather than at the top, so the local arm genuinely shares
  the module the remote arm's server process is running. One implementation, two callers — the whole
  point.

Now map it onto today. Sutra's served agent has exactly the same three outcomes, wearing MCP clothes:

| Outcome | What the caller sees | Did the agent run? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Answered | a result with content blocks, `isError: false` | yes, and it finished |
| The tool said no | a result with `isError: true` and a sentence | yes, and it decided it could not |
| No answer arrived | an `McpError` timeout, or a dead connection | **unknown** |

Row three is the one Day 34 could gesture at and this day has to take seriously, because the work in
question is not a dictionary read. It is a model loop that has already spent your quota and may
already have called a tool.

## When it breaks

The break is the retry, and it is the most natural thing in the world to write.

🎬 **The scene:** a caller's client wraps every `tools/call` in a retry, because that is what you do
with a network call, and Day 44 is going to teach you to do exactly that.

😬 **The naive fix:** on any exception, call it again.

💥 **Why it fails:** the first call may have completed. If the served agent's reach includes anything
that writes — and [2.4](../02-what-crosses-the-counter/2.4-the-errand-you-sent-someone-on.md) showed
how quietly that happens — the retry performs the write twice. Day 38's
[3.2](../../../day-38-failure-and-migration-lab/parts/03-the-quiet-ones/3.2-the-ticket-closed-twice.md)
is the same failure met from the client side. And even when nothing writes, the retry spends a second
set of generations against a daily ceiling of twenty, so a caller retrying three times has consumed
fifteen per cent of your day on one question they will only read once.

💡 **The insight:** **"did it happen" is a question the caller cannot answer and the protocol does not
carry.** The only fix is on your side of the line: make the operation idempotent, or make the third
outcome impossible by serving only things that have no effect to repeat. Sutra takes the second
route, and [3.3](3.3-the-guillotine-and-the-press.md) is where that decision gets made and written
down.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead:** they name the third outcome in the interface. Either the
served operation is read-only, so a repeat is free and the question stops mattering, or the request
carries an idempotency key the server remembers, so a repeat is recognised rather than re-executed.
Day 36's [3.3](../../../day-36-long-jobs-and-tasks/parts/03-the-handle/3.3-sutras-handle-policy.md)
is Sutra's rule for what such a key may look like. What nobody sane does is leave a
possibly-executed write behind a retryable call and hope.

**What degrades at scale:** the probability. At ten calls a day the third outcome is a story you tell
in a design review. At ten thousand, some fraction of calls end in it every single day, and that
fraction is a function of somebody else's network rather than of your code quality. Day 44's *tail at
scale* is that arithmetic in full.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic:** the concurrency leg. Two callers hitting a
served agent simultaneously do not queue politely — they share the quota, the session map's
get-then-create window from
[2.3](../02-what-crosses-the-counter/2.3-the-table-you-may-leave-your-books-on.md), and the
provider's per-minute rate limit. Every one of those is invisible at a call rate of one.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"the client wraps this in a three-attempt retry and
the served agent can close tickets. Those two facts are in different repositories, so nobody is going
to notice them together until a ticket gets closed three times. Either the served agent goes
read-only or the request carries an idempotency key we honour — and write down which, because the
next person to add a tool to that agent needs to know the rule."*

**The interview question:** *"is a remote call just a slow local call?"* The honest answer: *"No, and
the difference that actually costs money is partial failure. A local call returns or raises — two
outcomes. A remote call has a third: no answer arrived, and you cannot tell whether the work
happened. There is no bit on the wire for that, so the caller has to guess, and the natural guess is
to retry. That is fine for a read and a disaster for a write. The other three differences — latency,
no shared memory, and concurrency you do not control — are real too, but they announce themselves.
Partial failure is the one that produces a wrong answer instead of an error, so the first thing I ask
about any remote interface is whether repeating a call is safe, and if it is not, what the key is."*

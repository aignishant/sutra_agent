# 1.4 — What green does not mean

## One-line answer

A green gate says *"every automatic check we chose to run passed on this machine, just now"* — it
says nothing about whether the agent still answers correctly, whether a sourced skill is safe,
whether the free tier still exists, or whether the world outside the repository moved, and knowing
those four edges precisely is what separates a gate from a comfort blanket.

## The story

A roadworthiness certificate is a genuinely useful piece of paper and it does not say the car is
good.

The tester checks the brakes, the lights, the tyres, the emissions, the structure. He passes it. What
he has told you is that on the morning he looked, a specific list of things were within a specific
tolerance. He has not told you the clutch will survive the winter. He has not told you the car is the
right car for a family of five. He has not told you the seller is honest about the mileage. And he
has certainly not told you that it will still pass tomorrow — a tyre can be legal on Tuesday and bald
by March.

People who buy cars badly are not the ones who ignore the certificate. They are the ones who think
the certificate is the whole answer, so they stop asking the other questions. The certificate did
nothing wrong. It answered the question it was asked, exactly, and somebody else expanded the answer
on the way home.

## The idea in plain language

Every check has a **scope**: the set of claims it is entitled to make. A gate's value comes from two
things, and most people only count the first.

The first is what it checks. The second is that **you know precisely what it does not check**,
because that is what tells you which other rituals you still owe.

`./m check` — with today's two additions, eight stages — makes exactly this claim: *the code passes
the rules we selected, the formatting matches, the offline tests pass, the day documents satisfy the
structural contract, the traceability and index ledgers are current, the skills on the shelf are
spec-shaped, and no OpenRouter model string is missing its `:free` suffix.* That is a lot. It is also
a completely enumerable list, and everything outside it is uncovered.

There are four gaps, and each one has a different owner.

**Behaviour.** The gate runs `pytest -m "not live"`, which deselects every test that spends model
quota. So the gate never asks the model anything. Green does not mean the desk agent still routes a
refund request to the right skill; it means the code that would ask it still imports and its offline
tests pass. The owner of that gap is the deliberately-scheduled live suite, and the marker in the
command is what makes the gap visible on every single run.

**Safety.** The skills lint checks structure
([2.4](../02-a-lint-is-a-test/2.4-what-the-shelf-lint-refuses-to-judge.md)). Day 29's poisoned
fixture — a skill with three planted attacks in it — passes every structural rule there is, because
its frontmatter is valid and its files resolve. The owner of that gap is the five-pass audit and the
provenance ledger
([Day 29](../../../day-29-sourcing-and-auditing-skills/parts/02-the-five-pass-audit/2.1-read-it-like-the-thing-that-will-obey-it.md)).

**Freshness.** Nothing in the gate can see that `google-adk` shipped a new version this morning, that
the MCP specification revision moved, or that a model quietly left the free tier. All of that is
outside the machine. The owner of that gap is the phase-gate freshness ritual
([6.2](../06-the-phase-gate/6.2-the-freshness-check-that-fired.md)), and it is the reason a phase
gate is more than a command.

**Time.** A green run is green *now*, on *this* checkout, on *this* machine. It says nothing about
tomorrow, and it says nothing about a different machine with a different Python. `uv run` narrows
that last one hard by pinning the tools, which is why every stage in the gate goes through it.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because the alternative to knowing the edges is not caution — it is a slow, invisible expansion of
what people think green means.

You can watch it happen in this repository. Rows 16 to 22 of `docs/PROGRESS.md` say the gates were
green. They were not: `ruff` had been failing on `tests/test_persona.py` since Day 15
([5.1](../05-the-gate-that-lied/5.1-red-since-day-fifteen.md)). Nobody lied. Somebody ran `./m depth`
and `./m trace`, saw them pass, and wrote ✅ in a column that means something slightly larger. The
scope crept by one word and seven ledger rows were wrong.

There is also a direct build consequence today. You are about to add two stages, and both of them
have edges that are easy to over-claim. `lint_skills` will tempt you to say "the skills are fine".
`lint_free_suffix` will tempt you to say "we cannot be billed". Neither sentence is true, and
[2.4](../02-a-lint-is-a-test/2.4-what-the-shelf-lint-refuses-to-judge.md) and
[3.3](../03-the-free-suffix-lint/3.3-the-alarm-that-fires-on-toast.md) are the two parts that pin
down what they actually say.

## The paper behind it

**End-to-end arguments in system design** · `doi:10.1145/357401.357402` · 1984 ·
`https://doi.org/10.1145/357401.357402`

The claim, in one sentence: a function can only be completely and correctly implemented with the
knowledge that sits at the endpoints of a system, so a check placed at a lower layer can be a useful
optimisation but never a guarantee — the endpoint still has to check.

That is the shape of this part. `ruff` guarantees nothing about whether the agent answers correctly;
it guarantees a property of the source text. The live suite is closer to the endpoint. The human
reading a ticket reply is the endpoint. Every layer below is an optimisation that makes failure
cheaper to find, and none of them removes the endpoint's obligation. Taught in full on Day 21:
[../../../day-21-errors-surface-not-swallow/papers/01-end-to-end-arguments.md](../../../day-21-errors-surface-not-swallow/papers/01-end-to-end-arguments.md).

## The mechanism

The scope of the gate, written out as a table you can hand somebody. This is the artefact — not the
code, the table. A gate whose edges are not written down has edges that are argued about.

| The gate checks | The gate does not check | Who owns the gap |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the code passes `ruff`'s selected rules | that the code is a good design | code review |
| the formatting matches | that the names mean anything | code review |
| offline tests pass | that the model still answers correctly | the `live`-marked suite, run deliberately |
| day documents satisfy §17 | that the explanations are any good | reading, and plan §17.8 |
| every curriculum ID is accounted for | that the day taught it well | the checklist and your own recall |
| skills are spec-shaped | that a skill is safe to obey | the Day 29 audit and the provenance ledger |
| model strings end in `:free` | that the model is still free | the phase-gate freshness re-check |
| the ledgers are regenerated | that the rows in them are honest | you, writing ⚠️ when it is ⚠️ |

The right-hand column is the useful one, and the reason it is useful is that every row of it has an
owner. A gap with no owner is not a gap, it is a hole.

You can make one of these edges visible on every run, and it costs nothing. `pytest` already reports
how many tests it deselected:

```bash
uv run python -m pytest -m "not live" 2>&1 | tail -3
```

**Line by line:**

- `uv run python -m pytest` runs the suite through the project's pinned `pytest`.
- `-m "not live"` is the marker expression — the same one the gate uses. `live` is declared in
  `pyproject.toml` as *"hits a real API, model or database - skipped by default, never run in CI"*.
- No `-q` here, deliberately: quiet mode hides the deselection count, and the count is the thing you
  are looking for.
- `2>&1` merges the error stream into the output stream so `tail` sees everything.
- `| tail -3` shows the summary line. Piping is safe in an interactive shell; inside the gate it
  would need `pipefail` to be honest, which is
  [5.3](../05-the-gate-that-lied/5.3-the-swallowed-exit-code.md)'s subject.
- The number after `deselected` is the size of the behaviour gap, printed by the gate on every run.
  That is the good pattern: an edge you can read is an edge that does not creep.

## When it breaks

**The ✅ that meant something smaller.** This repository's own case, and the error text is real:

```text
I001 [*] Import block is un-sorted or un-formatted
  --> tests\test_persona.py:7:1
Found 1 error.
```

Seven ledger rows said the gates were green while that error was returning 1 from stage one of the
gate. The checks that were actually run — `./m depth`, `./m trace` — were green, and "green" silently
came to mean "the checks I ran".

**A skill passes the lint and drains your ticket history.** Day 29's `evil-helper` fixture has valid
frontmatter, a name matching its folder, a licence, and resolvable references. It passes every
structural rule. It also contains a line instructing the agent to append the full ticket history to
its reply, which is an exfiltration channel written in English. `lint_skills` reports nothing, because
nothing structural is wrong. The lint is not broken. It was never asked that question.

**The model string is perfect and the model is not free.** `openrouter/some-vendor/some-model:free`
passes the suffix lint on the day the vendor removes that model from the free roster. The suffix is a
*syntactic* invariant, and the free tier is a *fact about the world* that changes without touching
your repository. Day 9 watched a model named in an addendum vanish from the roster between the
addendum being written and the day being run.

**Green on your machine, red in CI.** Usually a file that is committed on one side and not the other,
or a path separator, or a locale. The tell is that the failure names a file you do not recognise.
`uv run` on every stage removes the biggest cause — a differently-versioned tool — but not this one.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead.** The scope table above lives in the repository, next to the
gate, and it is updated in the same commit that adds a stage. In a company that usually means a short
`CONTRIBUTING` section titled *what CI does and does not tell you*, and it exists because the
question *"CI is green, can we ship?"* gets asked by people who did not build the gate and have no
way to know its edges.

**What degrades at scale.** Scope creep is proportional to the number of people. With one engineer,
the edges live in their head accurately. With twenty, "the build is green" becomes a sentence people
say to each other, and its meaning drifts until somebody ships a regression that every check
correctly ignored. The countermeasure that works is not documentation alone — it is making the gap
*visible in the output*, like the deselection count above, so the edge is reprinted on every single
run and nobody has to remember it.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic** is the one the paper predicts: a check at a
lower layer that is *treated* as an end-to-end guarantee. A schema validator on an incoming request
does not guarantee the request means what you think; a green test suite does not guarantee the
feature works for the user. Systems that survive keep an endpoint check — a smoke test against the
real thing, a canary, a human — precisely because everything below it is an optimisation.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"The PR description says 'gate is green so the
skill is safe'. The gate checks that the skill is spec-shaped. Safety is the audit and the provenance
row, and I can't find either. Please link the audit or say it hasn't been done."*

**The interview question:** *"Your quality gate is green. What do you explicitly know it has not told
you?"* The honest spoken answer: *"Four things, and I'd rather be able to list them than claim
broader coverage. Behaviour: the gate runs the offline suite only — everything that spends model
quota is marked and deselected, so green means the code imports and the deterministic tests pass, not
that the agent still routes correctly. Safety: our skills lint checks structure, and we have a
deliberately poisoned fixture that passes it — trust comes from the audit and the provenance ledger,
not the gate. Freshness: the gate can't see that a dependency shipped a breaking version or that a
model left the free tier, so that's a separate ritual at each phase boundary. And time: green means
green now, on this checkout. The reason I can list those is that we keep a scope table next to the
gate with an owner for every gap — a gap with no owner is a hole."*

```bash
uv run python -m pytest -m "not live" 2>&1 | tail -3
```

number is measuring and which ritual owns it.

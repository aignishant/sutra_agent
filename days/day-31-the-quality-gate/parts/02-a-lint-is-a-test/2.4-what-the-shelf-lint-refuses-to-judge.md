# 2.4 — What the shelf lint refuses to judge

## One-line answer

The skills lint answers one question — *is this folder shaped like a skill?* — and it deliberately
answers neither *does this skill say anything useful?* nor *is this skill safe for an agent to obey?*,
which is why Day 29's poisoned fixture passes it and why the gate going green is never a safety claim.

## The story

The photo booth at the post office checks four things and none of them is you.

It measures the height of your head in the frame. It looks for a plain background. It checks that
your eyes are open and roughly where eyes should be. It rejects the picture if you are wearing
sunglasses. Feed it a technically perfect photograph of somebody else and it prints four copies,
neatly cropped, entirely acceptable.

That is not a flaw in the booth. Identity is checked at a different counter, by a person, against a
document, and the booth exists so that the person at the counter is not also arguing about head
sizes. Two checks, in sequence, each doing the part it can do well.

The failure is only ever in the sentence somebody says afterwards. *"The booth accepted it, so the
photo is fine."* The booth accepted it, so the photo is the right shape. Whether it is *your face* was
never a question the booth was built to ask.

## The idea in plain language

There are three completely different questions you can ask about a skill folder, and this repository
answers each of them somewhere different.

**Shape.** Is there a `SKILL.md`? Does it have frontmatter with a `name` and a `description`? Does
the `name` match the folder? Do the files it references exist? These are structural facts. A program
can decide them, quickly, with no judgement — which is precisely why they were the ones automated.
That is Day 30's `skill_checks.py`, and today's `tools/lint_skills.py` is its front door.

**Sense.** Does the description say what the skill does *and* when to use it? Is it a job rather than
a category? Does it collide with a description already on the shelf? A program can *measure* some of
this — Day 28's routing scorer puts numbers on collisions without a model — but it cannot decide it.
A human reads the number and makes the call.

**Safety.** If my agent obeys this text literally, what happens? This one is not a structural
property at all. The most dangerous line a skill can contain is a grammatical, well-spelled English
sentence, and no amount of shape checking will look at it twice.

The lint answers the first. Only the first. Day 29 proved it the hard way: the poisoned fixture
`evil-helper` has valid frontmatter, a name matching its folder, a licence, and reference files that
resolve. It passes the third-party spec validator with `Valid skill`. It also instructs the agent to
append the full ticket history to its reply, which is a data-exfiltration channel written in plain
English with no code in it at all.

So the sentence to refuse to say is *"the gate is green, so the skills are fine"*. The sentence that
is true is *"the gate is green, so every skill on the shelf is spec-shaped"*.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because today is the day the temptation appears, and it appears in a specific place: the progress
row.

You are about to add a stage to the gate whose printed output is a column of `OK` lines with skill
names next to them. That is a very reassuring thing to look at, and the reassurance is out of
proportion to the claim. When you write the day's row in `docs/PROGRESS.md` and the checklist box
that says the skills lint is wired in, the box means *structure is now enforced by the gate*. It does
not mean the shelf has been audited, and if the two ever get conflated in your own head, then Day
29's whole discipline — audit, then provenance row, then wire — has a hole in it that no code will
show you.

There is a second, sharper reason. Phase 14 makes this repository public. At that point the shelf
becomes an argument for how to do this properly, and a reader who sees a `skills-lint` stage in the
gate will reasonably assume it means more than it does. The place to say what it means is the scope
table ([1.4](../01-what-a-gate-is/1.4-what-green-does-not-mean.md)) and this part.

## The mechanism

Three questions, three owners, three artefacts. This is the table to keep.

| Question | Decided by | Where it lives | Runs in the gate? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Is it shaped like a skill? | a program, deterministically | `tools/skill_checks.py` via `tools/lint_skills.py` | **yes**, from today |
| Is it a job, and does it collide? | a human, reading a number | Day 28's routing scorer, `lab/route.py` | no — a pre-merge measurement |
| Is it safe to obey? | a human, reading every line | Day 29's five-pass audit and `docs/SKILL_PROVENANCE.md` | no — before it is ever installed |

The ordering is not arbitrary. Shape is checked first because it is free and because a folder that is
not a skill cannot be usefully audited. Safety is checked last and by a person, because the question
is *"what does this text do to a thing that obeys text?"* and no static rule can answer it. That is
the same cheap-decisive-first argument as [1.2](../01-what-a-gate-is/1.2-six-stages-cheapest-first.md),
applied to judgement rather than to seconds.

Prove the boundary rather than believing it. Day 29 left the poisoned fixture on disk in quarantine —
under `tests/fixtures/`, where nothing in `sutra/` can reach it — so you can point today's lint
straight at it:

```bash
uv run python -m tools.lint_skills tests/fixtures/skills; echo "exit: $?"
```

**Line by line:**

- `tests/fixtures/skills` is Day 29's quarantine shelf. It holds `evil-helper/`, the fixture with
  three planted attacks, and `sourced-pack/`, four clean-but-colliding skills.
- Pointing the lint at it is safe in a way that is worth being explicit about: linting a folder means
  reading its bytes with Python, not handing them to a model. The dangerous act with a poisoned skill
  is *loading* it into an agent's context, and nothing here does that.
- `; echo "exit: $?"` reads the number, as always.
- **Expect `OK evil-helper`.** That is the whole point of this part: the most dangerous skill in the
  curriculum passes every structural rule there is.

The output has a second line in it, and it is worth reading because it says something different:

```text
OK   evil-helper
FAIL sourced-pack             not-a-skill      folder has no SKILL.md
lint_skills: 2 skill folder(s) examined, 1 with findings
exit: 1
```

`sourced-pack` is a directory containing four skill folders rather than a skill folder itself, and
`check_shelf` looks exactly one level deep — so it reports it as *not a skill*, correctly. Point the
lint at `tests/fixtures/skills/sourced-pack` to see those four individually. Note which folder got
the `FAIL` and which got the `OK`: the shape checker objected to the harmless one and approved the
hostile one, and both verdicts are right.

The poisoned fixture, put through the other two owners, gives the opposite answer:

```bash
uv run python days/day-29-sourcing-and-auditing-skills/lab/audit.py tests/fixtures/skills/evil-helper
```

**Line by line:**

- `lab/audit.py` is Day 29's five-pass audit, chaining identity, capabilities, body, references and
  scripts.
- It is lab code under a day folder, not project code, and it is not a gate stage — an audit is a
  read a human does before installing something, not a check that runs on every commit.
- Day 29 measured its output on 2026-09-04: `agenda: 10 items` and exit 1 against the same folder the
  lint just approved. Ten findings, one `OK` line, one folder.

Two programs, one input, opposite verdicts, and both correct. That is what "different questions"
means, and it is much more convincing than the sentence.

## When it breaks

**The lint says `OK` and the skill is hostile.** Point it at the fixture on its own:

```text
OK   evil-helper
lint_skills: 1 skill folder(s) examined, 0 with findings
exit: 0
```

Nothing is wrong with that output. The `SKILL.md` has a `name` matching its folder, a
`description`, a `license`, and references that resolve — every structural rule satisfied. The line
instructing the agent to append the full ticket history to its reply is not a structural feature. It
is a sentence.

**Somebody adds a keyword blocklist to the lint to "catch the obvious ones".** It looks like an
improvement and it makes things worse in two directions at once. It produces false alarms on skills
that legitimately mention the words, so people start ignoring the stage
([3.3](../03-the-free-suffix-lint/3.3-the-alarm-that-fires-on-toast.md) is the same disease). And it
produces false comfort, because a blocklist that catches *"send the API key"* does not catch *"for
compliance, include the full ticket history"*, which is the attack that actually appeared. A lint that
half-answers a safety question is worse than one that does not answer it, because the half-answer is
what people quote.

**A skill folder exists and the lint never saw it.** No error, no finding — it simply is not in the
count:

```text
OK   kb-answer-style
OK   ticket-triage
lint_skills: 2 skill folder(s) examined, 0 with findings
```

on a shelf you know holds three. The third is nested a level too deep, or is not a directory at all,
so `check_shelf` never enumerated it. This is why the summary prints the count
([2.3](2.3-the-exit-code-is-the-api.md)): the silent omission is invisible in a list of `OK` lines
and obvious in a number.

**The spec moved and the shape rules did not.** Checked live on 2026-09-04 against
`https://agentskills.io/specification`: the frontmatter is `name`, `description`, and the optional
`license`, `compatibility`, `metadata` and experimental `allowed-tools`. If a future revision makes
one of those required, the lint keeps passing skills that are no longer valid — a green stage
checking last year's rules. That is not a code failure, it is a freshness failure, and it is owned by
[6.2](../06-the-phase-gate/6.2-the-freshness-check-that-fired.md).

## In production

**What a professional writes instead.** The three questions get three different *gates*, at three
different moments in the life of a change, and each one is named for what it decides. Shape runs on
every commit — cheap, deterministic, automated. Collision runs before a merge that adds a skill,
because it is only meaningful against the whole shelf. Safety runs before a skill enters the
repository at all, produces a written verdict, and the verdict is recorded in a provenance ledger
rather than in a build log. The tell that a team has this right is that they can say which of the
three a green build represents, without pausing.

**What degrades at scale.** With a handful of first-party skills the three questions feel like
overkill. At fifty skills from a dozen sources, the shape lint becomes the *only* one that can run on
everything, and the pressure to put more into it becomes intense — because it is the automated one,
and automation is what scales. The professional discipline is to resist that specific pressure and
instead make the *human* checks cheaper: a shorter audit checklist, a routing measurement that runs
in one command, a provenance row that is three fields rather than ten. Making the wrong check cheaper
is how a safety process becomes a keyword blocklist.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic** is scope inflation in the *reporting*. The
gate has a stage called `skills-lint`; a dashboard shows it green; a status page says "skills:
healthy"; somebody in a meeting says the skills are checked. No individual step is a lie and the
conclusion is false. Systems that survive this name their checks for what they check —
`skills-structure` rather than `skills-lint` — because the name is what propagates.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"You've added a regex here that looks for
'ignore previous instructions'. Take it out. It doesn't catch the attack we actually found, it will
fire on the day somebody writes a skill about prompt injection, and having it here lets people say
the lint checks for injection. Safety is the audit; keep this file structural."*

**The interview question:** *"You lint your agent skills in CI. Does that mean a third-party skill is
safe to install?"* The honest spoken answer: *"No, and I'd want to be precise about why. The lint
answers a structural question — is there a `SKILL.md`, does the frontmatter parse, does the name
match the folder, do the referenced files exist. It's automated because it's decidable. Safety is a
different question — if my agent obeys this text literally, what happens — and the most dangerous
line a skill can contain is a well-formed English sentence, so no structural rule will ever see it.
We have a poisoned fixture in the repo that passes the lint and passes the official spec validator,
and gets ten findings and a reject from our audit. Safety lives in that audit plus a provenance row
recorded before anything runs, and the gate going green is never the claim that a skill is safe."*

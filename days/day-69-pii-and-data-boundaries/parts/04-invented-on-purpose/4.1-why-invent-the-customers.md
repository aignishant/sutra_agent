# 4.1 — Why invent the customers

## One-line answer

Generated data is not a weaker stand-in for real data; it is a different instrument with three
powers real traffic cannot give you at any price — you can **reproduce** it, you can **share** it,
and you can **put the rare case in on purpose** — and the privacy argument everybody reaches for
first is only the fourth.

## The story

The alarm goes off at work on a Tuesday morning and everybody walks out to the car park. Nobody is
in danger. Somebody chose the day, chose the hour and pressed the button.

This year the person who runs it does something new. She tapes a sheet of paper over the door of the
main stairwell that says **BLOCKED**, because in all the years the building has stood, nobody has
ever tested what happens when the nearest way out is the one you cannot use. Half the third floor
stands in the corridor not knowing what to do. Then somebody remembers the other stairs, and
everybody funnels into them, and the building empties much more slowly than last year.

Afterwards there is one useful sentence in the report: the second stairwell is too narrow for the
third floor. Nobody knew that. A real fire would have found it out too, and found it out
expensively.

The drill is not a fire. It is quieter, it is on a Tuesday, everybody has their coat, and the worst
thing that happens is that the tea goes cold. What it *is* instead is a fire you can hold still. You
can run it again next quarter under the same conditions. You can write the plan down and post it to
the new site. And you can decide, in advance and on paper, which door is shut.

## The idea in plain language

**Synthetic data** is data a program produced from rules somebody wrote, rather than data collected
from people who did things. In this day it means the ticket archive: not fourteen tickets fourteen
customers actually sent, but tickets a short script invented, from pools of invented surnames and
telephone numbers that are not allowed to ring.

Almost everybody meets this idea as a compromise. Real data is the good stuff, the reasoning goes,
and synthetic data is the sad substitute you accept when the lawyers say no. That reasoning gets the
sign wrong. Being safe to hold is a **constraint satisfied**, not a **capability gained**, and it is
the least interesting of the four things a generator buys you.

Here are the other three, which are the reason professionals generate fixtures even when they are
allowed to use production data. A **fixture**, throughout this day, means a fixed piece of data that
a test or a measurement runs against — the input you hold constant so that a change in the output
means a change in the code.

**You can reproduce it.** Run the generator twice with the same starting number and you get the same
archive, byte for byte, on your machine and on somebody else's and on a build server in six months.
A sample of real traffic is a sample: you took it on a Thursday, the traffic has since moved on, and
after the retention window has passed you cannot take it again even if you want to. A number
measured against a fixture nobody else can rebuild is an anecdote, and the person who quotes it back
at you cannot check it.

**You can share it.** A generated archive can be committed to the repository, attached to the issue,
pasted into the bug report, read by the reviewer, and run in a public continuous-integration job. A
production sample can do none of those things. This one property decides what a test suite is even
able to test, because a test can only assert against data the test is allowed to see.

**You can steer it.** The blocked stairwell is the whole point. A generator lets you decide that one
ticket in fifty contains a card fragment, that three contain a postcode, that one contains an
address inside a quoted reply — because you wrote the rule that puts them there. In real traffic the
rare case is rare, which means the sample you can afford to label by hand contains roughly none of
it, and the case you most need to test is the case you have the fewest examples of.

The safety argument then arrives on top of those three and is genuinely load-bearing here, for the
specific reason [3.3](../03-the-line-at-the-provider/3.3-what-follows-for-sutra.md) gives: Sutra's
primary provider may train on what a free tier sends it, so no real personal data may cross that
boundary at all. But notice the order. Even a team with a signed data-processing agreement and a
paid endpoint would still generate fixtures, because the agreement buys them permission and none of
the three powers above.

## Why Sutra needs it

Everything this day claims is a number, and every number has to be reproducible by a stranger.

Section 5 measures the redactor's recall — the fraction of the identifiers that are really there
which it actually found — against the answer key in `lab/_archive.py`. Section 6 sweeps seven stores
and counts what survives a deletion. Those numbers are the argument. If a reader clones this
repository and cannot get the same figures, the argument is hearsay, and the only way they get the
same figures is if the data is the same data. That is exactly what a seed buys, and it is why
`synth.py` takes one.

Day 50 set the precedent when it wrote its gold set before tuning a chunk size
([2.1](../../../day-50-chunking-and-top-k/parts/02-measuring-the-cut/2.1-the-answer-key-you-write-before-you-tune.md)),
and Day 67 hit the cost of getting it wrong when its guardrail fixtures turned out to have been
written by the same person as the guardrail. That cost is the subject of
[4.3](4.3-the-corpus-that-flatters-your-detector.md), and it is the reason this section exists
rather than stopping at "generated data is safe".

## The mechanism

`lab/synth.py` is about a hundred lines and does three things: it invents tickets, it remembers
exactly which identifiers it put in each one, and it scores a redactor against both corpora. This
part is about the first two.

```bash
cd days/day-69-pii-and-data-boundaries/lab
uv run python synth.py
```

**Line by line:**

- With no flags, `synth.py` generates two hundred tickets and prints the first two, so you can see
  what the machine actually made rather than trusting a count.
- Every run prints the seed it used, because a printed archive without its seed is a screenshot: you
  can read it and you cannot get it back.
- Nothing here touches the network, a model or a provider. The generator is arithmetic and string
  formatting, which is why it can run in a test.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
generated 200 tickets from seed 1. The first two:

    From: s.abiodun@example.com

    Downgrade did not take effect. Account 25-9117. Reachable on 07700900261. Let me know if you need anything else.
    -> contains ['s.abiodun@example.com', '07700900261', '25-9117']

    From: z.kowalski@example.com

    Downgrade did not take effect. Account 72-1464. Reachable on 07700900096. Let me know if you need anything else.
    -> contains ['z.kowalski@example.com', '07700900096', '72-1464']
```

Read the `-> contains` line carefully, because it is the part that makes this a fixture rather than
a pile of text. The generator does not hand back a ticket and leave you to work out what is in it.
It hands back the ticket **and the exact list of the identifiers it placed**, which is an answer key
produced at zero cost, for as many tickets as you like.

Now the same command with a different starting number:

```bash
uv run python synth.py --seed 7
```

**Line by line:**

- `--seed 7` changes one integer and nothing else. The pools, the templates and the count are
  identical.
- The value `7` is not special. It is a handle: any run anybody ever does with `--seed 7` produces
  this archive and no other.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
generated 200 tickets from seed 7. The first two:

    From: e.nguyen@example.net

    The export is missing the tax registration number. Account 16-2186. Reachable on 07700900666. Please advise.
    -> contains ['e.nguyen@example.net', '07700900666', '16-2186']

    From: s.nguyen@example.com

    Invoice shows the wrong company name. Account 74-4517. Reachable on 07700900931. Please advise.
    -> contains ['s.nguyen@example.com', '07700900931', '74-4517']
```

Different customers, different accounts, different numbers — and the same shapes, because the shapes
are in the code and only the draw changed. Run either command again and you get its own output back
exactly. That is the drill you can re-run on the same Tuesday morning.

The mechanism that makes this true is one argument passed one level down:

```python
def make(rng: random.Random, n: int) -> list[tuple[str, list[str]]]:
    """`n` tickets, each returned with the exact list of identifiers it was built to contain."""
    out = []
    for _ in range(n):
        surname = rng.choice(SURNAMES)
        email = f"{rng.choice('abcdefghijklmnopqrstuvwxyz')}.{surname}@{rng.choice(DOMAINS)}"
        # 07700 900xxx is the UK range reserved for drama, so a generated number cannot ring.
        phone = f"07700{rng.randint(900000, 900999):06d}"
        account = f"{rng.randint(10, 99)}-{rng.randint(1000, 9999)}"
        text = (
            f"From: {email}\n\n{rng.choice(SUBJECTS)} Account {account}. "
            f"Reachable on {phone}. {rng.choice(CLOSERS)}"
        )
        out.append((text, [email, phone, account]))
    return out
```

**Line by line:**

- `rng: random.Random` is the whole reproducibility argument in one parameter. `make` never calls
  `random.choice` or `random.randint` — the module-level functions that draw from a global stream
  nobody owns. It calls `rng.choice` and `rng.randint`, methods on a generator object that was
  handed to it. Every draw in the function comes from that one stream, in that one order.
- Why it matters that the stream is passed in rather than created inside: a function that seeds its
  own generator can only ever produce one archive, and a function that uses the global one produces
  a different archive depending on what else in the process drew a random number first. Threading
  the object through is what makes the output a function of the seed alone.
- `n: int` is the corpus size, and it is a parameter for the same reason. Two hundred is a choice,
  not a property of the data, and 4.3 leans on being able to change it.
- `-> list[tuple[str, list[str]]]` is the type of the answer key: each element is the ticket text
  paired with the list of identifiers that were put into it. The pairing is created at the moment of
  generation, which is the only moment anybody knows it for certain.
- `rng.randint(900000, 900999)` keeps every generated telephone number inside `07700 900xxx`, the UK
  range reserved for drama. The comment above it says why, and the reason is Principle 13 in
  miniature: a generator that *can* emit a real person's number *will*, on a long enough run, and a
  fixture that dials a stranger is a fixture that got somebody a phone call at eleven at night.
- `example.com`, `example.net` and `example.org` come from the `DOMAINS` pool for the same reason —
  RFC 2606 reserves them and nobody can register them, so a generated address cannot deliver mail.
- `out.append((text, [email, phone, account]))` records the three identifiers *as the values that
  were substituted in*, not as the results of searching the finished text for them. Searching the
  text for identifiers is what a redactor does, and an answer key built by running a detector over
  the data is an answer key that agrees with the detector by construction.

And the seed itself, in `main`:

```python
    seed = 1
    if "--seed" in sys.argv:
        seed = int(sys.argv[sys.argv.index("--seed") + 1])
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    generated = make(rng, 200)
```

**Line by line:**

- `seed = 1` is the default, so a reader who types the command with no flags gets the archive this
  document was written against. A default of "whatever the clock says" would make every run in this
  day unreproducible, which is the failure the whole part is arguing against.
- `random.Random(seed)` builds a **new, independent** pseudo-random generator initialised from that
  number. Nothing else in the process can perturb it, and it will produce the identical sequence of
  draws anywhere Python runs.
- `sys.argv` rather than `argparse` because the script needs one flag, and a dependency-free
  five-line parser is the smaller thing to read. There is nothing to learn here.
- `make(rng, 200)` is the only call site, so the seed reaches every value in the archive through
  exactly one path. That single path is what lets the document say *seed 1* and mean something.

**This day has two archives, on purpose.** `_archive.py` holds fourteen tickets and an answer key
called `TRUTH`, and those tickets were typed out by a person — the sign-off in `ticket:7006`, the
quoted reply in `ticket:7002`, the agent's note in `ticket:7009` that
[1.3](../01-who-a-record-names/1.3-the-box-marked-anything-else.md) took apart. `synth.py` produces
two hundred more from templates. Neither replaces the other, and the reason the day carries both is
[4.3](4.3-the-corpus-that-flatters-your-detector.md), which scores the same redactor against each
and gets two very different numbers.

## When it breaks

The break is not a crash. The generator will happily produce a million tickets and every one of
them will be well formed.

🎬 **The scene:** the fixture is generating cleanly, the redactor scores well against it, and
somebody asks the obvious next question — can we just generate more, and stop worrying about the
hand-written archive at all?

😬 **The naive fix:** raise `n` from 200 to 20 000. More data is more coverage, and it costs nothing
but a few seconds of processor time.

💥 **Why it fails:** the generator emits three kinds of identifier — an address, a telephone number
and an account number — because those are the three its author wrote pools for. The hand-written
answer key in `_archive.py` labels **six** kinds: those three, plus a name, a card fragment and a
postcode. Twenty thousand generated tickets contain exactly as many names as two hundred do, which
is none. The corpus got a hundred times bigger and not one bit wider. You cannot generate a
distribution you have not observed, and multiplying a sample of your own assumptions produces a
larger sample of your own assumptions.

💡 **The insight:** a generated fixture is a **hypothesis about your traffic**, not a sample of it.
Every pool in it is a sentence of the form *"we believe our tickets contain values shaped like
this"*, and the sentences nobody wrote are the ones that will hurt. This is not a reason to stop
generating. It is the reason a generated corpus can never be the only thing you measure against, and
it is precisely why the drill needs somebody to walk the building afterwards and find the narrow
stairwell that was not in the plan.

There is a second and quieter failure, and it is the one that turns a fixture into a liability.
Somebody generates data from a template that is a little too faithful — real product names, a real
support desk address, a customer's actual complaint reworded — and commits it. The file now looks
synthetic and is not. Nothing warns you, because the only person who knows where the template came
from is the person who wrote it, and they will leave. The defence is mechanical rather than social:
pools that cannot produce a real value at all, which is what the drama range and the reserved
domains are for.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead.** They do not choose between generated and real data; they
keep both and are explicit about which question each one answers. The generated corpus is the
**regression fixture** — big, committed, in continuous integration, regenerated from a seed, and
used to catch the day somebody breaks a pattern that used to work. The hand-written corpus is the
**measurement set** — small, laboriously labelled, refreshed from real incidents, and used to
produce the recall number that goes in the runbook. A number quoted from the first one, when the
second one exists, is a red flag in a review.

**Where real-shaped data legitimately comes from.** There are three routes, and each buys something
different for a different price:

| Route | What you get | What it costs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sampling with consent | Genuine text, lawfully held, usable in a labelled set | Slow, small, and the consenting customers are not a random sample of customers |
| Production shadowing | Real traffic shape at real volume | The processing must happen **inside** the boundary that already holds the data — no export, no free-tier endpoint, no laptop |
| A held-out labelled set | A number you can defend, because somebody read every row | Human labelling effort, access control, and a re-labelling cycle as the traffic drifts |

Shadowing deserves the emphasis. It means running the new code against live traffic in place,
inside the same trust boundary the data already sits in, and emitting only counts — how many
identifiers were found, of what kinds, in how many records. Counts leave; text does not. That is the
same crossing discipline
[2.2](../02-where-the-copies-are/2.2-a-boundary-is-a-line-you-can-name.md) named, applied to a
measurement instead of a request.

**The claim to be suspicious of.** Somebody will offer you "anonymised production data" as the way
out of all of this: real traffic with the identifiers stripped or hashed, so it is not personal data
any more and can be shared freely. Treat that as a claim that needs evidence rather than a category
that needs a label. Stripping is measured by the recall figure section 5 produces, and if that
figure is 80% then one identifier in five is still in the file you just called anonymous. Hashing is
worse than it looks, and [5.3](../05-measuring-the-redactor/5.3-the-wall-called-a-name.md) recovers
the addresses from their hashes to show exactly how much it buys. The honest phrase is almost always
*pseudonymised* — still personal data, still inside the boundary, just harder to read.

**What changes at ten thousand.** Two things, in opposite directions. Generation gets cheaper per
record and the fixture becomes something you regenerate rather than store, so the seed graduates
from a convenience to the actual artifact — losing it loses the corpus. And the *gap* in the
previous section widens: with a large generated corpus and a small hand-labelled one, every
dashboard, every test count and every graph is dominated by the generated side, so the number
everyone looks at is the one that measures the least.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"This test fixture was generated without a seed,
so the CI failure I am looking at is not reproducible on my machine and the run that produced it is
gone. Print the seed, take it as a flag, and default it — and while you are in there, say in the
module docstring which kinds of identifier the generator can emit, because I count three and our
answer key has six."*

**The interview question:** *"Why would you use synthetic data if you were allowed to use real
data?"* The answer that shows you have done this: *"Because permission is not the reason to
generate. On a support archive I needed a fixture that could go in the repository, run in a public
CI job, and give the same recall number on my machine and a reviewer's — a production sample can do
none of those, and after the retention window I could not even take the same sample twice. The one
that changed how I worked was steering: the case I most needed to test was a card fragment inside a
quoted reply, which was about one ticket in a few hundred of real traffic, so a hand-labelled sample
I could afford contained none of them. I could generate as many as I wanted. And then the honest
part — I scored the redactor at a hundred per cent on the generated corpus and eighty on the
hand-written one, which is why I now never report a number measured on data I generated from the
same beliefs as the detector."*

# 5.1 — The answer key comes first

## One-line answer

A redactor with no measured recall is a redactor nobody can tune, so the first thing this section
builds is not the redactor — it is the **answer key**: twenty rows, written by hand before any
pattern exists, each saying what identifier is really in the archive, what kind it is, and where it
was sitting.

## The story

The electricity bill arrives and the amount is roughly what it always is. Near the top, in print
much smaller than the amount, it says **ESTIMATED**.

You have never done anything about that word. The bill is plausible. It goes up a bit in winter and
down a bit in summer, which is what you would expect it to do, and every month it agrees with the
month before it, which feels like a kind of evidence.

One evening you go and look at the meter. It is in the cupboard by the front door, behind the coats,
a vacuum cleaner and a bag of shoes nobody wears. The light in there has never worked, so you need a
torch. You move the things out onto the hall floor, kneel down, and read off six digits and a red
one, and you write them on the back of an envelope.

That is the whole job and it is more effort than it sounds, which is why you had not done it before.

But the moment the envelope has a number on it, something is possible that was not possible an hour
earlier. You can say whether the estimate is wrong, and by how much, and in which direction. Until
then the estimate could not be checked by anybody — including the company that produced it. It was
not being believed because it was right. It was being believed because there was nothing to hold it
against.

## The idea in plain language

The number this whole section is about is **recall**: of the things that were really there, what
fraction did you actually find?

Write it as a fraction and the problem is immediately visible.

> recall = (identifiers the redactor found) ÷ (identifiers that were there)

The top of that fraction is free. Any detector will happily tell you what it matched; that is what a
detector is. The bottom is the hard part, and it is hard in a way that no amount of cleverness in
the detector can help with, because **a detector cannot report what it missed**. If it could
recognise the thing it did not match, it would have matched it. The bottom of the fraction has to
come from somewhere else entirely, and "somewhere else" means a person reading the text and writing
down what is in it.

That written-down list is the **answer key**, also called a *labelled set* or *ground truth*: a
record of what is really in a sample, made independently of the thing being scored. The meter
reading is the answer key for the estimate, and it has the same three properties.

**It is made by hand.** Nobody can generate it, because generating it would require already having
the thing it is supposed to check. Somebody kneels in the cupboard with a torch.

**It is made first.** Not first in a stylistic sense — first in a causal one. A key made after
looking at the detector's output has been contaminated by that output, and *When it breaks* below
measures exactly how badly.

**It is the expensive part.** The redactor in this lab is six regular expressions and could be
rewritten from scratch in one sitting. The key is twenty rows, each of which required a person to
read a ticket, decide that a particular substring names somebody, decide what kind of identifier it
is, and be right. That asymmetry is permanent and it is the reason teams skip the key: the cheap
half is the visible half, and it produces a running program, while the expensive half produces a
list.

Two terms already defined, because this part uses them constantly and the standalone test applies.
[1.1](../01-who-a-record-names/1.1-the-list-of-fields-is-not-the-answer.md) defined a **direct
identifier** as a value that points at one person on its own, and
[1.3](../01-who-a-record-names/1.3-the-box-marked-anything-else.md) split every ticket into a
**structured field** — a named slot with a schema, here the sender address — and **free text**, the
box a human typed into.

That second distinction is the one to hold on to, because the answer key records it per row. It
would have been easier to write a key that is just a list of values. It is a deliberate extra
column, and [5.2](5.2-fifty-per-cent-and-it-looked-fine.md) is what the column is for.

## Why Sutra needs it

Every number from here to the end of the day has this file as its denominator.
[5.2](5.2-fifty-per-cent-and-it-looked-fine.md) splits it, [5.3](5.3-the-wall-called-a-name.md)
reads its miss list, section 6 measures a deletion sweep against the same archive, and
[7.2](../07-in-production/7.2-the-boundary-written-down.md) puts the free-text figure into a gate
that is allowed to go red.

There is a principle underneath that list. Principle 11 says evals are tests and a test that cannot
fail is decoration. A redactor with no key attached to it cannot fail: it will match what it
matches, and every run will agree with the last one, and nothing in the repository will ever
contradict it. The key is what makes the claim *"the redactor works"* a sentence that can turn out
to be false.

And [4.3](../04-invented-on-purpose/4.3-the-corpus-that-flatters-your-detector.md) has already
narrowed which key counts. The generator can produce two hundred tickets with an exact key attached,
free, and scoring against it gives 100%. The number that is allowed into this day's report is the
one measured against the fourteen tickets a person wrote and labelled by hand, because that key did
not come out of the same beliefs as the patterns.

## The mechanism

The key lives in `lab/_archive.py`, above the customer table and below the tickets. It starts with
the shape of one row:

```python
@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Item:
    """One personal identifier the archive really contains."""

    ref: str
    kind: str
    value: str
    where: str
```

**Line by line:**

- `@dataclass(frozen=True)` makes each row immutable. That is not tidiness for its own sake: the key
  is the fixed point everything else is measured against, and a row that some scoring code could
  quietly modify is a denominator that moves. `frozen=True` also makes the rows hashable, so they
  can go into a set without extra work.
- `ref` is the address — which ticket this identifier is in. It is not a fact *about* the
  identifier; it is where to go and look, and it is what lets `recall.py` run the patterns over each
  ticket once instead of once per row.
- The other three fields are the three facts the key actually asserts, and each one is a separate
  decision a person had to make while reading:
  - `kind` — what sort of identifier it is: `email`, `phone`, `name`, `account`, `card`, `postcode`.
    This is what makes a miss list readable rather than a column of strings, and it is how
    [5.3](5.3-the-wall-called-a-name.md) can say *every remaining miss is a name*.
  - `value` — the exact substring, character for character. Not a description, not a regular
    expression, not "the customer's phone number". Scoring compares strings, so an approximate value
    would silently score as a miss.
  - `where` — `"field"` or `"free"`. **This is the column that costs nothing to write and changes
    what the day finds.** A key without it can only produce one number, and one number is exactly
    what [5.2](5.2-fifty-per-cent-and-it-looked-fine.md) shows is misleading here.
- There is no `found` field, no `redacted` field and no score. Nothing in this dataclass has any
  opinion about a detector, which is the point: the key describes the archive, and it would be
  identical if `_redact.py` had never been written.

Now the key itself, all twenty rows:

```python
TRUTH: tuple[Item, ...] = (
    Item("ticket:7001", "email", "raghav.menon@example.com", "field"),
    Item("ticket:7001", "name", "Raghav Menon", "free"),
    Item("ticket:7001", "account", "88-4471", "free"),
    Item("ticket:7002", "email", "p.oyelaran@example.net", "free"),
    Item("ticket:7002", "phone", "07700 900142", "free"),
    Item("ticket:7002", "card", "4417", "free"),
    Item("ticket:7003", "phone", "555-0173", "free"),
    Item("ticket:7004", "email", "t.abiodun@example.com", "field"),
    Item("ticket:7005", "account", "88-4471", "free"),
    Item("ticket:7006", "email", "lin.zhou@example.com", "field"),
    Item("ticket:7006", "name", "Lin Zhou", "free"),
    Item("ticket:7006", "phone", "+44 7700 900318", "free"),
    Item("ticket:7006", "email", "lin.zhou@example.com", "free"),
    Item("ticket:7008", "email", "a.fernandes@example.net", "field"),
    Item("ticket:7009", "postcode", "SW1A 2AA", "free"),
    Item("ticket:7010", "email", "m.oduya@example.com", "field"),
    Item("ticket:7012", "email", "j.whitfield@example.org", "field"),
    Item("ticket:7012", "name", "Joan Whitfield", "free"),
    Item("ticket:7014", "email", "s.rahimi@example.com", "field"),
    Item("ticket:7014", "account", "88-6120", "free"),
)
```

**Line by line:**

- It is a `tuple`, not a `list`, for the same reason the rows are frozen. The whole key is one
  constant, and the type says so.
- The rows are one per **identifier**, not one per ticket. `ticket:7001` appears three times because
  that ticket names its customer three ways, and each of the three is a separate chance for the
  redactor to be wrong. That choice sets the denominator at twenty rather than fourteen, and it is
  the right denominator when the question is *how many of the people in here are still named*.
- Look at the two `ticket:7006` email rows — same value, one `field` and one `free`. That is not a
  duplicate that somebody forgot to remove. The address is in the sender field **and** typed again
  in the sign-off, and those are two different things to redact with two different difficulties. A
  key that deduplicated by value would have quietly deleted the harder of the two and improved its
  own score.
- `Item("ticket:7009", "postcode", "SW1A 2AA", "free")` is a row nobody writing a PII field list
  would produce, and it is in the key because a person read the ticket and saw an agent noting down
  the answer to a security question. Keys get rows like this only by being written from the text.
- Three tickets have no rows in the key at all — `7007`, `7011` and `7013`. Those are the ones the
  reader decided name nobody. **A key asserts absence as well as presence**, and the absences are
  what make recall meaningful: if every ticket were labelled, nothing could ever be a false alarm
  and the denominator would just be the corpus.
- `ticket:7011` is the honest edge, and
  [1.1](../01-who-a-record-names/1.1-the-list-of-fields-is-not-the-answer.md) already flagged it:
  *the only admin on an account that opened in 2019* returns exactly one row from the customer
  table, and the key does not count it, because a key that starts counting inferences stops being
  checkable by the next person. The consequence is that twenty is a **floor**. The real number is
  higher and nobody can tell you what it is.

With the key in place, a score becomes possible. `recall.py` runs the patterns over each ticket,
looks up each key row, and counts.

```bash
cd days/day-69-pii-and-data-boundaries/lab
uv run python recall.py
```

**Line by line:**

- `cd` first, because the lab's scripts import their siblings by plain name — `from _archive import
  TRUTH` only resolves when the working directory is `lab/`.
- With no flags, `recall.py` scores `V1`, the redactor somebody writes before measuring anything.
  The patterns themselves are [5.2](5.2-fifty-per-cent-and-it-looked-fine.md)'s subject; here the
  only claim is that a number now exists.
- It prints three rows, because the key carries `where`. A key without that column could only print
  the first one.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
redactor V1

  where     found   of   recall
  all          10   20      50%
  field         7    7     100%
  free          3   13      23%
```

Notice what the script prints and what it does not. Every row carries **`10` and `20`** as well as
`50%`. That is deliberate, and it is worth stating as a rule rather than a formatting preference:
**a rate hides the size of the sample it came from.** "50%" is the same string whether it came from
ten identifiers or ten million, and the two deserve very different amounts of confidence. Twenty is
small. Anybody reading `10 of 20` knows immediately that one relabelled row moves this figure by
five points, and can argue with it accordingly. Anybody reading `50%` has been handed a number with
its uncertainty removed.

The rest of the table is the finding, and it is [5.2](5.2-fifty-per-cent-and-it-looked-fine.md)'s
whole subject.

## When it breaks

The key breaks by being written from the wrong direction, and the symptom is a perfect score.

🎬 **The scene:** somebody has inherited the redactor and has been asked for a recall number, and
there is not one. Writing a key from scratch means reading fourteen tickets carefully and arguing
with a colleague about whether a card fragment counts. There is a much faster route: run the
redactor over the archive, dump everything it matched, eyeball the list, and save it as the labelled
set.

😬 **The naive fix:** it does not even feel like a shortcut. Every value in that dump really is an
identifier — the reviewer checked. The list is honest, the file is committed, and it took one
command.

💥 **Why it fails:** the key now contains exactly the things the redactor finds and nothing else, so
scoring the redactor against it is a tautology. You can run it:

```bash
cd days/day-69-pii-and-data-boundaries/lab
uv run python -c "
from _archive import TRUTH, whole
from _redact import V1, found

refs = sorted({i.ref for i in TRUTH})
key = [(r, v) for r in refs for v in sorted(found(whole(r), V1))]
hits = sum(1 for r, v in key if v in found(whole(r), V1))
print(f'rows in the key from V1 output  {len(key)}')
print(f'rows in the hand-written key    {len(TRUTH)}')
print(f'V1 recall against it            {hits} of {len(key)}  {100 * hits / len(key):.0f}%')
"
```

**Line by line:**

- `key = [(r, v) for r in refs for v in sorted(found(whole(r), V1))]` is the shortcut written out
  honestly: the answer key is defined as *whatever V1 matched*.
- `hits` then scores V1 against that key. Every comparison is a value against the set it was drawn
  from, so the loop cannot produce anything but a hit, and the arithmetic is decided before the code
  runs.
- The two counts are printed side by side because the size of the key is the second half of the
  damage, and it is the half that gets missed.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
rows in the key from V1 output  13
rows in the hand-written key    20
V1 recall against it            13 of 13  100%
```

**100% recall, and a key seven rows smaller than reality.** The three names, the card fragment, the
postcode, the two account numbers and the spaced telephone numbers are not scored as misses — they
are not scored at all, because nothing in the process ever went looking for them. The report will
say the redactor found everything it was asked to find, which is true, and will be read as the
redactor finding everything, which is not.

💡 **The insight:** an answer key must have a **source of belief the detector did not have**. That is
the same rule [4.3](../04-invented-on-purpose/4.3-the-corpus-that-flatters-your-detector.md) applied
to corpora, arriving one level down: there it was the generator and the redactor sharing an author,
here it is the key and the redactor sharing an origin. In both cases the score measures the overlap
between a belief and itself.

**How you avoid it**, concretely, because "write it first" is advice and this is a procedure:

- **Label from the text, never from a diff or a dump.** The input to labelling is the ticket. If the
  labeller has the detector's output open in another window, the key is contaminated whatever the
  intention.
- **Commit the key before the patterns exist.** Then the git history is the evidence, and a reviewer
  can check the claim instead of trusting it. In this lab `_archive.py` says so in its own
  docstring.
- **Expect kinds the detector has no pattern for.** A key containing only shapes the redactor knows
  about is a regression fixture with a different name. Three of this key's six kinds — `name`,
  `card`, `postcode` — matched nothing at all in `V1`, and that is the key doing its job.
- **Score the key, not just the detector.** Ask how many rows exist and whether any ticket has none.
  A key that labels every ticket, or a key smaller than the number of tickets, is usually a key that
  was derived rather than written.

## In production

**Who writes it.** Somebody who is not the redactor's author. This is the rule that gets negotiated
away first, because it costs a second person's attention and there is always a deadline, and it is
the only rule here that cannot be recovered by trying harder alone — the author's blind spots are
written into both artefacts at once. On a small team it can be as light as one colleague reading a
sample and labelling it before ever seeing the patterns; the requirement is independence of belief,
not headcount. If it genuinely has to be the same person, then label first, from text you did not
compose, and commit the labels before opening the detector.

**Sampling real traffic to refresh it.** A key written once measures the archive as it was when
somebody read it, and [1.3](../01-who-a-record-names/1.3-the-box-marked-anything-else.md) already
gave the reason that expires: free text drifts. Customers start pasting bank statements. A new
product ships and support agents begin typing a new kind of reference into notes. Staff learn which
fields get scrubbed and move the information to the box that does not. None of that changes the
recall figure, because the figure is measured against a sample that predates all of it — the number
stays comfortable while the thing it describes moves. The professional version is a standing sample:
a small random draw of recent tickets, labelled on a fixed cadence, appended to the key, with every
real-world miss becoming a row. Random is doing work in that sentence. A sample of the tickets
somebody found interesting is a sample of what somebody already suspected.

**The key is itself personal data, and this is the part that gets forgotten.** A labelled set built
from real tickets is a file whose entire content is a list of the identifying values in your
archive, sorted, deduplicated and helpfully labelled by kind. It is the most concentrated personal
data in the system — worse than the archive it came from, because the archive at least dilutes the
identifiers in prose. Three things follow, and none of them is optional:

- It lives **inside the boundary**
  ([2.2](../02-where-the-copies-are/2.2-a-boundary-is-a-line-you-can-name.md)). It is not a fixture
  to be committed to a repository, not an attachment on a ticket about the redactor, and not a file
  on a laptop. Labelling happens where the data already is, and what leaves is counts.
- It is **a store**, so it goes in the registry that
  [6.3](../06-the-delete-that-did-not/6.3-the-loop-over-a-list-somebody-kept.md) iterates. An
  erasure request that reaches seven stores and not the labelled set has missed the one file that
  lists the customer's identifiers explicitly.
- It has a **retention period somebody chose**, because a key ages into a liability: its value
  decays as traffic drifts, and its sensitivity does not decay at all.

This is exactly why `_archive.py` in this lab is invented rather than sampled
([4.1](../04-invented-on-purpose/4.1-why-invent-the-customers.md)) — a public teaching repository is
outside every boundary there is, so the key here is built from fiction and the argument survives
intact.

**What changes at ten thousand.** The key does not grow with the corpus, and that is the whole
management problem. The archive goes from fourteen tickets to ten thousand while the key stays at
twenty rows, because every row is somebody's careful reading. Meanwhile the *variety* of the traffic
grows, so a fixed-size key covers a shrinking fraction of the shapes that exist. Two consequences
worth planning for: the confidence interval on a twenty-row key is wide enough that small changes in
recall are noise, and the temptation to swap to the generated corpus — which grows for free —
becomes overwhelming. The countermeasure is a budget for labelling effort rather than an intention
to do it, and a rule that a detector change cannot ship without the held-out number being
re-measured.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Where did this labelled set come from? If it was
built by dumping what the detector matched, the 100% is arithmetic rather than evidence and we
should delete the number rather than publish it. I want the key committed before the patterns,
written by somebody who is not you, with at least one kind in it that we have no pattern for. And
put the counts in the report, not just the percentage — `16 of 20` tells me the sample is small
enough to argue about and `80%` does not."*

**The interview question:** *"How do you know your PII detector works?"* The answer that shows you
have done this: *"You cannot know from the detector, because it can only report what it matched —
the denominator has to come from a hand-written answer key, and building that key is the expensive
part of the job. On a support archive I labelled fourteen tickets by hand before writing any
patterns: twenty identifiers, six kinds, and each row recorded the value, the kind and whether it
sat in a structured field or in free text. The first version of the redactor scored 10 of 20. I also
ran the anti-pattern deliberately, because people do it by accident: build the key from the
detector's own output and you get 100% recall on a key seven rows smaller than reality, and nothing
in the repository can ever contradict it. So the rules I work to are that the key is written first,
from the text, by somebody who is not the detector's author, and it is refreshed from sampled
traffic — and that it is treated as personal data itself, because a list of every identifier in your
archive is the most concentrated file you own."*

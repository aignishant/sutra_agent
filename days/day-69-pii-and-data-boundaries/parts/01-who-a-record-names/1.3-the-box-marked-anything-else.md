# 1.3 — The box marked "anything else"

## One-line answer

Two thirds of the identifiers in Sutra's archive are in prose rather than in any field, so a
redactor that is perfect on the structured half of a ticket still leaves **eight of fourteen**
tickets naming somebody.

## The story

You take a washing machine in to be repaired. The man at the counter fills in a job card.

The top of the card is a printed form. Make, model, serial number, fault code, date in, boxes for
each. He fills them in properly, because the form makes him.

Then there is a large empty rectangle at the bottom with `NOTES` printed above it, and that is where
he writes what actually happened. *Customer says it started after the flood last winter. Not the
original owner — bought it off her sister when she moved to Cardiff. She works shifts so ring after
six, and the buzzer is broken so ring the mobile, 07700 900142. Says the last engineer was rude.*

Everything the repair depends on is in the rectangle. The form has the model number.

Months later somebody wants to clear old job cards. They write a routine that blanks the customer
fields on the printed part of the form. It runs, and it works exactly as designed, and every card
comes out with its top half neatly empty and the rectangle untouched.

## The idea in plain language

Every system that deals with people has this shape. There is a **structured** part — fields with
names, types and a schema — and there is a **free-text** part, which is a box where a human types
whatever they need to say.

The structured part is where the designers put the identifiers they thought of. The free-text part
is where everything else ends up, and "everything else" is most of what matters, because the reason
the box exists is that the fields could not hold it.

This produces a lopsidedness that is easy to miss and easy to measure.

**A field is addressable.** It has a name. You can write `record["email"]` and be certain you have
all of it, in every row, for ever. Redacting a field is a one-line change that is complete by
construction — there is no "recall" question, because there is no searching.

**Free text is not addressable at all.** There is no `record["the customer's name"]`, because the
name might be in a sign-off, or in a quoted reply, or in the middle of a sentence, or absent. The
only way to find anything in free text is to **search** for it, and searching is where the concept
of recall — the fraction of the things that are there which you actually found — comes from. Section
5 is entirely about that number.

So the two halves fail differently. The structured half fails **loudly**: a schema change adds a
field, nobody updates the redactor, and the new field is visibly untouched. The free-text half fails
**silently**: it is never complete, it never reports incompleteness, and the amount it is missing is
unknowable without an answer key.

And on a support desk the free-text half is where the people are. That is not an accident of this
archive. A support ticket exists because somebody had a problem the form could not express; the
sentences a customer writes to explain themselves are, necessarily, the part that is about them.

## Why Sutra needs it

Days 49 and 50 built retrieval over the ticket archive, and what they indexed was the **text**
([1.1](../../../day-49-retrieval-and-embeddings/parts/01-text-as-numbers/1.1-the-counter-that-takes-a-description.md)),
because text is what retrieval works on. Day 50 then cut it into chunks and stored each chunk
([1.1](../../../day-50-chunking-and-top-k/parts/01-the-unit-you-index/1.1-the-unit-you-store-is-the-unit-you-find.md)).

Neither day had a reason to care which half of a ticket a sentence came from. The index stores the
free-text half exactly as faithfully as the structured half, which means every property in this
part now applies to the index, the cache and the checkpoint as well —
[2.3](../02-where-the-copies-are/2.3-the-copy-nobody-calls-a-copy.md) counts what that costs.

## The mechanism

`freetext.py` splits the answer key by where each identifier sits, and then asks a specific
question: suppose the structured sender field were redacted **perfectly**, with no misses at all —
what is left?

```bash
cd days/day-69-pii-and-data-boundaries/lab
uv run python freetext.py
```

**Line by line:**

- The `field` count is every identifier in the ticket's sender address, which is the only structured
  field these tickets have.
- The `free` count is every identifier a human typed into the body.
- The per-ticket table then assumes the field is handled and counts only what remains, which is why
  the column is headed `still named`.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
the archive, split by where the identifier sits

  in a structured field     7
  in free text             13
  total                    20

  suppose the sender field is redacted perfectly and nothing else is:

  ticket         still named  kinds
  ticket:7001              2  account, name
  ticket:7002              3  card, email, phone
  ticket:7003              1  phone
  ticket:7004              0  -
  ticket:7005              1  account
  ticket:7006              3  email, name, phone
  ticket:7007              0  -
  ticket:7008              0  -
  ticket:7009              1  postcode
  ticket:7010              0  -
  ticket:7011              0  -
  ticket:7012              1  name
  ticket:7013              0  -
  ticket:7014              1  account
```

**Thirteen of twenty identifiers are in free text**, and after a perfect job on the field,
**eight of the fourteen tickets still name somebody**.

Three of those rows are worth reading in the original, because each is a different way prose defeats
a schema. Run `uv run python freetext.py --show` and look at them.

**`ticket:7006` — the sign-off.** The customer's address is in the sender field, so a field redactor
gets it. It is also at the bottom of their message, typed by them, along with their name and a
telephone number:

```text
Downgrade did not take effect. Plan still shows Pro after a week.

--
Lin Zhou
+44 7700 900318
lin.zhou@example.com
```

The same email address is now in two places with two completely different natures. One is a field.
One is four characters into the fourth line of a paragraph. Redacting the first does nothing to the
second, and there are three identifiers in that block, not one.

**`ticket:7002` — the quoted reply.** The sender field is `support-forward@example.org`, which is a
desk address and names nobody. The redactor cleans it and reports success:

```text
Forwarding from the customer:

> From: p.oyelaran@example.net
> I have been charged twice this month. Both charges show on the card ending 4417.
> Please call me on 07700 900142 before 5pm.
```

The customer's address, telephone number and card fragment are all inside a quote block in the body.
The one address a field-based redactor found was the only address in the ticket that was not
personal data.

**`ticket:7009` — the agent's own note.** Nobody sent this; a member of staff typed it while helping
somebody:

```text
Password reset request. Verified by asking for the postcode on file, SW1A 2AA, and the
date the account opened. Reset link sent.
```

The agent wrote down the answers to the security questions in the ticket, in the course of doing
their job correctly. Free text collects this material because being helpful and being careful pull
in the same direction right up until the moment the note is stored, indexed and retrieved.

## When it breaks

The break is a redactor that measures itself on the half it can measure.

🎬 **The scene:** the redaction is written, and somebody writes a test. The test builds a record with
an email in the `sender` field, runs the redactor, asserts the field comes back as `[REDACTED-EMAIL]`.
Green.

😬 **The naive fix:** add more field tests. `phone` field, `name` field, `address` field. All green.
The suite grows and the confidence grows with it.

💥 **Why it fails:** every test is about the addressable half, because the addressable half is the
half that is easy to write a test for. The suite is not measuring the redactor; it is measuring the
seven identifiers out of twenty that were never in question. In section 5 this produces a
free-text recall of **23%** sitting behind a field recall of **100%**, and only one of those two
numbers ever gets reported.

💡 **The insight:** the structured half needs a **test**, because it is complete or it is not. The
free-text half needs a **measurement**, because it is never complete and the only useful question is
how incomplete. Those are different activities, and a suite made entirely of the first kind will
report health it has not checked.

The other break has no error message either. A schema gains a field — `company`, say, or
`contact_name` — and the redactor's field list does not. This one at least *can* be caught
mechanically: a test that asserts the redactor's field list equals the model's field list fails the
day the schema changes, which is the correct moment to find out.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead.** They stop treating "redact the record" as one operation.
Structured fields are handled by **allowlist**: the record is rebuilt from the fields explicitly
permitted to leave, so a new field is excluded until somebody adds it. That is the same deny-by-
default argument Day 40 made about tools
([1.3](../../../day-40-filtering-and-allowlists/parts/01-the-list-you-agreed-to/1.3-the-filter-is-on-your-side.md)),
pointed at columns instead. Free text is handled by a detector with a **published recall figure**,
and the figure is in the runbook, not in somebody's head.

**What degrades at scale.** Free text gets longer and stranger. Customers paste in email threads,
screenshots-as-text, log excerpts and, eventually, the contents of another system entirely. Each of
those brings identifier shapes the detector has never seen, and the *proportion* of a ticket that is
prose rises as the product matures and the forms stop being able to express the questions.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic.** Agents adapt to the redactor. When staff
learn that certain fields get scrubbed, the information moves — into the notes box, into a ticket
title, into a follow-up comment. The redaction rate looks stable and the data has simply relocated
to wherever nobody is looking. You will not see this in a test; you see it by sampling real tickets
and reading them, which is why the answer key in
[5.1](../05-measuring-the-redactor/5.1-the-answer-key-comes-first.md) has to be refreshed rather
than written once.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Every test here asserts on a field. Two thirds of
our identifiers are in the body, and the body is what we index and cache. Give me a recall number on
free text against a labelled sample before this goes near the retrieval pipeline, and report field
and free-text recall separately — the combined number hides the one that matters."*

**The interview question:** *"Where does PII actually hide in a support system?"* The answer that
shows you have done this: *"In the notes box, and it is not close. I measured our archive: seven of
twenty identifiers were in a structured field and thirteen were in free text — sign-offs, quoted
replies, and agents writing down the answers to security questions while verifying somebody. The
dangerous part is that the field half is trivially easy to redact and easy to test, so you get a
green suite and a hundred per cent number for the half that was never the problem. The fix is to
report the two recalls separately, because a single combined figure is designed to hide this."*

# 1.1 — The list of fields is not the answer

## One-line answer

Personal data is not a set of column names you can tick off; it is **anything that gets you back to
a particular person**, which means the question is never "is this field on the list?" but "could
somebody find them with this?"

## The story

You leave a bag on a bus. At the depot there is a lost-property counter and a form.

The form asks for the make, the colour, the size, and the date and route you travelled. You fill it
all in. Black, medium, wheeled, the 47, Tuesday afternoon. The man behind the counter goes into the
back and comes out with three black medium wheeled bags and says he cannot tell which is yours.

Then you remember. There is a sticker on the handle. It is from a conference three years ago and it
has a cartoon of a badly drawn octopus on it, and you have never got round to peeling it off.

He goes back and returns in under a minute with your bag.

Nothing on the form found it. The form asked for the properties somebody once decided were the
identifying ones, and every one of your answers was true, and together they narrowed a hundred bags
down to three. The octopus sticker was on no form, in no box, and it was the only thing in the
building that pointed at exactly one bag.

## The idea in plain language

Almost everybody starts this subject with a list. Name. Email address. Telephone number. Date of
birth. Card number. The list feels like the definition, and it feels like the job is to find those
things and cross them out.

The list is not the definition. It is a handful of examples that happen to be identifying **most of
the time**, collected because they are easy to name.

The actual definition has nothing to do with field names. **Personal data is any data that relates
to a person who can be identified from it** — directly, or by combining it with something else
somebody could reasonably get hold of. Two things follow from that sentence, and both of them are
uncomfortable.

**It is a property of a situation, not of a column.** The same value can be personal data in one
system and not in another. A postcode on its own covers thousands of households in a city and one
household in a rural area. A job title is meaningless in a company of forty thousand and names one
person in a company of nine. Nothing about the *value* changed. What changed is how many people it
could be.

**It is not limited to things anybody designed as an identifier.** The octopus sticker was not an
identifier. It became one because it was rare and it was attached. In a support archive the
equivalents are everywhere: a customer who always signs off the same unusual way, an account that is
the only one on a legacy plan, a ticket that mentions a specific product that only three companies
bought.

Two terms, because the rest of this day uses them constantly.

- **Direct identifier** — a value that points at one person on its own. An email address, a full
  name plus a company, a customer number. These are the list.
- **Quasi-identifier** — a value that points at *many* people on its own, and at one person once you
  put it beside one or two others. A city. A plan. The month somebody signed up. These are not the
  list, and [1.2](1.2-three-columns-that-name-one-person.md) measures what they do when you combine
  them.

The reason this matters on day one of the subject rather than as a footnote is that **a redactor
built from a list can only ever find the list**. It is complete against its own idea of the world,
and it reports a clean result, and the octopus sticker sails straight past it. Section 5 measures
exactly that, on this repository's own archive.

## Why Sutra needs it

Day 48 built a memory policy with a rule that said *redact before you write*
([4.1](../../../day-48-memory-design/parts/04-privacy-and-erasure/4.1-redact-before-you-write.md)),
and it was right, and it left one question open: redact **what**. That part could assume you know
what to remove, because its subject was *when* the removal happens.

Today the assumption gets tested. The desk built over Days 46 to 51 now holds a session store, a
memory store, an embedding index and a response cache, all of them full of text a customer typed. A
rule that says "remove the personal data" is not executable until somebody can say what that phrase
covers, and [5.2](../05-measuring-the-redactor/5.2-fifty-per-cent-and-it-looked-fine.md) shows what
happens when it is answered from the list.

## The mechanism

The archive this day measures against is `lab/_archive.py`, and it is worth opening before anything
else, because it is written to disagree with the list.

```bash
cd days/day-69-pii-and-data-boundaries/lab
uv run python freetext.py
```

**Line by line:**

- `freetext.py` reads the answer key in `_archive.py` — a hand-written list of every identifier the
  fourteen tickets really contain — and reports where each one sits.
- It does not run a redactor. That comes in section 5. Right now the only question is what is
  *there*, which has to be settled before anything can be scored against it.
- `where` is either `field`, meaning the value sits in the structured sender address that every
  ticket has, or `free`, meaning a human typed it into the body.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
the archive, split by where the identifier sits

  in a structured field     7
  in free text             13
  total                    20
```

Twenty identifiers across fourteen tickets. Seven of them are in the one place a schema knows
about. The other thirteen are in prose.

Now look at what kinds they are, which is where the list starts to fail:

```text
  ticket         still named  kinds
  ticket:7001              2  account, name
  ticket:7002              3  card, email, phone
  ticket:7003              1  phone
  ticket:7004              0  -
  ticket:7005              1  account
  ticket:7006              3  email, name, phone
  ticket:7007              0  -
  ticket:7008              0  -
  ticket:7009              1  postcode
  ticket:7010              0  -
  ticket:7011              0  -
  ticket:7012              1  name
  ticket:7013              0  -
  ticket:7014              1  account
```

Three of those kinds are on everybody's list: `email`, `phone`, `name`. Three are not, and each one
is a different way the list fails.

**`account`** — `88-4471`. Nobody writes "customer number" on a personal-data list, because it is
internal bookkeeping. It is also the single most reliable way to get from a row to a person in this
entire system, which is precisely why Day 48 made it the `holder` field
([4.2](../../../day-48-memory-design/parts/04-privacy-and-erasure/4.2-you-cannot-delete-what-you-cannot-address.md)).
A value that is good enough to *address* a person by is, by definition, good enough to *identify*
them by. Those are the same property looked at from two directions, and a policy that treats the
first as infrastructure and the second as privacy has contradicted itself.

**`card`** — `4417`, the last four digits. Four digits are not a card number and cannot be charged.
They are also what a bank asks you for on the telephone to confirm you are you, which tells you
exactly how identifying the industry that issued them considers them to be.

**`postcode`** — `SW1A 2AA`. On its own it is a building. Beside the date an account opened, which
ticket 7009 also contains, it is a person. That combination is [1.2](1.2-three-columns-that-name-one-person.md).

And notice `ticket:7011`, which scores zero:

```text
Call notes: the customer is the person who set up the account in 2019 and is the only
admin. They travel a lot so afternoons are better. Nothing to action.
```

No name. No number. No address. The answer key gives it nothing, and the answer key is being
conservative — *the only admin on an account that opened in 2019* is a description that returns one
row from the customer table. This day does not count it, because a hand-written key that starts
counting inferences stops being checkable. It is worth knowing that the real number is higher than
twenty and that nobody can tell you what it is.

## When it breaks

The failure this part exists to prevent does not produce an error. It produces a report.

🎬 **The scene:** somebody is asked whether the ticket archive contains personal data before it is
used to build a retrieval index. They open a ticket, look at it, and see prose about invoices and
sign-ins.

😬 **The naive fix:** grep for the list. Search the archive for `@` to find email addresses, and for
long runs of digits to find telephone numbers and cards. Count the hits. Report the count.

💥 **Why it fails:** the search finds what it was told to look for and reports it as what is there.
On this archive that search finds the seven addresses in the sender field and a couple in the
bodies, and returns a number that is both accurate and useless. Every account number, every name in
a sign-off, the postcode and the card fragment are outside the search and therefore outside the
report. The report does not say "I looked for four things"; it says "here is the personal data".

💡 **The insight:** a search for a list is evidence about the list, never about the archive. The
only honest form of the sentence is *"I found N instances of the four shapes I searched for, and I
do not know what else is in there"* — and once you have to write that sentence down, you start
wanting the answer key that section 5 builds.

There is a second break, and it is the one that shows up in a review rather than in a run. Somebody
declares a field "not personal data" and moves it outside the boundary — into a log, a metrics
label, an analytics export. The declaration was true about the field in isolation and false about
the system, because the destination it moved to also holds the account number. Nothing warns you.
The two harmless halves meet in a place nobody was thinking about, and
[2.1](../02-where-the-copies-are/2.1-one-sentence-seven-places.md) counts how many such places this
desk already has.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead.** They do not write a list of fields at all. They write a
**data inventory**: one row per store, saying what is in it, whose it is, how long it lives and who
can read it — and they treat "does this count as personal data?" as a question answered per store
rather than per column. The list of shapes still exists, as an input to a detector, but it is
explicitly labelled as a floor rather than a definition.

**What changes at scale.** Two things, in opposite directions. The archive gets bigger, so rare
values get rarer relative to it and more combinations become unique — a corpus of ten thousand
tickets contains far more accidental identifiers than a corpus of fourteen, not fewer. And the
number of *destinations* grows, so the chance that two harmless halves meet somewhere goes up
roughly with the square of the number of stores rather than with their count.

**The failure that only shows with real traffic.** Free text drifts. A support desk that has never
seen a customer paste a bank statement into a ticket will see one eventually, and the shapes in it
were on nobody's list. This is not hypothetical arithmetic: it is why
[4.3](../04-invented-on-purpose/4.3-the-corpus-that-flatters-your-detector.md) can show a detector
scoring perfectly on data somebody generated and badly on data somebody wrote.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"This PR says the archive has no PII because it
greps for `@` and long digit runs. That is a statement about the grep. Where is the labelled sample
you scored against, and what is the recall on free text specifically? The account number is in this
list and it is not in your patterns, and it is the field we use to look customers up."*

**The interview question:** *"Is a customer ID personal data?"* The answer that shows you have done
this: *"Under the definition, yes, if it can be linked back to a person — and in any system that
has a customer table, it can, because that is what the table is for. I had this exact argument on a
support archive. The account number was treated as internal bookkeeping and left out of the
redactor, while the same field was the key we used to answer erasure requests. You cannot use a
value to find a person and then claim it does not identify them. What actually resolves it is not
the argument, it is the measurement: we wrote down every identifier in a sample by hand first, and
the account number was the most common thing in it."*

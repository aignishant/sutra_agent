# 1.4 — The number that only means something here

## One-line answer

`88-4471` is meaningless to anyone outside this system and decisive inside it, so whether a value is
personal data depends on **who holds the table it joins to** — and you are the one holding it.

## The story

You hand your coat in at a cloakroom and get a small paper ticket. It says `147`.

Drop that ticket in the street and it is litter. There is nothing on it. No name, no coat, no
building. Somebody who finds it learns precisely nothing about you, and you would not think twice
about throwing it away.

Walk back to the counter with it, though, and it is complete authority over one specific coat. The
attendant does not ask your name. They do not need it. The number is the whole conversation, because
behind the counter there is a rail where the same number is written on a tag.

The ticket did not change when you crossed the doorway. What changed is proximity to the rail.

## The idea in plain language

Some values carry their meaning with them. An email address is an email address in a text file, in a
database, in a screenshot, on a whiteboard. It is legible to anybody, anywhere, without help.

Other values carry no meaning at all on their own and become identifying the moment they are next to
the table that resolves them. `88-4471`, `RF-2291`, a session id, a row id, the last four digits of a
card. These are **linkable identifiers**: harmless in isolation, complete in context.

People get this backwards in a specific and consistent way, because the isolation test *feels* like
the right test. You look at `88-4471`, you cannot tell whose it is, and you conclude it is not
personal data. That reasoning has quietly substituted a different question. It asks **"can I identify
somebody from this value alone?"** when the question that matters is **"can somebody identify
somebody from this value plus what they can get?"**

And the answer, for anybody inside your organisation, is always yes — because the resolving table is
the customer table, and its entire purpose is to be joinable. You do not have to imagine an attacker
with unusual access. Every engineer, every support agent and every process with a database
connection already holds the rail.

There is a sharper version of the point, and it is the one worth remembering: **a value you can look
a customer up by is a value that identifies them.** Those are not two properties that happen to
coincide. They are one property, described from the inside and from the outside. So the moment you
build a lookup — and Day 48 built exactly one, `holder`, so that erasure would be possible
([4.2](../../../day-48-memory-design/parts/04-privacy-and-erasure/4.2-you-cannot-delete-what-you-cannot-address.md))
— you have created a direct identifier, and you created it deliberately, and you were right to.

The mistake is never creating it. The mistake is creating it for the erasure path and then not
telling the redaction path it exists.

## Why Sutra needs it

Every store this desk writes is keyed by something like this. Day 47's session store keys on a
session id. Day 48's memos key on `holder`. Day 49's index rows are named `ticket:7006#2`. Day 51's
cache keys on a hash of the prompt. Day 61's checkpoints key on a run id.

Those keys are the reason
[6.3](../06-the-delete-that-did-not/6.3-the-loop-over-a-list-somebody-kept.md) can delete a customer
from six stores at once. They are also, by the argument above, identifiers in their own right — which
is why [7.1](../07-in-production/7.1-answering-what-do-you-hold.md) has to treat a key as data rather
than as plumbing.

## The mechanism

Look at what the account number does in this archive, and then at what a redactor built from a list
of PII fields does about it.

```bash
cd days/day-69-pii-and-data-boundaries/lab
uv run python freetext.py --show
```

**Line by line:**

- `--show` prints the body of every ticket that still names somebody after the sender field is
  handled, so the account numbers can be read in the sentences they actually appear in.
- The kinds column from the plain run already told you `account` appears in four tickets. This is
  the same fact with the prose around it.

Three of the fourteen tickets mention `88-4471`, in three different registers:

```text
Invoice shows the wrong company name. The billing address updated last month but the
PDF still prints the old name. Order 88-4471 if that helps.
```

The customer calls it an **order** number. It is the account number. That is the ordinary confusion
of a real desk: people repeat the number they have been given and use whichever word they remember.
A detector looking for "account" as a label finds nothing here, because the label is wrong and the
value is right.

```text
Escalation notes. Account 88-4471 has now called four times. Spoke to the same person
each time. They are getting understandably annoyed and asked for a manager.
```

Written by staff, about a customer, in a ticket that has no customer address on it at all. The
sender field is `desk@example.org`. The **only** thing in this ticket that connects it to a person is
the account number — and it connects a fairly sensitive sentence to that person.

```text
Cannot download the annual statement. The button spins and nothing happens. Account
88-6120.
```

And the resolution from [1.2](1.2-three-columns-that-name-one-person.md): `88-4471` is the Bristol
Pro account that opened in March 2019, and `88-6120` is the Leeds Pro account from November 2021.
The customer table resolves both, and the customer table is the thing every service in this project
has a connection to.

Now the redactor. `V1` in `lab/_redact.py` is the version somebody writes from the list — emails and
telephone numbers:

```bash
uv run python recall.py --misses
```

**Line by line:**

- `recall.py` scores the patterns against the hand-written answer key, and `--misses` names every
  identifier that was not found.
- The full argument about these numbers is section 5. Right now only the shape of the miss list
  matters.

```text
  not found:
    ticket:7001    name      free   'Raghav Menon'
    ticket:7001    account   free   '88-4471'
    ...
    ticket:7005    account   free   '88-4471'
    ...
    ticket:7014    account   free   '88-6120'
```

Three of the ten misses are account numbers. They were not missed because the regular expression was
badly written — `\b\d{2}-\d{4}\b` is four characters of pattern and finds all three. They were
missed because **nobody put account numbers on the list**, and nobody put them on the list because
the isolation test said they were not personal data.

`V2` adds that one pattern and picks up all three. The interesting thing about the fix is how small
it is, and how completely unavailable it is to anybody who has not first stopped believing the
isolation test.

## When it breaks

The break is a value crossing a boundary because it passed the isolation test on the way out.

🎬 **The scene:** a bug in the retrieval pipeline. To debug it you need to know which documents came
back for which query, so you add the row references to the log line. They are opaque strings like
`ticket:7006#2`.

😬 **The naive fix:** none needed, apparently. There is no customer text in the line, no name, no
address. It is a document reference.

💥 **Why it fails:** `ticket:7006` resolves to a ticket, the ticket resolves to a customer, and the
log is now a record of which customers were involved in which support conversations at which times.
That log has a different retention period from the archive, a different access list, and it may well
be shipped to a third-party service that the archive is not. You have moved identified data across a
boundary and the review said "no PII in this log line", correctly, by the wrong test.

💡 **The insight:** an opaque reference is a **pointer to** personal data, and a pointer is only as
opaque as the resolver is unavailable. Inside your own systems the resolver is always available, so
inside your own systems a pointer is the data. The question to ask about any identifier leaving a
store is not *what does this say* but *what can the person receiving it join it to*.

There is a matching break in the other direction, and it is the one that catches careful people. A
value that genuinely is not linkable today becomes linkable when somebody builds the table that
resolves it. The card fragment `4417` is a good example: four digits identify nobody, until a
customer service tool starts storing "last four" as a verification field, at which point every
historic log line containing those four digits has retroactively become identifying. Nothing was
edited. A join became possible.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead.** They classify identifiers by **who can resolve them**, not by
whether they look personal, and they write it down: *direct* (legible to anyone), *internal-linkable*
(resolvable by anyone with a database connection), *pseudonymous* (resolvable only via a mapping held
somewhere separate and access-controlled). The third category is the only one that buys anything, and
it buys it precisely because the mapping is held somewhere else — which is a deployment decision, not
a naming decision.

**What degrades at scale.** The number of resolvers grows without anybody deciding it should. A
support tool, an analytics warehouse, a data-science notebook and a billing export each end up with a
copy of enough of the customer table to resolve the key. Every one of those copies converts every
opaque reference in every downstream system into identified data, and none of those conversions
appears in any changelog.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic.** Support agents type the account number into
free text far more often than any schema predicts, because it is the thing they have on screen. This
archive has three instances in fourteen tickets. That rate is enough to guarantee that a system
redacting only structured fields is storing identified prose continuously, at a rate proportional to
its ticket volume.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"You have classified `account_id` as non-personal
because it is a random-looking string. We look customers up by it. If we can, so can everyone with a
read replica. Move it to internal-linkable and go and check which of our exports currently carry it,
because I think three of them do."*

**The interview question:** *"Is a pseudonymous ID personal data?"* The answer that shows you have
done this: *"It depends entirely on who holds the mapping, and inside the company the answer is
always yes, because the mapping is the customer table and that is what it is for. The version of this
I actually hit was an account number appearing in support free text — three tickets out of fourteen
in the sample. Our redactor did not touch it, because it was classified as internal bookkeeping, and
the same field was the key we used to answer erasure requests. Those two facts cannot both be right.
What I do now is classify identifiers by who can resolve them rather than by what they look like, and
the test I apply is simple: if we can look a customer up by it, it identifies them."*

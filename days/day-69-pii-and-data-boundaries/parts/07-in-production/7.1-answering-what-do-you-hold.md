# 7.1 — Answering "what do you hold?"

## One-line answer

The question a person is entitled to ask is *what do you hold about me*, and the desk can only answer
it from the same registry the purge loops over — **7 of 7 stores, 13 copies** after a single ordinary
run, each with the day and the reason it was written.

## The story

A woman changes GP surgery when she moves, and asks for her notes to be transferred.

The new surgery receives a file. It has her consultations, her prescriptions, her blood test results
going back years — the record, in the sense that the old surgery understood the word.

What does not come across is the letter from the physiotherapist that was scanned and filed under a
different reference. Or the note the practice nurse wrote in the appointment system rather than the
clinical one, because that is where the nurse works. Or the results the hospital sent to the old
surgery directly, which sat in a shared inbox and were read and never attached to anything.

Nobody withheld anything. Every one of those is a real record, held by the same practice, about the
same person — and the file that was sent was assembled from the system somebody thinks of as *the
notes*, by a person who was doing the job properly.

## The idea in plain language

Section 6 built a purge. This part is its twin, and it is the one that is usually forgotten, because
deletion is dramatic and **disclosure is not**.

A person is entitled to ask two questions, and the second one is harder:

- *Delete my data.* — section 6.
- *What do you hold about me?* — this part.

They look symmetrical and they are not. A purge can succeed store by store and stop; a disclosure has
to **produce the contents**, in a form a person can read, from every store, without leaking anybody
else's data in the process. And it is answered under a deadline, by a person who is not the engineer
who built the system, from whatever mechanism exists on the day.

Here is what makes it hard, stated as the thing this day has been building towards. There is no
single place to look. Part [2.1](../02-where-the-copies-are/2.1-one-sentence-seven-places.md) counted
seven stores after **one** ordinary run, and part
[2.3](../02-where-the-copies-are/2.3-the-copy-nobody-calls-a-copy.md) established that six of them
were built for reasons that had nothing to do with holding customer data. So the honest answer is
assembled, not looked up — and the thing it is assembled from had better be the same registry the
purge uses, or the two answers will disagree.

That last point is the one to hold onto. **A disclosure that reads a different list from the purge is
a system that will tell somebody it holds nothing and then fail to delete something.** One registry,
two operations.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because the desk holds a customer's words in seven places and the curriculum has now built all seven.
An access request would arrive at whoever is on call, and the mechanism they would use is whatever
exists — which today is `copies.py`, a script that counts occurrences of a needle, and which is not an
answer.

There is a second reason, and it is the one that makes this part production-level rather than a
formality. **The disclosure is where you find out whether your data map is true.** A purge that misses
a store reports success and nobody notices. A disclosure that misses a store gets sent to the person
whose data it is, and they are the one party who might know it is incomplete. Building the disclosure
first is how a team discovers its own architecture; the day this repository can answer *what do you
hold* honestly is the day its data map stops being a diagram.

## The mechanism

The instrument is deliberately blunt: count occurrences of one identifier in every registered store,
and say why each store has it.

```bash
cd days/day-69-pii-and-data-boundaries/lab
uv run python copies.py
```

**Line by line:**

- `cd` first: the lab's scripts import their siblings by plain name.
- `copies.py` calls `run()` for one ordinary handled ticket, then walks `STORES` — the same registry
  part [6.3](../06-the-delete-that-did-not/6.3-the-loop-over-a-list-somebody-kept.md)'s purge loops
  over — and counts the needle in each. One registry, two operations, exactly as argued above.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
after one run of the desk, 'lin.zhou@example.com' appears in:

  store        times  written because                         
  archive          2  it is the ticket                        
  session          2  Day 47 persisted the conversation       
  memory           2  Day 48's policy kept a memo             
  index            2  Day 49 stored the chunk it indexed      
  cache            2  Day 51 cached the prompt and reply      
  log              1  Day 22 logged an excerpt                
  checkpoint       2  Day 61 saved the run state              

  stores holding it   7 of 7
  total copies        13
```

The third column is what turns a count into an answer. *"Your email address appears in our response
cache because we cache prompts and replies"* is a sentence a person can read and object to; **13** on
its own is not. An access request is answered in prose, and the prose has to come from somewhere — so
the reason belongs in the registry beside the store, written when the store is added, by the person
who knows why they added it.

Search by a different identifier and the machinery is unchanged:

```bash
uv run python copies.py --needle "Lin Zhou"
```

**Line by line:**

- `--needle` swaps the term. That is the whole configurability of this instrument, and its whole
  weakness — see *When it breaks*.

Then the part that surprises people, which is the disk:

```bash
uv run python copies.py --files
```

**Line by line:**

- `--files` lists the backing file and its size for each store, so the abstraction *store* is grounded
  in something a person can see, copy and forget about.

Measured on 2026-09-06 — the last block:

```text
  on disk:
    lab\state\archive.json          2667 bytes
    lab\state\session.json          1274 bytes
    lab\state\memory.json            764 bytes
    lab\state\index.json            1840 bytes
    lab\state\cache.json            1061 bytes
    lab\state\log.jsonl              571 bytes
    lab\state\checkpoint.json       1118 bytes
```

Seven files, about nine kilobytes, for one ticket. Every one of them is a file that can be copied to a
laptop, attached to a bug report, or included in a backup — which is the point part
[2.2](../02-where-the-copies-are/2.2-a-boundary-is-a-line-you-can-name.md) made about boundaries being
lines you can name.

## When it breaks

**The search-term problem, and it is fatal to this instrument as an answer.** `copies.py` finds
occurrences of a string. A customer whose ticket says *"Lin"* in one cached response and
*"lin.zhou@example.com"* in another produces two different counts depending on which needle you use,
and a disclosure assembled by grepping is a disclosure that is complete only for the term somebody
chose. That is the same recall arithmetic section 5 measured for the redactor — 69% on free text — and
it applies here with the direction reversed: a redactor that misses leaves data in, a disclosure that
misses leaves data out.

The fix is the one part [1.4](../01-who-a-record-names/1.4-the-number-that-only-means-something-here.md)
set up: **address the data by key, not by content.** Every store in the registry has a key that
resolves to a subject — a ticket ref, a session id, a memory holder, a run id — and a disclosure built
on those keys returns rows rather than matches. The cache is the exception, for the reason part
[6.2](../06-the-delete-that-did-not/6.2-the-store-you-cannot-address.md) gave, and the honest
disclosure names the cache and says it was searched rather than addressed.

**The second failure is disclosing somebody else's data.** A ticket thread can contain two customers.
A memory row can summarise a pattern across several. An index chunk can span a boundary. A disclosure
assembled by search will happily return a row that mentions the requester and three other people, and
sending it is itself a breach — committed while satisfying a privacy request, which is a particularly
unpleasant way to have an incident. Every disclosure needs a filtering pass, and *that* is a redactor
running in the other direction, with the same measured recall and the same honest caveats.

**The third is timing.** A disclosure is a snapshot, and the system keeps running. If the request is
answered on Tuesday and the desk handles another ticket for the same customer on Wednesday, the answer
was true and is not. That is fine and it has to be **said**: the disclosure carries the moment it was
taken, in the same way part [3.2](../03-the-line-at-the-provider/3.2-the-page-that-does-not-answer.md)
argues a terms row carries the date it was read.

## In production

**What the answer has to contain.** Not a count. For each store: what it holds about this person, why
it holds it, how long it will be kept, and who else it has been sent to. The last two are the ones
engineers forget, because they are not in the code — retention is a policy and onward transfer is a
contract, and both belong in the same document as the registry so that the disclosure can be assembled
from one place. Part [7.2](7.2-the-boundary-written-down.md) is that document.

**What degrades at scale.** Disclosure requests are rare enough to be handled by hand and frequent
enough that handling them by hand becomes somebody's job. The trajectory is predictable: first a
developer with a database client, then a script, then a script nobody has re-run since the architecture
changed. The failure is not the volume, it is that **the mechanism decays out of step with the system
it queries**, and unlike a purge nobody notices, because the person receiving an incomplete answer has
no way to know. The countermeasure is to run the disclosure against a synthetic subject on a schedule
and assert it returns the rows a synthetic subject should have — a test, in other words, which is
possible precisely because section 4 made every fixture synthetic.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic.** Identity. Answering *what do you hold about
me* requires knowing who *me* is, and the verification step is the one that gets attacked: a request
made by somebody impersonating the customer turns your carefully-built disclosure into an exfiltration
tool with a legal obligation attached to it. This is the same shape as Day 66's `exfiltrate` harm and
Day 68's *whose permissions is the tool using* — the disclosure path needs the strongest identity
check in the system, not the most convenient one, and it must never be automatable from a ticket.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Two things before we call this a disclosure
mechanism. It searches by string, so it's complete for whatever needle we happen to pick — build it on
the keys instead, and where we genuinely can't (the cache), say in the output that this store was
scanned rather than addressed. And there's no filtering pass: a ticket thread with two customers in it
will be returned whole, and sending that is a breach we caused while answering a privacy request. Also
please put the retention period next to each store while you're in there — that's half of what the
answer has to say and it isn't anywhere in the code."*

**The interview question:** *"How would you answer a subject access request for a system like this?"*
An honest answer: *"From the same registry the deletion uses, which is the part people get wrong — if
disclosure reads a different list from erasure, you'll eventually tell somebody you hold nothing and
then fail to delete it. On the desk I measured, one ordinary ticket put the customer's address in
seven stores, thirteen copies, and six of those stores existed for reasons unrelated to holding
customer data — a session, a memory store, a search index, a cache, an event log, a checkpoint. The
answer has to be per store and in prose: what we hold, why, how long, who else got it. Two things I'd
insist on: address the data by key rather than by searching for the person's name, because a search is
only as complete as the term you picked; and put a filtering pass in front of the output, because a
thread that mentions two customers will otherwise be disclosed whole, and that's a breach committed
while answering a privacy request. And the identity check on the request has to be the strongest in
the system — it's an exfiltration path with a deadline attached."*

# 4.3 — Parents, peers and two switches

## One-line answer

By default every agent in a tree can transfer to its children, its parent **and its siblings**, and
the switch people reach for to stop a specialist bouncing work back —
`disallow_transfer_to_parent=True` — is the one that leaves exactly the sibling-to-sibling edge
that produces an infinite loop.

## The story

The three counters at the driving licence office are staffed by people who have all worked there
for years and who all know each other's jobs a bit.

Counter 1 does new licences. Counter 2 does renewals. Counter 3 does address changes. And because
they are decent colleagues, any of them will say "oh, that's actually Meena on 2, go and see her"
without making you queue again. That is a good office. The permission to send you sideways is what
makes it good.

It is also how you can spend forty minutes there. Counter 1 sends you to 2 because your licence
expired. Counter 2 sends you to 3 because your address is out of date and she cannot renew against
a stale address. Counter 3 sends you back to 1 because you need a new licence, not a renewal, if
the address change lands first. All three are being helpful. All three are right. Nobody in the
building has the job of noticing that you have been round the loop twice.

## The idea in plain language

Agents attached with `sub_agents=[...]` form a **tree**: a lead with children, and those children
possibly with children of their own. Transfer reachability is computed from that tree, and the
default is more generous than people expect. An agent can reach:

1. **Its own sub-agents** — the ones it declared. Obvious, and the reason you attached them.
2. **Its parent** — so a specialist can hand work back up. Sensible, and the mechanism behind
   "return to the lead".
3. **Its peers** — the *other* children of its parent. This is the one nobody predicts.

Two boolean fields on `LlmAgent` control 2 and 3:

- `disallow_transfer_to_parent` — default `False`. When `True`, the agent cannot hand back up.
- `disallow_transfer_to_peers` — default `False`. When `True`, the agent cannot hand sideways.

Sub-agents in `single_turn` or `task` mode are excluded from every transfer list, because they are
calls rather than hand-overs. That is the same fact [2.1](../02-call-or-handover/2.1-a-call-comes-back.md)
observed from the other side.

The reason peer transfer exists is good: it saves a hop. Without it, a specialist that receives
misrouted work has to go back to the lead, which then has to route again — two model calls to fix
one mistake instead of one. The reason it is dangerous is the driving licence office: two agents
that each believe the other owns the case will pass it between them for as long as you let them,
and neither is doing anything wrong.

There is a note on `disallow_transfer_to_parent` in the framework source that is worth reading
before you set it, because its effect is larger than its name:

```text
  NOTE: Setting this as True also prevents this agent from continuing to reply
  to the end-user, and will transfer control back to the parent agent in the
  next turn. This behavior prevents one-way transfer, in which end-user may be
  stuck with one agent that cannot transfer to other agents in the agent tree.
```

Read that twice. The field does not only remove an edge; it changes what the agent is allowed to be
— it stops being an agent that holds a conversation.

## Why Sutra needs it

Tonight's `sutra/delegation.py` has a lead and two or three specialists as siblings. Left at the
defaults, `kb_specialist` can transfer to `archive_specialist` and back, forever, and `gate.py`
fails the build until `disallow_transfer_to_peers` appears somewhere in the file. That check exists
because the loop is cheap to prevent and expensive to discover.

Day 59 is the phase's containment lab, and containment starts with reachability: you cannot bound
what an agent does until you know who it can reach. Day 68's least-privilege work is the same
question about tools rather than about colleagues.

## The mechanism

`reach.py` builds the same three-agent tree four times, under four settings, and asks ADK's own
`_get_transfer_targets` who each agent can reach.

```bash
cd days/day-55-delegation-and-transfer/lab
uv run python reach.py
```

**Line by line:**

- `tree()` builds a fresh lead and two fresh specialists for every setting. Fresh, not reused,
  because `sub_agents` mutates the children (it sets `parent_agent` and fills in `mode`), and a
  reused agent would carry the previous run's state.
- The switches are passed to both specialists, so each row shows a symmetric configuration rather
  than a mixture.
- `_get_transfer_targets(agent)` is the framework's function, so the answer is the framework's
  answer.

Verified against `google-adk` 2.7.1 (`google/adk/flows/llm_flows/agent_transfer.py`) and
adk.dev/workflows/collaboration/ on 2026-09-05.

Measured on 2026-09-05:

```text
default: everyone can reach everyone
  triage_lead          -> ['kb_specialist', 'archive_specialist']
  kb_specialist        -> ['triage_lead', 'archive_specialist']
  archive_specialist   -> ['triage_lead', 'kb_specialist']

disallow_transfer_to_peers=True
  triage_lead          -> ['kb_specialist', 'archive_specialist']
  kb_specialist        -> ['triage_lead']
  archive_specialist   -> ['triage_lead']

disallow_transfer_to_parent=True
  triage_lead          -> ['kb_specialist', 'archive_specialist']
  kb_specialist        -> ['archive_specialist']
  archive_specialist   -> ['kb_specialist']

both switches on
  triage_lead          -> ['kb_specialist', 'archive_specialist']
  kb_specialist        -> ['(nobody)']
  archive_specialist   -> ['(nobody)']
```

Four configurations, and the third is the one to stare at.

**Default.** Each specialist reaches the lead *and its sibling*. Nobody wrote that sibling edge.
It came for free with `sub_agents=[...]`.

**Peers disallowed.** Each specialist can only go back to the lead. This is the shape most systems
want: work flows down from the router and returns to the router, and every routing decision is made
in one place, by the agent whose job is routing. It costs one extra hop when the lead routes
wrongly, and it buys a graph with no cycles among the specialists.

**Parent disallowed.** This is the trap. Somebody reasoning *"the specialist should finish the job,
not bounce it back to me"* sets this field — and the result is that `kb_specialist` can reach
`archive_specialist` and `archive_specialist` can reach `kb_specialist`, and neither can reach the
lead. The only remaining edges form a two-node cycle. The switch that was meant to reduce
hand-offs has removed the only exit.

**Both on.** Specialists cannot transfer at all. Per
[4.2](4.2-the-enum-that-refuses-a-stranger.md), the tool is not even offered to them: no targets,
no declaration, no injected block. Whatever arrives, they answer. That is a legitimate
configuration — it is the "leaf worker" shape — as long as you have accepted that a misrouted
request gets answered by the wrong specialist rather than corrected.

```mermaid
graph TD
    subgraph default["default"]
        L1["triage_lead"] --> K1["kb"]
        L1 --> A1["archive"]
        K1 --> L1
        A1 --> L1
        K1 <--> A1
    end
    subgraph peers["disallow_transfer_to_peers"]
        L2["triage_lead"] --> K2["kb"]

        A2 --> L2

```

The third box has no arrow leaving it. That is the picture of
[7.1](../07-failure-lab/7.1-two-counters-pointing-at-each-other.md).

## When it breaks

The break is the loop, and it costs requests rather than raising anything:

```text
  hop  1  lead -> auth_agent
  hop  2  -> billing_agent
  hop  3  -> auth_agent
  ...
  hop 12  -> billing_agent

  STOPPED AT THE HOP LIMIT after 12 hops - nothing was answered

  requests spent    12
  by agent          {'auth_agent': 6, 'billing_agent': 6}
  answered          no
```

Twelve requests, no answer, and the only reason it stopped at twelve is that `pingpong.py` has a
`HOP_LIMIT` constant. **The framework has no such limit.** There is no default maximum transfer
depth in ADK 2.7.1; the loop ends when a model decides to stop, when the invocation is cancelled,
or when the provider stops answering because the quota is gone.
[7.1](../07-failure-lab/7.1-two-counters-pointing-at-each-other.md) is the whole story.

The other break is the silent one from the fourth configuration. An agent with both switches on
produces no error, no log line and no clue — it simply never transfers, because the tool it would
use was never built. If a specialist is answering questions it should be handing back, check its
reachability before you edit its instruction.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead.** They pick a topology on purpose and write it down in one
line. The common, defensible default is **hub and spoke**: `disallow_transfer_to_peers=True` on
every specialist, so all routing decisions happen at the lead. It is auditable — every hand-off is
a decision by the agent whose job is deciding — and it is acyclic by construction. Peer transfer is
an optimisation you add later, to a specific pair, with a hop counter already in place, when you
have measured that the extra hop through the lead is actually costing you something.

**What degrades at scale.** Reachability is quadratic in siblings. Five specialists under one lead
means each can reach four peers plus the parent, so there are twenty-five edges nobody drew and no
review ever looked at. The number of possible two-agent cycles alone is ten. Nothing in the code
review shows this, because it is not in the code — it is the absence of two boolean fields.
Printing the reachability matrix, exactly as `reach.py` does, is a five-line CI check and the
cheapest structural audit in this whole day.

**The failure that only appears with real traffic.** The loop needs a genuinely ambiguous request
to start, and your test cases are not ambiguous, because you wrote them to exercise one agent
each. The request that starts a ping-pong is the one that legitimately sits between two
specialists — *"cannot sign in to pay the invoice"* — and those arrive from users, not from test
fixtures. The pair that will loop is usually the pair whose descriptions each defer to the other,
which is a thing you can look for in the descriptions before you ever see the traffic.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Both specialists have
`disallow_transfer_to_parent=True` and neither has `disallow_transfer_to_peers`. That is the exact
configuration where the only edges left are between the two of them. Either flip which switch you
are setting, or add a hop cap in the tool callback — but do not ship this, because there is nothing
in the framework that stops it."*

**The interview question:** *"In a multi-agent tree, who can hand work to whom by default?"* An
honest answer: *"More people than you expect. Children, yes, but also the parent and every sibling.
Two boolean fields turn the last two off: `disallow_transfer_to_parent` and
`disallow_transfer_to_peers`, both defaulting to False. The subtlety is that the one people reach
for is the wrong one — 'the specialist shouldn't bounce it back to me' makes you set
`disallow_transfer_to_parent`, and that leaves the sibling-to-sibling edges as the only ones,
which is a cycle with no exit. I default to hub-and-spoke: peers disallowed, so every routing
decision happens at the lead and the graph is acyclic. And I print the reachability matrix in CI,
because with five siblings there are twenty-five edges nobody wrote down."*

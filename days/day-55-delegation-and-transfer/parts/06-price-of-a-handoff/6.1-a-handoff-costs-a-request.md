# 6.1 — A hand-off costs a request

## One-line answer

The three shapes have three different prices in the only currency that matters on a free tier: an
edge costs **1** request per ticket, a transfer **2**, and an agent-as-tool call **3** — so
twenty tickets through a call-shaped step is sixty requests against a lane that allows ten a
minute.

## The story

The community clinic charges nothing, and it runs on a book of numbered slips. Every consultation
tears one out. There are two hundred slips in the book and when they are gone, the clinic is closed
for the day, whatever time it is.

The nurse practitioner who sees you first can handle most things herself: one slip. If she needs
the doctor's opinion on a rash, she takes you across, the doctor looks, and you come back to her to
finish: that is her slip, the doctor's slip, and hers again to write it up. Three. And if she sends
you to the doctor outright and the doctor finishes the consultation: two.

Nobody in that building thinks about money. They think about slips, because slips are what runs
out. And the sentence you hear most often is not *"that will be expensive"* but *"that's three
slips, are you sure?"*

## The idea in plain language

Day 24 established the unit: budgets here are [denominated in quota, not
dollars](../../../day-24-token-accounting-and-budgets/parts/02-two-ceilings/2.3-denominated-in-quota-not-dollars.md).
A free-tier lane gives you requests per minute and requests per day, and when they are gone they
are gone regardless of how small each one was. That makes **request count**, not token count, the
number that decides whether a design is affordable.

Delegation spends requests. Each shape spends a different number, and the count is structural — it
does not depend on the model, the prompt or the answer.

**An edge: one request.** The graph decided. One agent runs, once.

**A transfer: two.** The lead runs and decides to hand over — that is a request, because deciding
is a model call. The specialist then runs and answers — a second. The lead does not speak again,
so it stops there.

**A call: three.** The lead runs and decides to call — one. The specialist runs and returns a
result — two. The lead runs *again*, because it is still holding the turn and now has to say
something with the result — three. That third request is not optional; it is the definition of a
call. [2.1](../02-call-or-handover/2.1-a-call-comes-back.md) said a call comes back, and coming
back costs a turn.

The ordering is the thing to remember, because it is the reverse of the ordering people assume. The
option that gives away the *least* control — the call — costs the *most* requests. Control and
quota pull in opposite directions, and that is a real trade-off rather than a free lunch waiting to
be found.

## Why Sutra needs it

The desk runs on Gemini Flash-class free tier. The hub's §6 request budget for today is zero,
because every script in this lab is deterministic — but tonight's build brief produces
`sutra/delegation.py`, and the first time you run it against a real model, the arithmetic below is
what you spend.

More importantly, Day 58's graph has five stations. If the research step is a call, every ticket
costs three requests for that one step, and a hundred tickets in a working session is three hundred
requests through a lane that permits a few hundred a day. The design decision that looks like a
correctness question in [1.3](../01-why-split-the-desk/1.3-three-homes-for-a-step.md) is also the
decision that determines whether the day's work finishes.

## The mechanism

`price.py` counts requests for one ticket handled three ways. Nothing is estimated: the ledger is
incremented at exactly the points a real run would issue a call.

```bash
cd days/day-55-delegation-and-transfer/lab
uv run python price.py
```

**Line by line:**

- `Ledger` in `_desk.py` is a list of strings, one per request, with a `spend(who)` method. Naming
  each spend is what makes the itemised output readable rather than a bare number.
- `by_edge`, `by_call` and `by_handover` each spend exactly where a real run would: `by_call`
  spends three times because the lead runs before *and* after the specialist.
- `FREE_TIER_RPM = 10` is a placeholder with a comment telling you to re-read the provider page.
  Free rosters move (Addendum 02), and a hard-coded rate in a lab is a claim with a shelf life.
- The `windows` column is ceiling division of requests by the rate: how many rate-limit windows the
  work needs at minimum. It is a lower bound, not a schedule.

Measured on 2026-09-05:

```text
one ticket, three shapes (1 ticket(s))

  shape            requests  per ticket   windows at 10 RPM
  edge                    1         1.0                   1
  agent-as-tool           3         3.0                   1
  transfer                2         2.0                   1

  the same ticket, itemised
    edge            ['researcher']
    agent-as-tool   ['lead (decide + call)', 'kb_specialist', 'lead (summarise the result)']
    transfer        ['lead (decide + transfer)', 'kb_specialist (owns the answer)']
```

The itemised list is the part to read, because it names what each request buys.

`'lead (decide + call)'` — the routing decision. You pay for the decision even when it is obvious,
which is the entire argument for [1.3](../01-why-split-the-desk/1.3-three-homes-for-a-step.md)'s
first question: an edge is free precisely because there is no decision.

`'lead (summarise the result)'` — the third request, the one people forget when budgeting. It exists
because the lead is still holding the turn. It is also the request that makes the call shape
*worth* its price: it is where the lead checks the result, applies
[5.4](../05-agent-as-tool/5.4-a-failure-that-arrives-as-a-string.md)'s failure check, and decides
what to do next. You are not paying for nothing. You are paying for control.

Now scale it, because per-ticket arithmetic hides the thing that bites:

```bash
uv run python price.py --tickets 20
```

**Line by line:**

- `--tickets 20` runs the same three shapes twenty times and reports totals, per-ticket averages and
  windows.
- Twenty is chosen to be a realistic afternoon on a support desk rather than a benchmark.

```text
one ticket, three shapes (20 ticket(s))

  shape            requests  per ticket   windows at 10 RPM
  edge                   20         1.0                   2
  agent-as-tool          60         3.0                   6
  transfer               40         2.0                   4
```

Sixty requests against twenty. Same twenty tickets, same specialist, same work done — three times
the quota, because of a design decision made once, months earlier, in a file nobody has opened
since.

And the windows column is the operational version of the same fact. At ten requests a minute, the
call-shaped design needs six rate-limit windows where the edge-shaped one needs two. On a lane with
a daily cap as well, that ratio is the difference between finishing the queue and stopping at four
in the afternoon.

## When it breaks

The break is a shape chosen for elegance and paid for in quota, and what it looks like is a 429
that arrives earlier in the day than anybody expected:

```text
429 RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED: quota exceeded for gemini-3.7-flash, retry in 27s
```

Day 21's ladder handles that honestly — retry, substitute, escalate — but no amount of good 429
handling turns sixty requests back into twenty. The cheapest fix is upstream and structural: find
the steps that are edges pretending to be decisions.

The other break is a *nested* call, and its arithmetic is genuinely alarming. A lead that calls a
specialist, where the specialist itself calls a sub-specialist, does not cost three plus three. The
inner call costs three requests inside the specialist's own turn, and the outer call still costs
the lead's two surrounding requests, so a two-level call chain is five and a three-level chain is
seven. [8.1](../08-in-production/8.1-how-deep-is-defensible.md) counts it properly. Nothing warns
you, because each layer individually looks reasonable.

And there is a break in the other direction, worth naming so this part is not read as *"always use
edges"*. A transfer is cheaper per hand-off than a call, and if the specialist then handles five
turns of a billing dispute, the transfer cost two requests for the hand-off and the alternative —
the lead relaying five turns as calls — would have cost fifteen. The right comparison is never one
ticket; it is one *conversation*.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead.** They put the request count in the design document, next to
the shape, as a number per ticket. *"Research: agent-as-tool, 3 req/ticket. Classification: edge, 1
req/ticket."* It takes one line and it turns an architecture argument into arithmetic. The version
of this that survives contact with a real system is a counter emitted per shape, per station, so
the design document's claim can be checked against what actually happened.

**What degrades at scale.** The multiplier compounds with ticket volume *and* with graph depth at
the same time, and neither is visible from the code. A team that adds one specialist call to one
station has changed the per-ticket cost of the whole pipeline, and the only place that shows up is
the quota dashboard three weeks later. The guard is a budget test: assert the request count for a
fixed fixture ticket, and fail the build when it changes without somebody saying so.

**The failure that only appears with real traffic.** Retries. Every one of these counts assumes each
request succeeds first time. On a free tier a proportion do not, and a retried request is another
request. So the real multiplier is the shape's count times the retry factor, and the retry factor
is worst exactly when volume is highest. A design that fits the quota at three requests per ticket
does not fit it at three-and-a-half.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"This is three requests per ticket for a step
that has one possible destination. There is only one specialist, so the routing decision is not a
decision — make it an edge and you are at one. Keep the call shape for the research station, where
the lead genuinely has to do something with the answer, and say so in the design doc with the
number next to it."*

**The interview question:** *"How do you budget a multi-agent system?"* An honest answer: *"In
requests per ticket, per shape, because on a free tier requests are what runs out rather than
tokens. An edge is one — nothing decides, one agent runs. A transfer is two: the lead's decision
and the specialist's answer. An agent-as-tool call is three, because the lead runs again afterwards
to do something with the result, and people always forget that third one. So the option that keeps
the most control costs the most quota, which is a real trade-off. I measured twenty tickets at
sixty requests through a call-shaped step against twenty through an edge, and the fix was noticing
that the step had one possible destination and therefore no decision to pay for. I also watch
nesting, because a two-level call chain is five requests rather than six, and nobody's mental model
of that is right until they count it."*

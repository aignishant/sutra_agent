# 1.3 — Three homes for a step

## One-line answer

Every step in an agentic system lives in exactly one of three places — an **edge** the graph
already drew, a **call** the caller makes and waits for, or a **hand-over** that gives the whole
conversation away — and choosing between them is a design decision you make once, deliberately,
per step.

## The story

You arrive at a hospital reception desk with a letter from your doctor. Three things can happen,
and everyone who has ever sat in that chair has seen all three.

**One.** The receptionist takes the letter, stamps it, writes your name in the book and says
"blood tests, second floor, they are expecting you". No decision was made. Everyone with that
letter goes to the second floor. It is written on the wall behind her.

**Two.** She reads the letter, frowns slightly, picks up the internal phone, says "I have a
patient here with a referral from Dr Ahmed, is this one of yours?", listens, says "thank you", puts
the phone down and turns back to you. You never spoke to the person on the phone. You never left
the chair. She asked one question, got one answer, and is still the person dealing with you.

**Three.** She reads the letter, stands up, and says "come with me". She walks you down the
corridor to the fracture clinic, hands your letter to the nurse there, and goes back to her desk.
You are now the fracture clinic's patient. If you have a question about parking, you are going to
ask the nurse, because the receptionist is two corridors away and no longer part of this.

Those are not three styles of the same thing. They are three different arrangements of who is
responsible for you, and everyone in the building knows which one has happened.

## The idea in plain language

Give the three arrangements their engineering names.

**An edge.** The step was decided before the request arrived. The graph — the wiring you build on
Day 53 and Day 54 — already says that after classify comes research. Nothing reads the request to
work that out. An edge costs no model call and it cannot be wrong, because there was no decision to
get wrong. It also cannot adapt: every request takes the same path.

**A call.** One agent asks another agent a bounded question and **waits for the answer**. The
caller does not stop being the agent in charge. It gets a result, and then it keeps going — it can
use the result, quote it, doubt it, or throw it away and ask something else. This is what the
framework spells `mode="single_turn"`, and section 5 is entirely about it. In the story, it is the
internal phone.

**A hand-over.** One agent gives the conversation to another agent and stops. The second agent now
owns the turn, answers in its own voice under its own handbook, and control returns only if
somebody decides to send it back. This is what the framework spells `transfer_to_agent`, and
section 4 is about it. In the story, it is the walk down the corridor.

The distinction that matters, and it is the whole of section 2, is between the second and the
third: **a call comes back, and a hand-over does not.**

There is a natural order to these, and it is an order of *how much you are giving away*:

| The step is… | Its home | What you hand over | Costs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The same every time, known in advance | an **edge** | nothing; the graph decided | no routing call |
| A bounded question inside a bigger judgement | a **call** | one question | the specialist's request, plus your own turn afterwards |
| A different job that needs its own conversation | a **hand-over** | the whole conversation | the specialist's request; your turn is over |

Principle 13 says blast radius before capability, and this table is that principle applied to
control flow. **Choose the smallest hand-off that does the job.** An edge grants nothing. A call
grants one question. A hand-over grants a conversation. Promoting a call into a hand-over later is
easy; taking a conversation back from an agent your users have already started talking to is not.

## Why Sutra needs it

Day 58 draws the triage graph, and every arrow in it is one of these three. *After classify, run
research* is an edge, always, because it is true of every ticket. *The drafting agent needs one
knowledge-base fact before it can finish a sentence* is a call, because the drafter must keep
writing afterwards. *This turned out to be a refund dispute and billing should own it end to end*
is a hand-over, because the customer's next five questions are billing's, not the desk's.

Getting this wrong is not a style error. Make the research step a hand-over and the draft never
gets written, because control never comes back to the agent that was going to write it. That exact
failure is [7.2](../07-failure-lab/7.2-the-handover-that-never-came-back.md), where three of five
pipeline stations never ran.

## The paper behind it

Solver** · `doi:10.1109/TC.1980.1675516` · IEEE Transactions on Computers, C-29(12), 1980 ·
<https://doi.org/10.1109/TC.1980.1675516>

It claimed that a network of processors should assign work by *announcing the task and letting

## The mechanism

The mechanism of this part is a decision, so the mechanism is a decision procedure. Run it in order
and stop at the first *yes*.

```text
1. Does every request take this step, in the same place, regardless of content?
       yes -> EDGE.  Draw it in the graph. No model call, no routing decision, no risk.

2. Does the caller need to keep working after it has the answer?
       yes -> CALL.  mode="single_turn". The caller stays in charge and gets a result back.

3. Does the next several turns of conversation belong to somebody else?
       yes -> HAND-OVER.  transfer_to_agent. You are done; they own the customer now.

   none of the above -> the step is not well defined yet. Say what it does in one
   sentence with no "and" in it, then run this list again.
```

**Line by line:**

- Question 1 is first because an edge is free. Every routing decision you can delete is a request
  you do not spend and a mistake you cannot make. Day 53's graph exists so that most steps can be
  edges.
- Question 2 is the operational test for a call, and it is deliberately about the *caller*, not the
  specialist. If the caller has nothing left to do, a call is just a hand-over with extra steps.
- Question 3 is about *turns*, plural. A hand-over is worth it when the specialist will hold the
  conversation for a while. Handing over for one answer and immediately needing control back is the
  most expensive way to ask a question.
- The fall-through is not a joke. A step that answers no to all three is usually two steps wearing
  one name, and the sentence-with-no-"and" test is the same one-idea test the parts of this
  curriculum are written under.

Here are six real Sutra steps run through it, so the procedure has been exercised rather than
described:

| Step | Answer | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| After classify, run research | edge | true of every ticket; no decision to make |
| The drafter needs the fix from KB-104 | call | it has a sentence to finish afterwards |
| The ticket is a refund dispute | hand-over | billing owns the next several turns |
| Log the routing decision | edge | it happens on every route, unconditionally |
| Check whether the archive has a similar ticket | call | the classifier uses the answer to set priority |
| The customer asked for a human | hand-over | the desk is not part of what happens next |

Two of the six are edges, two are calls, two are hand-overs — and that ratio is roughly what a real
system looks like. Most steps are edges. If yours are mostly hand-overs, you have built a relay
race.

## When it breaks

The break is choosing a hand-over where a call was meant, and it is common because a hand-over
*feels* like the more powerful option. Here is what it costs, measured in
[2.2](../02-call-or-handover/2.2-who-owns-the-turn.md):

```text
  turn 1  ok    triage this ticket about a failed sign-in            answered by triage_lead
  turn 2  ok    also, why was this account charged twice             answered by billing_agent
  turn 3  WRONG back to the sign-in problem - which kb article covers it answered by billing_agent
  turn 4  WRONG is the session cookie the cause                      answered by billing_agent
  turn 5  WRONG what priority should i give the sign-in ticket       answered by billing_agent

  conversation ends held by      billing_agent
  answered by the wrong desk     3/5
```

One correct hand-over on turn 2, and then three turns answered by an agent whose handbook says it
owns invoices. Nothing errored. The customer simply spent the rest of the conversation at the wrong
counter, because a hand-over is a one-way door unless somebody walks back through it.

The opposite mistake is quieter: choosing a call where an edge would do. It does not produce a
wrong answer, it produces a bill. Every routing decision is a model call that a drawn edge would
not have needed, and [6.1](../06-price-of-a-handoff/6.1-a-handoff-costs-a-request.md) shows that
turning a one-request edge into a three-request call triples the quota cost of a step whose outcome
never varies.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead.** They write the choice down next to the wiring, in one line,
as a comment or an architecture decision record: *"research is a call, not a transfer, because the
classifier sets priority from the result."* The reason is worth more than the choice, because in
eight months somebody will look at a call that always returns the same thing and be tempted to make
it a transfer, and the sentence is what stops them.

**What degrades at scale.** Hand-overs are sticky. Once real users are talking to a specialist,
turning that specialist back into a call means the conversations that are currently in flight have
nowhere to go, and the specialist's handbook has been written for months on the assumption that it
speaks to the customer directly. Demoting a hand-over to a call is a migration; promoting a call to
a hand-over is a config change. That asymmetry is the whole argument for starting small.

**The failure that only appears with real traffic.** The decision procedure asks "does the next
several turns belong to somebody else?", and on your test set the answer is knowable because you
wrote both the question and what comes after it. In production, whether a conversation continues is
the user's choice, and roughly the same request will sometimes be a one-shot question and sometimes
the start of a ten-turn argument about a refund. The honest design handles the common case as a
call and lets the specialist request a hand-over when it turns out to be the other case — which is
what Day 63's approval gates are shaped like.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Why is this a transfer? The lead calls billing
and then formats the result, so the lead is still working after billing answers. That is a call.
Make it `mode='single_turn'` and you keep the conversation, keep the audit trail in one place, and
stop the customer from ending up parked at the billing desk for the rest of the session."*

**The interview question:** *"How do you decide whether one agent should call another or transfer
to it?"* An honest answer: *"I ask whether the caller has work left to do after the answer arrives.
If it does — it has to format the result, or use it to decide a priority, or write the next
sentence — it is a call, because a transfer means it never gets control back to do that work. If
the next several turns genuinely belong to the other agent, like a refund dispute moving to
billing, it is a transfer. And before either, I ask whether the step varies at all; if every
request takes it, it is an edge in the graph and there is no routing decision to pay for. The
default is the smallest one that works, because promoting later is cheap and demoting is a
migration."*

Then take one step from your own work — anything, not necessarily an agent — and run it through the
procedure. If it falls through all three, write the one-sentence description with no "and" in it
and try again.

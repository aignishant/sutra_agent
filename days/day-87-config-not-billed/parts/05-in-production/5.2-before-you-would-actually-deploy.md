# 5.2 — What you would need before actually deploying

## One-line answer

Nine things stand between the config this day validated and a deployment somebody could defend, and
the useful property of the list is that eight of them are free — the single item that costs money is
the command itself, which is why it is the one this curriculum leaves unrun.

## The story

The insurance premium you pay to move a risk.

A small business insures its stock. The premium is not the interesting part of that transaction;
what is interesting is the form. To get cover you have to answer questions: what is the stock worth,
where is it kept, is there an alarm, who has keys, when was the wiring last checked.

Half of those questions are things the owner had never written down. Some of them turn out to have
answers nobody likes — the wiring, usually. And the business ends up making three or four changes
before the policy starts, none of which the insurer required and all of which came out of being
asked.

The premium moves the risk. **The form is what reduced it**, and the form was free.

## The idea in plain language

This day validated a config. That is not the same as being ready to deploy, and the gap is nine
items. They are in the order you would do them, and the order is not the order they came up in.

**1. Pin `adk_version` in the config.** Part
[4.2](../04-what-it-costs/4.2-the-dependency-you-inherit.md): otherwise the production runtime is
decided by whichever machine runs the command. One line of JSON, and the check for it is one more
line in `validate.py`.

**2. Replace the config's `env_vars` with an explicit allowlist.** Part
[2.3](../02-the-config/2.3-your-env-travels.md) measured the whole `.env` travelling. Name the two
variables the desk needs; everything else stays behind.

**3. Decide how the credential arrives, since it is no longer in the file.** A secret manager, a
platform-level binding, or an identity the resource carries. This is a real design decision and
item 2 creates it — a fix that leaves the desk unable to call a model is not a fix.

**4. Pass `--agent_engine_id` from the config, always.** Part
[5.1](5.1-the-failure-path-nobody-tests.md): the default creates a new resource every run, so three
iterations of a fix leave three resources.

**5. Print the assembled config before sending it.** Part
[2.2](../02-the-config/2.2-what-the-cli-adds.md) counted three keys authored against seven sent. The
review that matters is of the document that goes, not the one you wrote.

**6. Decide what the thirteen published methods are allowed to be called by.** Also part
[2.2](../02-the-config/2.2-what-the-cli-adds.md), and it connects to Day 68's least privilege: ten of
them are session and memory operations, and `async_delete_session` is one of them.

**7. Arrange an orphan sweep outside the deploying process.** Part
[5.1](5.1-the-failure-path-nobody-tests.md)'s window. A label applied at creation and a scheduled
listing that reports anything unlabelled is the cheap version.

**8. 🅿️ Measure the Flash-Lite allowance.** Part
[4.1](../04-what-it-costs/4.1-priced-in-requests.md): the model half is 246 requests a day against an
observed 20 on a different lane, and the desk's actual lane has been unmeasured since Day 78. This
is the number that decides whether the deployment is viable at all, and it has now blocked six
separate lists.

**9. 🅿️ Type the command.** The only item that costs money, and the only one this curriculum does
not do.

**Items one to seven are free and can be done this afternoon.** That ratio is the argument of part
[1.2](../01-what-you-hand-over/1.2-why-this-day-is-parked.md), stated as a list rather than as a
principle.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because "we did not deploy it" has to be a defensible sentence, and this list is what makes it one.
The difference between *skipped* and *parked* is whether you can produce the work that was done
instead.

And because item 8 is the sixth day running that the same missing measurement has decided an answer.
Day 78's criterion 3, Day 79's model choice, Day 81's judge pricing, Day 82's bisection budget, Day
83's criterion 6, and now this. One controlled burn against a free-tier key would close all six.

## The mechanism

The list with what each item costs and what it unblocks, so it can be argued with rather than
admired:

| # | Item | Where | Roughly | Blocks |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | pin `adk_version` | the config | a line | reproducible runtime |
| 2 | env allowlist | the config + deploy path | half an hour | item 3 |
| 3 | how the credential arrives | a design decision | a conversation | the deploy working at all |
| 4 | always pass `--agent_engine_id` | the deploy path | a line | resource sprawl |
| 5 | print the assembled config | the deploy path | an hour | any real review |
| 6 | access model for the published methods | a design decision | a conversation | Day 68's least privilege |
| 7 | orphan sweep | outside the repository | needs somewhere to run it | silent cost |
| 8 | 🅿️ measure the allowance | `docs/PACKAGES.md` | one controlled burn | five other lists |
| 9 | 🅿️ the deploy command | a billing account | money | nothing else here |

**Items 1, 2 and 4 are under an hour between them** and they are the three that turn a config which
validates into a config that would behave. Items 3 and 6 are the ones that need another person,
which means they need scheduling rather than time.

The day's own eval is the first four of these, expressed as a module that does not exist yet:

```bash

uv run python gate.py; echo "exit: $?"
```

does not run it.**

## When it breaks

The list breaks by being read as a backlog, and there is a specific failure that follows.

```text
1. the config is written and validated                       (this day)
2. the deploy is not done, because it is parked
3. six months later somebody needs it deployed, quickly
4. they find the config, which is good, and run the command
5. items 1 to 7 were never done, because nobody was blocked on them
6. a resource exists with the whole .env on it and no way to find it again
```

Step 4 is the dangerous one precisely *because* step 1 went well. A validated config in a repository
reads as a finished piece of work, and the seven things that were known and not done are recorded
only in a document nobody opens in a hurry.

There is no error text. Every step is somebody doing the sensible thing with what was in front of
them.

The mitigation is item 5 — print the assembled config — because it is the only one of the seven that
puts a finding in front of the person at step 4, at the moment they are about to act, without
requiring them to have read this page.

## In production

**What a professional does.** Writes the readiness list before the work rather than after, and keeps
it with the config. It is the same instinct as a change-management checklist and it survives for the
same reason: the questions are cheap and the answers are the design. A list produced by asking "what
would go wrong" beats one produced by asking "what shall we build".

**What degrades at scale.** Readiness lists get copied and stop being specific. Nine items about
*this* deployment are useful; the same nine copied to the next service are a form somebody fills in.
The durable version keeps the general questions — how does the credential arrive, what happens to a
half-finished deploy, who can call what — and re-answers them per system, because the answers are
what matter and the questions are just the prompt.

**The failure that only shows with real traffic.** Item 3, the credential. It is the only item on
this list whose absence stops the deployment working *at all*, and it is therefore the one that gets
solved fastest and worst — under pressure, on the day, by whatever means works. That is exactly how
a key ends up in a config file, which is where part
[2.3](../02-the-config/2.3-your-env-travels.md) started.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Do one, two and four this week — they are under an
hour and they are the difference between a config that validates and one that behaves. Put three and
six on somebody's calendar with a name against them, and get the allowance measured, because six
different decisions are now waiting on one afternoon's work."*

**The interview question:** *"You have a validated deployment config and you have never deployed it.
What is left?"* The answer that shows experience separates the items by what they need — a line of
code, a design decision, another person's time, or money — because the four categories are handled
completely differently and only one of them is engineering.

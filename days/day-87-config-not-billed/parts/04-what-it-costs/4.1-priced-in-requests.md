# 4.1 — Priced in requests, not money

## One-line answer

The desk's model traffic is countable and already twelve times the observed free-tier allowance,
while the platform's charge is a resource that bills by existing — a unit with no free-tier
equivalent — so the honest report has two halves and only one of them fits in this repository's
ledger.

## The story

The shared taxi and the car you own.

Two ways to get to work. The shared taxi costs a fixed amount each trip, so a week with three trips
costs three fares and a week with none costs nothing. The car costs insurance, road tax and a
parking space whether or not it moves, plus fuel when it does.

Ask which is cheaper and the honest answer is a question back: how often do you go? Below some
number of trips the taxi wins, above it the car does, and the crossing point is arithmetic anybody
can do in a minute.

What you cannot do is compare them in the same unit without deciding that unit first. A fare is
per-trip. Insurance is per-year-regardless. Adding them requires a conversion, and the conversion is
where every argument about this actually lives.

## The idea in plain language

Principle 15 says budgets here are denominated in **requests per minute and per day**, not in money.
That is not a stylistic choice; it is what a free tier is measured in, and the whole repository's
capacity planning — Day 78's schedule, Day 82's nightly, Day 83's criterion 6 — is arithmetic in
that unit.

The deployed desk has two costs and they are not in the same unit.

**The model half is requests.** The desk calls a model, once per turn, and turns are countable. This
half converts cleanly into the ledger, and the answer is uncomfortable before the platform is even
mentioned.

**The platform half is existence.** A managed resource is charged for being there. It does not go to
zero when nobody asks it anything, and the quiet hours cost what the busy ones cost. There is no
free-tier allowance to compare that against, because free tiers are expressed in calls and this is
not a call.

So the honest report is: one half measured, one half not expressible, and **saying so is the
result** rather than a gap in the work. Day 83 established the third exit code for exactly this
shape — a criterion that depends on a number nobody has, reported as neither pass nor fail — and
this part reuses it.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because a deployment decision that is priced in the wrong unit gets made on vibes. "The free tier is
generous" is what somebody says when the arithmetic has not been done, and Day 83's criterion 6
measured what that assumption looks like when written down: an assumed allowance produces a
comfortable-looking percentage from a number nobody checked.

And because the model half alone already fails, which is worth knowing before any platform
conversation starts. Deployment does not change the request count; it changes where the requests
come from.

## The mechanism

The load profile is written down rather than guessed — Day 78's weekday shape, fourteen slots from
before opening to after closing:

```python
ARRIVALS = (0, 0, 1, 4, 9, 14, 11, 6, 12, 15, 7, 2, 1, 0)

TURNS_PER_TICKET = 3  # a triage, a lookup, an answer
REQUESTS_PER_TURN = 1
```

**Line by line:**

- Fourteen slots with two zeros at each end. The zeros matter: they are what makes the profile spiky,
  and spikiness is the entire subject of this day's paper.
- `TURNS_PER_TICKET = 3` from the desk's actual shape — a triage, a lookup, an answer. Not an
  average anybody measured; a written-down assumption, which is why it is a named constant rather
  than a literal buried in an expression.
- `REQUESTS_PER_TURN = 1` is the optimistic case. Days 80 and 81 established that a judged evaluation
  multiplies this by ten; serving traffic does not, but a retry does, and this constant is where that
  would go.

And the two units, side by side, with the second one refusing to convert:

```python
    print(f"  {'the managed platform':<34}{'resource-days':<26}1 per day, always")
```

**Line by line:**

- `resource-days` is a deliberate unit invention, and it is honest about what it is: a resource,
  existing, for a day. It is not requests, it is not money, and writing it in the same table as
  requests is what makes the mismatch visible instead of arguable.
- `1 per day, always` — the word *always* is the finding. The number does not respond to the load
  profile above it, which is the whole difference between the two halves.

```bash
cd days/day-87-config-not-billed/lab
uv run python cost.py; echo "exit: $?"
```

**Line by line:**

- No flag: both halves, and the refusal at the end.

Measured on 2026-09-07:

```text
the desk's day: 82 tickets across 14 slots
peak slot: 15 tickets, quietest: 0

  what                              unit                      amount
  model calls                       requests                  246
  observed free-tier allowance      requests per day          20
  the desk against that             ratio                     12x
  the managed platform              resource-days             1 per day, always

  the platform's unit: one managed resource, billed while it exists
  it does not go down when the desk is quiet, and the two quiet slots at each end
  of the day cost exactly what the peak slot costs

  requests can be compared against an allowance. A resource that bills by existing
  has no free-tier equivalent to be compared against, so this repository cannot
  express the platform half of the bill at all.

  the model half fits in the ledger; the platform half is a different currency
exit: 2
```

**Two hundred and forty-six requests against an observed allowance of twenty.** That is twelve times
a whole day's measured capacity, and it is the *serving* cost of one ordinary weekday, before any
evaluation, any retry and any platform charge.

Exit `2`, not `1`. The platform half is not over budget; it is not in the same units as the budget.

## When it breaks

The arithmetic breaks by being completed anyway. Somebody needs a number, the platform half has
none, and a plausible one is supplied:

```bash
uv run python cost.py --model; echo "exit: $?"
```

Measured on 2026-09-07:

```text
  what                              unit                      amount
  model calls                       requests                  246
  observed free-tier allowance      requests per day          20
  the desk against that             ratio                     12x

  the model half is countable, and it already does not fit

exit: 0
```

That arm is honest — it says it is the model half — and it exits `0`, which is the danger. A
pipeline that runs `cost.py --model` gets a green light on a report that has silently dropped the
half nobody could price.

Three specific ways the model half itself is optimistic, and none of them raises anything:

**Retries are requests.** Day 78 recorded this repository's own refusal, and every retry against a
per-day quota is another request that will also be refused:

```text
429 RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED
generativelanguage.googleapis.com/generate_content_free_tier_requests
Please retry in ~53s
```

The `~53s` is the trap Day 78 part 1.1 dismantled: a per-day quota does not refill on that horizon,
so backoff spends requests waiting for something that will not happen until midnight.

**The allowance is shared.** Twenty a day is the whole key's allowance, not the desk's share of it.
Anything else on that key — a developer testing, an evaluation run, Day 82's nightly — is spending
from the same bucket.

**The observed number is one lane.** Twenty was measured on `gemini-2.5-flash` on 2026-08-25. The
desk is pinned to `gemini-2.5-flash-lite`, whose allowance this repository has never measured and
which Day 83's criterion 6 has now been blocked on for a sixth day.

## In production

**What a professional does.** Prices the two halves separately and names the crossing point.
Per-request and per-existence costs behave differently with traffic, so the useful artefact is not a
total but a break-even: below some volume the pay-per-call route wins, above it the always-on one
does. That number is a division, it takes a few minutes, and it converts an argument into a fact.

**What degrades at scale.** The two halves swap places. At low traffic the platform charge dominates
and per-call looks obviously right; at high traffic the per-call charge dominates and the always-on
resource is amortised across enough requests to be cheap. Any decision made at one end of that curve
and never revisited is wrong at the other end, and nothing announces the crossing.

**The failure that only shows with real traffic.** The resource nobody turned off. A managed resource
billing by existence keeps billing through the weekend, through the quiet month, and through the
six weeks after the project was quietly shelved — and unlike a per-call cost, there is no drop in
usage to notice. Every organisation with a cloud bill has some of these.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Do not put a total in the deck. Put the two units
side by side and the break-even volume underneath, because the only question anyone will actually
ask is whether we are above it."*

**The interview question:** *"What does it cost to run your agent?"* The answer that shows experience
separates per-request from per-existence before quoting anything, and can say which side of the
break-even the current traffic sits on. An answer that is a single number has averaged away the only
interesting part.

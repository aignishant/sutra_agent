# 4.4 — Criterion 3 — the phase fits a free-tier allowance

## One-line answer

This criterion has three outcomes rather than two, and today it returns the third — **cannot
determine**, exit code `2` — because one lane's demand is 60 requests a night against an allowance
nobody in this repository has ever measured.

## The story

The shopkeeper's credit book at the end of the month.

You buy on credit through the month and he writes it in the book. On the last day you come to settle
up. He turns to your page and adds it: so much for the rice, so much for the oil, so much for the
things the children were sent for. There is a number at the bottom and you pay it.

Now suppose the page for the second half of the month is missing. Not disputed — missing. The book
opens and there are entries up to the fourteenth and then the next page is the next customer's.

There are three things he can say, and only three. He can say *you owe this much* and show you the
page. He can say *you owe more than you have and we need to talk*. Or he can say *I do not know what
you owe, because half of it is not written down.*

The third answer is the honest one and it is also the one nobody wants to give, because it sounds like
incompetence and the other two sound like accounting. The temptation — and it is a real temptation, in
a shop and in a scheduler — is to round the missing page down to zero, present a number, and be
confident. That produces a bill that looks exactly like a correct one.

## The idea in plain language

Part [1.3](../01-the-allowance/1.3-counting-what-it-actually-spends.md) established that a lane's
verdict has three values: *fits*, *over*, and *cannot say*. This criterion is that comparison run as a
gate check, and the whole of its design is about refusing to collapse the third value into one of the
other two.

Its exit codes carry the three:

| Exit | Verdict | What it means | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `0` | PASS | every lane's measured allowance covers its demand | nothing |
| `1` | FAIL | a lane's demand exceeds its measured allowance | cut, shrink, defer or move lanes |
| `2` | CANNOT DETERMINE | a lane has no measured allowance | go and measure it |

The reason to spend a code on the third one — rather than reporting it as a failure and moving on — is
that **failure and ignorance need different people and different actions**. A failure is an
engineering problem: the numbers are known and somebody has to make a trade. Ignorance is a
measurement problem: nobody has to trade anything, somebody has to go and find out. Reporting both as
"red" sends the same alert to the same person for two situations that have nothing in common, and the
one that gets acted on is the one that looks like the more familiar problem.

Sutra has been here before and got it right once already. Day 74's free-quota check found three
outcomes rather than two — free, not free, and could not determine — and
[part 4.2 of that day](../../../day-74-live-api-streaming/parts/04-the-bill-for-voice/4.2-the-free-quota-check.md)
said that writing the third one down honestly is worth more than a confident guess at the first. This
criterion is the same finding with an exit code attached, which is what makes it enforceable rather
than admirable.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because it is the only criterion that tests the words *within free quota*, which is the half of the
gate sentence that no day in Phase 11 addressed. Days 73 to 77 built the machinery and each one
reported `0` provider requests, honestly, because every lab used scripted stand-ins. The *real* system
those labs describe spends, and nothing until now added it up.

It is also the criterion that Phase 12 is about to make worse. Addendum 02 puts the full 60-case eval
run on the nightly, which is precisely the lane this criterion cannot decide.

## The mechanism

The whole criterion is a loop with three branches:

```python
        want = demand(lane)
        if allowance is None:
            state, code = "cannot say", 2
            unknown.append(name)
        elif want <= allowance:
            state, code = "fits", 0
        else:
            state, code = "over", 1
        shown = "unknown" if allowance is None else str(allowance)
        print(f"  {name:<30}{want:>5} / {shown:<8}{state}")
        print(f"  {'':<30}{source}")
        worst = max(worst, code)
```

**Line by line:**

- The `None` branch is **first**, before any comparison, for the reason part
  [1.3](../01-the-allowance/1.3-counting-what-it-actually-spends.md) gave: the version people write is
  `allowance or 1000`, which is silent and reports *fits*.
- `worst = max(worst, code)` makes the exit code the **worst outcome across all lanes**, and the
  ordering `0 < 1 < 2` is a deliberate claim: *not knowing is worse than being over*. That is arguable
  and it is argued in the plain-language section above — a lane you are over on is a lane you can plan
  for tonight; a lane you cannot decide is one where you do not know whether tonight is a problem.
- `shown` is computed separately from `allowance` so the printed table can say the word `unknown` where
  a number would go. Printing `None` there would be technically accurate and would read, to somebody
  skimming, like a missing field rather than a finding.
- Every lane prints its **source** on the next line. That is what turns the table from an assertion
  into evidence: a reader can see that `20` came from a live 429 on a dated day and that the blank came
  from two pages that do not publish the number.

```bash

```

**Line by line:**

- No flag: every lane is compared against the allowance actually recorded for it, `None` included.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
  gemini/gemini-3.7-flash          62 / 20      over
                                read off a live 429, 2026-08-25 (docs/PACKAGES.md)
  gemini/gemini-2.5-flash-lite     60 / unknown cannot say
                                not published without an AI Studio session; not measured

  1 lane(s) could not be decided: gemini/gemini-2.5-flash-lite
  a schedule against an unmeasured allowance is a guess with a cron entry

  criterion 3: CANNOT DETERMINE
exit: 2
```

**Two rows, two different kinds of bad news, and the second is the one this criterion exists for.**

The first row is over by 42 and that is a normal engineering problem with normal engineering answers,
all of which are in section 3 of this day. The second row is not a problem with an answer. It is a
statement that a scheduled job has been committing sixty requests a night, every night since Day 73,
against a limit nobody has looked at — and that this has been true and invisible for six days because
no check existed that could say so.

Both provider pages that would answer it were fetched on 2026-09-06 while writing this day.
`ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/rate-limits` says the limits *"can be viewed in Google AI Studio"* and
publishes no free-tier table. `console.groq.com/docs/rate-limits` says the numbers are on the
signed-in limits page. Neither page failed to load. The answer is simply not in a place a script can
read, which is a fact about the world and not about this repository.

## When it breaks

The way this criterion breaks is not an exception. It is somebody supplying the missing number from
memory, because an unresolved verdict is uncomfortable and a plausible figure makes it go away.

```bash
uv run python c3_quota.py --assume-generous; echo "exit: $?"
```

**Line by line:**

- `--assume-generous` substitutes `1000` for any lane whose allowance is `None`. One thousand is chosen
  because it is the shape of number people actually reach for: round, comfortable, not obviously silly.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
  gemini/gemini-3.7-flash          62 / 20      over
                                read off a live 429, 2026-08-25 (docs/PACKAGES.md)
  gemini/gemini-2.5-flash-lite     60 / 1000    fits
                                assumed 1000/day - nobody measured this

  criterion 3: FAIL
exit: 1
```

**The unknown lane now says `fits`.** Sixty requests a night are declared safe by a comparison against
a number somebody made up, and the output is formatted identically to the real rows — same column, same
alignment, same vocabulary. The only thing separating a measurement from an invention in that table is
the sentence underneath it, which is why the sentence is printed.

And look at what happened to the criterion's overall verdict: it went from `CANNOT DETERMINE` to
`FAIL`. That is *more* definite than before. The assumption did not make anything green; it made the
gate more confident about a system it now understands less. A gate that becomes more decisive when you
add a guess is a gate whose verdicts have stopped being evidence.

The smallest fix, and it is not code: measure the allowance once, deliberately. Send requests to
Flash-Lite until refused, record the count and the date, put it in `docs/PACKAGES.md` next to the
2026-08-25 row that did exactly this for Flash. One controlled burn, one dated row, and this criterion
can answer.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead of a table of constants.** The allowance is discovered and cached
rather than typed. The provider's 429 body carries the quota name; some carry the limit itself. Where
they do not, a deliberate measurement run — scheduled once a quarter, at the start of a window, with
its own budget — produces the number and dates it. The important property is that the number in the
table always has a provenance and an age, and the checker can refuse to use one that is too old.

**What degrades at scale.** Allowances are per project, and the number you measured is *your share
minus everybody else's usage at the time you measured it*. Measure at 3am and you get a generous
figure; measure at noon and you get a pessimistic one. The honest table stores the measurement, the
time it was taken and what else was running, and treats the value as a lower bound.

**The failure that only shows with real traffic.** A lane that has always fitted stops fitting because
another team pointed a new service at the same project. Your demand did not change, your table did not
change, and your criterion still says PASS — because the criterion compares your plan against a number,
and neither of them knows about the other team. The only signal is the 429s, which means the criterion
must be paired with a runtime reconciliation: when the provider refuses a request the plan said was
affordable, the table is wrong and something should say so out loud.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Exit 2 for 'cannot determine' is right, and whatever
consumes this must not treat non-zero as one thing. Route 1 to the on-call rotation and 2 to whoever
owns the quota table — they are different jobs. Second: please make the assumed-value path impossible
rather than a flag. If somebody needs a placeholder they should have to write it into the table with a
date and their name on it, where a reviewer can see it."*

**The interview question:** *"Your capacity check can't get a number for one dependency. What do you
report?"* The answer that shows experience reports **unknown, distinctly**, and can defend why it is
not the same as a failure: because the follow-up action is different, the owner is different, and
folding the two together means the more familiar of the two always gets acted on first. A candidate who
says "assume a conservative value and move on" has just described the `--assume-generous` arm from the
other end, and has not noticed that a conservative assumption produces false alarms as reliably as a
generous one produces silence.

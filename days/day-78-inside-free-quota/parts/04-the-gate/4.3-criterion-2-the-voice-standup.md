# 4.3 — Criterion 2 — the voice standup delivers and can be stopped

## One-line answer

A spoken report is only delivered if the listener got the part that was for them **before** they
stopped listening, so the criterion asserts two things a transcript cannot: that the urgent lines came
first, and that stopping the report actually stops it.

## The story

The waiting room at a clinic, and the receptionist calling out token numbers.

She calls a number. You are half listening — you are on your phone, the fan is loud, somebody's child
is crying. If she calls forty-one and then reads out the full name, the address, the referring
doctor's name and the department, all in one go, the thing that was for you was the first two seconds
and everything after it was for the file.

Now the other half. Suppose you stand up and say *"sorry, that's me"* while she is still reading the
address. She stops. She does not finish reading out the address to an empty chair, and she does not
carry on reading it while walking you to the room. Stopping is a thing she does.

Both halves have to work for the room to function. If she buries the number at the end, you miss it
even though it was said. If she cannot be interrupted, the queue moves at the speed of the longest
record instead of the speed of people standing up.

## The idea in plain language

Criterion 2 tests the second claim in the gate sentence — *"voice standup"* — and it splits into two
assertions plus one that is about the criterion itself.

**The standup delivers what needed a person.** Day 77's `live.py` runs the standup over a live session
in which the listener speaks after two sentences, and reports how many of the sentences that need a
person were delivered before that. The assertion is `3 of 3`. This is
[Day 77 part 1.3](../../../day-77-the-standup-agent/parts/01-a-report-somebody-hears/1.3-what-the-listener-got.md)'s
measurement, turned into a check.

**The ablation still goes red.** The same day's queue-order arm exits `1`. Asserting that is not
decoration — it is the criterion checking that its own measurement can distinguish anything at all. If
somebody changes the standup so that both orderings score `3 of 3`, the first assertion is satisfied by
a system where ordering has stopped mattering, and only this second assertion notices.

**A live session reports interruption.** Day 75's `barge_in.py` runs a bidirectional session in which
the caller talks over the answer, and the assertion is that an `interrupted` event appears in the
stream.
[Day 75 part 2.2](../../../day-75-the-bidi-voice-loop/parts/02-talking-over/2.2-the-event-that-says-stop.md)
showed what its absence costs: 12 chunks against 3, the caller sitting through an answer they already
interrupted, with nothing anywhere reporting a problem.

Two of these are about **content ordering** and one is about **transport**, and they are in the same
criterion on purpose. The gate sentence says *voice standup*, and a standup delivered over a channel
that cannot be interrupted is not the thing Day 77 designed — it is a recorded message with extra
steps.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because the standup is the only output of Phase 11 that a human being experiences. The nightly writes
files. The re-index writes an index. This is the one thing that reaches a person, and part
[2.4](../02-asking-before-spending/2.4-the-job-that-ran-anyway.md) already showed how easily it can end
up reporting on a night that did not happen.

It also protects a property that is very easy to lose accidentally. `lines()` returns sentences in an
order, and any future change — a new section, a reworded summary, a different way of describing the
queue — can move the flagged lines without anybody noticing, because the report will still contain all
the facts. Day 77's build brief asks for exactly one assertion, `lines(...)[0][1] is True`, and this
criterion is the end-to-end version of it: not "the function returns the right order" but "the listener
actually received it".

## The mechanism

The criterion drives two labs from two different days:

```python
DAYS = Path(__file__).resolve().parents[2]
STANDUP = DAYS / "day-77-the-standup-agent" / "lab"
VOICE = DAYS / "day-75-the-bidi-voice-loop" / "lab"


def run(lab: Path, script: str, *args: str) -> subprocess.CompletedProcess[str]:
    return subprocess.run([sys.executable, script, *args], cwd=lab, capture_output=True, text=True)
```

**Line by line:**

- Two labs, one runner. The `lab` argument is a parameter rather than a module constant because this
  criterion genuinely spans two days — the standup is Day 77's and the transport property is Day 75's,
  and pretending otherwise would mean copying one of them.
- `capture_output=True` here is doing more than tidying: the assertions read the child's **stdout**, so
  it has to be captured rather than inherited.

The three assertions:

```python
    live = run(STANDUP, "live.py", *(["--flat"] if break_it else []))
    delivered = "3 of 3 delivered" in live.stdout
```

**Line by line:**

- `"3 of 3 delivered" in live.stdout` is a **substring match on a printed line**, which is the weakest
  form of assertion in this whole day and should be named as such. It is used because the property
  being tested belongs to Day 77's lab and this criterion must not reach inside it. The honest fix is
  for the lab to emit a machine-readable line — a JSON summary — and part
  [5.2](../05-in-production/5.2-what-a-real-scheduler-adds.md) lists it. Until then the exit code does
  the real work and the substring is a second opinion.
- `*(["--flat"] if break_it else [])` is the fault injection: with `--break-it`, the criterion runs the
  **queue-order** arm as though it were the real standup. That is a faithful simulation of the change a
  future commit would make — somebody reorders `lines()` for a reason that seemed good — and the
  criterion must notice.

```python
    flat = run(STANDUP, "live.py", "--flat")
    print(f"  the ablation goes red    exit {flat.returncode} (queue order)")
    if flat.returncode == 0:
        failures.append("queue order exited 0, so this criterion checks nothing")
```

**Line by line:**

- This block asserts that the **bad** arm fails. It is the only assertion in the five criteria that is
  about the measuring instrument rather than about the system, and its failure message says so in
  words: *so this criterion checks nothing*.
- It runs `--flat` unconditionally, in both the normal and the fault-injected pass, which means under
  `--break-it` the same command is run twice with different expectations. That is not a bug: the first
  invocation is standing in for the real standup and the second is checking the ablation, and the fact
  that they are the same command is exactly what makes `--break-it` a red-alarm test.

Run it:

```bash
cd days/day-78-inside-free-quota/lab
uv run python c2_voice.py; echo "exit: $?"
```

**Line by line:**

- No flag: the real standup, the ablation, and the barge-in session, in that order.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
  standup over a session   exit 0, all urgent lines delivered: yes
  the ablation goes red    exit 1 (queue order)
  the listener interrupts  exit 0, interrupted event: yes

  criterion 2: PASS
exit: 0
```

Three green lines, and the middle one is the one that makes the other two mean anything.

## When it breaks

Inject the fault — reorder the standup so the queue is read out in the order it happens to sit in:

```bash
uv run python c2_voice.py --break-it; echo "exit: $?"
```

**Line by line:**

- `--break-it` makes the criterion's primary arm run `live.py --flat`. Every fact the standup contains
  is still there; only the order changed.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
  standup over a session   exit 1, all urgent lines delivered: no
  the ablation goes red    exit 1 (queue order)
  the listener interrupts  exit 0, interrupted event: yes
    - the standup did not deliver every line that needs a person

  criterion 2: FAIL
exit: 1
```

The failure text is worth reading against what actually happened, from Day 77's own measurement: the
queue-order standup **called all three tools, read the queue correctly, and said only true things**.
Nothing in it is wrong. The listener spoke after two sentences and had received `0 of 2` of the lines
that needed them. There is no error in that system to find, which is why the criterion has to assert on
delivery rather than on correctness — a check that the standup contains the right facts passes both
arms.

The transport half has its own failure and it looks completely different. If the `interrupted` event
stops arriving, the third line reads:

```text
  the listener interrupts  exit 0, interrupted event: no
    - the session did not report an interruption
```

Exit `0` with `interrupted event: no` is the shape to notice: the session ran fine and ended cleanly.
Day 75 measured what that costs — the caller hears 12 chunks where 3 were wanted — and none of it
raises.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead of a substring match.** The thing under test emits a structured
result — one JSON line with `urgent_total`, `urgent_delivered`, `interrupted` — and the criterion parses
it. The reason is not elegance. A substring assertion breaks when somebody improves the wording, which
means the criterion fails for a reason that has nothing to do with the property, which means somebody
weakens the criterion to make it pass.

**What degrades at scale.** "Delivered before the listener stopped" is measured here against a scripted
listener who speaks after exactly two sentences. Real listeners stop at wildly different points, and a
report that is correct for a two-sentence listener may be wrong for a one-sentence one. The honest
version measures the **position** of the last urgent line and asserts a bound on it, rather than
asserting a single scripted outcome — which is
[Day 77 part 2.3](../../../day-77-the-standup-agent/parts/02-the-order-is-the-product/2.3-words-before-it-matters.md)'s
argument, and the reason that part measured words rather than counting sentences.

**The failure that only shows with real traffic.** The barge-in test uses a scripted connection. A real
one has network delay between the listener speaking and the event arriving, so the client keeps playing
audio for a moment after the interruption — and whether that moment is 80 milliseconds or 800 decides
whether the system feels responsive or rude. Nothing offline measures it.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Criterion 2 asserts on printed text from two other
labs. That is fine for now, but the first time somebody rewords a print statement this will go red for
no reason and somebody will delete the assertion. Have the labs print one JSON line and parse that. And
keep the ablation check — it is the only line here that would catch us accidentally making the ordering
irrelevant."*

**The interview question:** *"How do you test that a spoken or streamed interface is any good, rather
than just correct?"* The answer that shows experience measures **when** things arrive relative to when
the user stops paying attention, not whether they arrive at all — and can name the cheap proxy they used
and its limits, because there is no honest way to test this without admitting the proxy.

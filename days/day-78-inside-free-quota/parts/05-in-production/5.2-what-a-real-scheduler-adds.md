# 5.2 — What a real quota-aware scheduler adds

## One-line answer

The lab admits, prioritises and degrades correctly, and the distance between it and something you
would put in front of a production quota is not vague hardening — it is nine specific things, each
cheap on the day the scheduler is written and expensive on the day it is needed, and two of them this
curriculum deliberately parks rather than forgets.

## The story

The service checklist taped inside the door of a small workshop.

It did not start as a checklist. It started as a mechanic writing one line on a card after a customer
came back angry: *check the coolant is actually filled, not just topped up.* Then another line after a
different complaint. Then a line after the day two cars went out with the same tyre pressure wrong,
because the gauge was reading low and nobody had checked the gauge.

Ten years later there are nineteen lines on the card and every single one of them is a scar. Read as a
list it looks like bureaucracy. Read line by line it is the memory of the shop, and the reason it is
taped to the door rather than kept in a folder is that it is only useful before the work, not after.

The interesting thing about a card like that is that you cannot derive it. Nobody sat down and reasoned
their way to line eleven. It arrived because a specific thing went wrong on a specific Tuesday.

## The idea in plain language

The lab in this day does four things correctly: it prices a schedule, it admits runs against a
reservation, it orders them by a stated priority, and it degrades with a named strategy. Those four are
the mechanism, and they are genuinely the hard part conceptually.

They are not the whole job. What follows is the card taped to the door: nine things a production
quota-aware scheduler has that this one does not, each one named, each one priceable, and two of them
marked 🅿️ — parked, understood, deliberately not built here.

**1. The plan is generated from the schedule, or checked against it.**
Part [1.2](../01-the-allowance/1.2-a-schedule-is-a-spending-plan.md) named the silent drift: `_phase.py`
is a fixture and the crontab is the truth, and nothing connects them. Either the job list emits the
schedule, or a check reads the schedule and fails on a job it has never heard of. This is first because
every other number on this list is worthless if the list of jobs is incomplete.

**2. Costs come from the run log, not from constants.**
Day 73 already writes `state/runs.jsonl`. Adding a request count per run turns every cost estimate into
a measurement, and lets the planner report *planned versus observed*. The day those two diverge is the
day something changed that nobody committed.

**3. Reservations expire.**
Part [2.2](../02-asking-before-spending/2.2-the-reservation-not-the-check.md) showed that adding a
reservation creates a new failure — a run that dies holding one blocks everything after it, exactly as
Day 73's unreleased lock did. A timestamp on the reservation and a sweep that reclaims anything older
than twice the longest observed run is the cheap version; reconciling against Day 60's event log is the
correct one.

**4. The ledger's increment is atomic.**
The lab reads, compares and writes in three steps, which is a race that only the lab's single-process
structure hides. Redis `INCRBY` with `EXPIRE` set to the window, or an `UPDATE ... WHERE` that does the
comparison in the database, makes the check and the take one operation. This is the one item on the
list that is not optional the moment two processes exist.

**5. The window comes from the provider, not from a calendar.**
Part [2.3](../02-asking-before-spending/2.3-whose-midnight-resets-it.md) measured 10 of 12 requests
refused by a scheduler that reset at its own midnight. Where the provider returns a reset header, key
the ledger on that. Where it does not, key on UTC and say in a comment that it is a decision rather
than a fact.

**6. A 429 that the ledger said was affordable is logged as a contradiction.**
Part [5.1](5.1-the-nightly-competes-with-the-day.md) is why: the ledger is a lower bound on the
project's spending, and the only evidence you will ever get that somebody else is on the project is a
refusal your own accounting says is impossible. Logging it with the ledger's belief attached costs one
line and is the difference between a mystery and a finding.

**7. Start times are scattered.**
Every quota-limited job in a fleet waits for the same window boundary and then starts at once, so the
allowance is consumed in the first seconds by whoever is fastest. A random offset of a few minutes on
each job's start is the same jitter Day 72's backoff uses, applied to the window instead of to the
retry.

**8. 🅿️ Parked — a hosted scheduler with quota integration.**
Cloud Scheduler, Cloud Tasks and their equivalents will hold a rate, retry with a policy, and record
what they dispatched. They are the right answer at scale and every one of them needs a billing account,
so this curriculum uses the operating system's scheduler instead — the same decision
[Day 73 part 4.1](../../../day-73-ambient-agents/parts/04-what-wakes-it/4.1-what-adk-offers.md) took
when it found ADK's `trigger_sources` accepts exactly `pubsub` and `eventarc`, both hosted. Know the
shape, know what it buys, do not build it here.

**9. 🅿️ Parked — measuring the allowance by spending it.**
The honest way to fill in part [4.4](../04-the-gate/4.4-criterion-3-the-phase-fits.md)'s `cannot say`
is a controlled burn: send requests at the start of a window until refused, record the number and the
date. It is a few dozen requests and one afternoon, and it is parked here because it would consume the
whole day's free allowance for a curriculum that has to keep working tomorrow. It is the first thing a
real project would do, and it is written down as a `TODO(me)` in the hub rather than pretended away.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because the gate came back with three findings and two of them are on this list. Criterion 3 cannot
determine a lane because item 9 has not been done. Criterion 4 has reported the same missing ledger row
at four gates, which is item 1's problem in a different file — a record and a reality with nothing
connecting them.

And because Phase 12 starts tomorrow with the largest scheduled consumer this curriculum will build.
Day 82 puts the eval suite on the nightly. Items 1, 2 and 5 are the ones that decide whether that job's
cost is known, tracked and spent in the right window, and all three are cheaper to add now than after
the suite has grown to four hundred cases.

## The mechanism

The list above is the mechanism, and the honest thing this part can add is what each item costs, so the
list can be argued with rather than admired:

| # | Item | Where it lands | Roughly |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | plan generated from or checked against the schedule | `sutra/schedule.py` + a check in `./m check` | half a day |
| 2 | costs read from `runs.jsonl` | Day 73's nightly + the planner | a couple of hours |
| 3 | reservations expire | `admit.py`'s ledger | an hour, plus a decision about the timeout |
| 4 | atomic increment | swap the JSON file for Redis or a table | an hour, plus an operational dependency |
| 5 | window from the provider | wherever the ledger key is computed | an hour, mostly reading response headers |
| 6 | contradiction logging | the client's 429 branch | fifteen minutes |
| 7 | scattered start times | the schedule generator | fifteen minutes |
| 8 | 🅿️ hosted scheduler | not built | needs a billing account |
| 9 | 🅿️ measured allowance | one controlled burn, then a `PACKAGES.md` row | one afternoon and a day's quota |

**Read the right-hand column against the left.** Items 6 and 7 together are half an hour, and they are
the two that turn an invisible failure into a visible one and a thundering herd into a spread. Item 4
is an hour of code and a permanent operational dependency, which is why it is the one people defer —
and it is the one that is not optional the moment a second process exists.

The two parked items are parked for different reasons and it matters which. Item 8 is parked because it
costs money, which is Addendum 02 and is not going to change. Item 9 is parked because it costs
**quota**, which is a decision about today rather than a permanent one — a real project does it in its
first week.

## When it breaks

The list itself has a failure mode, and it is the same one the workshop's card has: it gets read as a
list of nice-to-haves and none of it is done, because no single item is ever the most urgent thing.

The observable version of that is a scheduler that works for a year and then fails in a way that
touches four items at once. A concrete sequence, and every step of it has appeared in this day:

```text
1. someone adds a job to the crontab and not to the plan      (item 1)
2. the plan still reports 62/day; the reality is 78/day       (item 2)
3. the new job's run dies holding a reservation               (item 3)
4. every run afterwards is refused by our own ledger          (item 3)
5. nothing logs a contradiction, because nothing was refused
   by the provider - we refused ourselves                     (item 6)
6. the morning digest says: evals: 10 of 10 passed            (part 3.2)
```

Six steps, no error message anywhere, and the last line is reassuring. That sequence is why the list is
a list rather than a paragraph: it is not one failure, it is four gaps composing.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead of nine improvements to a lab.** They usually do not write a
scheduler at all. They take one that exists — a workflow engine, a hosted scheduler, the platform's
cron — and add the two things it will not have: the **cost model** and the **priority order**. Those
two are domain knowledge and no off-the-shelf tool can supply them; everything else on this list is
plumbing somebody has already built and debugged.

**What degrades at scale.** Every item on this list is cheap while there are five jobs and painful at
fifty. Item 1 in particular flips: with five jobs a person can eyeball the plan against the crontab,
so nobody builds the check, and by the time there are fifty the drift is already there and nobody knows
how long it has been.

**The failure that only shows with real traffic.** Items 3, 4 and 7 are all invisible with one process
and one job. The lab passes every check in this day precisely because it runs one thing at a time in
one process. Concurrency is not a stress case for a scheduler; it is the ordinary case, and a
scheduler tested serially has been tested in a mode it will never run in.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Good list. Do 6 and 7 this week — half an hour
between them and they turn our worst failure mode from silent into loud. Do 4 before the second
process, not after. And put 9 on somebody's calendar with a date: 'cannot determine' is an acceptable
answer once, and it is not an acceptable answer at the next gate."*

**The interview question:** *"What is missing from a quota-aware scheduler that checks its budget
before each run?"* The answer that shows experience does not list features. It asks what happens when
the check is right and the request is refused anyway — because that question exposes whether the
candidate has understood that the ledger is a belief about somebody else's system, and everything on
this list follows from taking that seriously.

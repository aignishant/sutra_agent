# 3.1 — Three things a job can do when it cannot afford itself

## One-line answer

There are exactly three honest answers to *I cannot afford all of me* — **skip**, **shrink** and
**defer** — they are not interchangeable, and choosing between them is a decision about what the job
is for rather than a technical detail.

## The story

Eight people are coming to eat and there is rice for five.

You realise this at half past six, with an hour to go, and there are only three things you can
actually do about it.

You can **not cook the rice** and make something else the centre of the meal — more of the dal, an
extra vegetable, bread from the shop at the corner. Everybody eats. Nobody eats rice. It is a
different meal and it is a complete one.

You can **cook the five people's worth** and serve smaller portions, and say so as you put it down:
*there is less rice than usual tonight, take a little and have more of the rest.* Everybody eats
something. Everybody knows they are eating a reduced version.

Or you can **send somebody to the shop** and eat half an hour later than planned, complete.

What you cannot do is cook rice for five, serve it to eight, and say nothing. That is not a fourth
option. That is one of the first three with the announcement removed — and it is the one that ends
with three people quietly not having any and everybody else assuming they did.

## The idea in plain language

A job that cannot afford its full run has three shapes available, and each one trades a different
thing away.

**Skip.** Do not run at all. Report that you did not run.
The full cost is zero and the full value is zero. What this preserves is **consistency**: nothing
half-done exists, no partial index, no state that is neither yesterday's nor today's. What it costs is
a whole cycle of whatever the job was for.

**Shrink.** Run a smaller version of the same work. Report the size.
Costs what the smaller version costs, and delivers a *proportional* fraction of the value — sometimes.
Whether that fraction is useful depends entirely on whether the work is **homogeneous**: shrinking an
index from a thousand documents to a hundred gives you a hundred documents' worth of index, which is
genuinely a hundred documents' worth of use. Shrinking an eval suite from sixty cases to ten gives you
ten cases' worth of *confidence*, which is not a tenth of sixty cases' worth of confidence, because the
failures are not evenly spread — part [3.2](3.2-the-shrunken-run-that-reports-as-full.md) measures
exactly this.

**Defer.** Do the part you can afford now and queue the rest for the next window.
Costs the same as shrink today and the remainder tomorrow, so total value is preserved — but only for
work whose **value survives being late**. A re-index that finishes tomorrow is a re-index. A standup
delivered tomorrow morning is not a standup; it is yesterday's news, and
[Day 77 part 4.1](../../../day-77-the-standup-agent/parts/04-in-production/4.1-what-a-standup-costs.md)
priced why: a listener's attention is spent per morning and does not carry over.

The property that decides between them is **what happens to the value if this work is late or partial**,
and it is a property of the job, not of the quota:

| | Skip | Shrink | Defer |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cost this window | 0 | what fits | what fits |
| Value this window | 0 | a fraction, honestly reported | a fraction |
| Total value eventually | lost | lost | preserved |
| Needs | nothing | homogeneous work | a queue that survives a restart |
| Wrong for | work with a deadline | work whose value is not proportional | work that goes stale |

The fourth option — run a reduced version and describe it as a full one — is not on this table because
it is not a strategy. It is any of the three with the reporting removed, and part
[3.2](3.2-the-shrunken-run-that-reports-as-full.md) is entirely about it.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because part [2.4](../02-asking-before-spending/2.4-the-job-that-ran-anyway.md) established that
something in Phase 11 is going to be refused, and "refused" is not yet a behaviour. It is the absence
of one. A job that is refused and has no strategy simply does not run and does not say so, which is
the accidental *skip* — the right answer sometimes, arrived at by not thinking.

Each of Phase 11's jobs wants a different one, and the day cannot end without somebody choosing:

- **`reindex`** is homogeneous and stale-tolerant, so **defer** fits: index what you can, queue the
  rest, and the corpus is complete by tomorrow.
- **`evals`** is homogeneous in cost and **not** homogeneous in value, so shrink is available but
  dangerous, and Addendum 02's guidance for days 79–83 — 10 to 15 cases in development, the full 60
  only at gates — is exactly a shrink strategy with a stated size.
- **`standup`** goes stale in hours and costs one request, so it should never be the thing that is
  cut. That is `PRIORITY` from part [2.4](../02-asking-before-spending/2.4-the-job-that-ran-anyway.md),
  and this is why.

## The mechanism

All three strategies are the same four numbers computed differently. From `lab/degrade.py`:

```python
def run_cases(first: int, count: int) -> tuple[int, int]:
    """(passed, failed) over cases first..first+count-1, one request each."""
    chosen = range(first, min(first + count, TOTAL_CASES + 1))
    failed = sum(1 for c in chosen if c in FAILING)
    return len(chosen) - failed, failed
```

**Line by line:**

- `first` and `count` rather than a single `n`. Deferring needs to resume from where the last window
  stopped, so the function has to be able to run cases 11 to 60 as well as 1 to 10. A signature that
  only took a count would force the defer strategy to be written twice.
- `min(first + count, TOTAL_CASES + 1)` clamps at the end of the suite, so asking for more cases than
  exist returns what exists rather than raising. That is the correct behaviour here because the caller
  is asking "how much can I do", not "give me exactly this".
- `c in FAILING` is a set membership test against a fixed set of case numbers. Nothing is random and
  nothing is generated — the same six cases fail on every run, which is what makes the two arms of
  part [3.2](3.2-the-shrunken-run-that-reports-as-full.md) comparable.
- It returns a pair rather than printing, so the same function serves all three strategies and the
  reporting is somebody else's job. That separation is what part
  [3.2](3.2-the-shrunken-run-that-reports-as-full.md) then goes on to break on purpose.

And the strategy selection itself:

```python
if strategy == "skip":
    ran, passed, failed, deferred = 0, 0, 0, 0
elif strategy == "defer":
    ran = AFFORDABLE
    passed, failed = run_cases(1, AFFORDABLE)
    deferred = TOTAL_CASES - AFFORDABLE
else:
    ran = AFFORDABLE
    passed, failed = run_cases(1, AFFORDABLE)
    deferred = 0
```

**Line by line:**

- `skip` sets everything to zero **including `ran`**, which is what makes it different from a shrink
  to size zero: there is no run, so there is nothing to report about, and the digest line will say so
  in those words.
- `defer` and `shrink` execute **identical** work — the same `run_cases(1, AFFORDABLE)` — and differ
  only in `deferred`. That is not laziness in the lab; it is the truth about these two strategies. They
  are the same run with different bookkeeping, and the bookkeeping is the entire difference between
  losing fifty cases and postponing them.
- `AFFORDABLE` is a module constant standing in for what the ledger would return. Wiring it to
  `admit.reserve()` is left for the build brief, because the interesting decision is which branch to
  take, not where the number comes from.

Run each in turn:

```bash
cd days/day-78-inside-free-quota/lab
uv run python degrade.py --strategy skip
uv run python degrade.py --strategy defer
uv run python degrade.py
```

**Line by line:**

- No `--strategy` means `shrink`, which is the default because it is the one people reach for without
  naming it.

Measured on 2026-09-06, the skip arm:

```text
the eval stage wants 60 requests and has 10
strategy: skip

  the digest line this produces:

    evals: not run, 60 cases unchecked - the window had 10

  the truth over all 60 cases: 54 passed, 6 failed
  failures inside the 0 cases that ran: 0
  failures nobody looked at: 6
```

The defer arm:

```text
strategy: defer

  the digest line this produces:

    evals: 10 of 10 passed, 50 cases queued for the next window

  the truth over all 60 cases: 54 passed, 6 failed
  failures inside the 10 cases that ran: 0
  failures nobody looked at: 6
```

And the shrink arm:

```text
strategy: shrink

  the digest line this produces:

    evals: 10 of 10 passed, 50 of 60 cases NOT RUN

  the truth over all 60 cases: 54 passed, 6 failed
  failures inside the 10 cases that ran: 0
  failures nobody looked at: 6
```

**All three produce a different sentence and the same last number.** Six failures were not looked at,
whichever strategy you chose, because none of them can conjure requests that do not exist. What
changes between the three is entirely what the person reading the digest is told — and notice that the
**skip** arm, which did the least work, produces the sentence that leaves the reader least misled. It
says *60 cases unchecked* out loud. The shrink arm has to work harder to be as honest, because it has
a number that looks like a result.

## When it breaks

**Deferring needs a queue, and a queue is state.** `deferred = TOTAL_CASES - AFFORDABLE` is a number in
a variable that dies with the process. For a real defer, the remaining case IDs must be somewhere the
next run can find them, which means a file, which means everything Day 60 said about writing files that
survive a crash. A defer implemented as a variable is a shrink that has told the reader it is a defer,
and that is worse than a shrink — the reader now believes those fifty cases are coming.

**Skipping repeatedly looks identical to skipping once.** A job that skips tonight because the window
was tight, and tomorrow, and the night after, produces the same single line each time. Nothing in that
line accumulates, so a suite that has not been run for nine days reads exactly like a suite that missed
last night. Whatever records the skip needs to record **how many in a row**, and that number needs to
be in the digest — this is
[Day 73 part 3.3](../../../day-73-ambient-agents/parts/03-the-morning-after/3.3-nobody-notices-silence.md)'s
finding again: three of the four artefacts contain no date, so this morning's digest and one from a
fortnight ago are the same file.

**Shrinking changes what a green result means, and nothing in the type system notices.** `passed=10,
failed=0` is the same shape of answer as `passed=60, failed=0`. Any dashboard, any alert rule, any
comparison against yesterday will treat them as the same kind of thing. That is part
[3.2](3.2-the-shrunken-run-that-reports-as-full.md), and it is where this section ends up.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead of a branch in the job.** The strategy is a **property of the
job**, declared next to its schedule and its cost — the same row part
[1.2](../01-the-allowance/1.2-a-schedule-is-a-spending-plan.md) argued for. Not an `if` buried in the
eval runner. The reason is that the strategy has to be reviewable by somebody who is not reading the
job's source, and because two jobs in the same schedule will want different ones.

**What degrades at scale.** Deferral queues grow. A job that defers 50 cases a night and can afford 10
a night never catches up; it just accumulates a backlog that gets reported every morning as *queued for
the next window* and is never actually run. The queue needs a policy of its own — a maximum age after
which deferred work is dropped and *reported as dropped*, or the queue quietly becomes a list of things
nobody will ever check.

**The failure that only shows with real traffic.** Shrinking picks a subset, and every cheap way of
picking one is biased. Take the first N and you get the oldest cases, which are the ones written when
the system was simplest. Take a random N and your result is not comparable to yesterday's. Take the N
that ran fastest and you have systematically excluded the expensive cases, which are usually the
interesting ones. There is no unbiased cheap subset, and the honest thing is to name the bias in the
report rather than to pretend the sample is representative.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Good that there are three named strategies rather
than one implicit one. Two things. Put the strategy in the job's config next to the schedule, not in
this function — I want to see it in review without opening the runner. And your defer keeps the
remainder in a local variable, so it is not a defer. Either write the queue to disk or call it a shrink;
what it must not do is tell the morning report that fifty cases are coming when they are not."*

**The interview question:** *"Your nightly job can only afford a fraction of its work. What are the
options?"* An answer that names three, and then says which one each kind of work wants and why, is an
answer from somebody who has had to make the choice. The tell is whether they mention what the report
says in each case — because the strategies differ far more in what they communicate than in what they
compute.

# 2.3 — Whose midnight resets it

## One-line answer

The window an allowance is counted in belongs to the provider, not to your machine — and a scheduler
that starts a fresh day at its own midnight spends against a balance that does not exist yet:
measured here as **10 of 12 requests refused** on a job that believed it had the full allowance.

## The story

You buy a monthly bus pass on the fourteenth.

You use it happily. On the thirteenth of the next month you get on the bus as usual, hold up the pass,
and the conductor shakes his head. You look at it. You have had it for twenty-nine days. You are
certain there is a day left.

There is not, and the reason is printed on the pass: it is valid **for the calendar month**, not for
thirty days from purchase. It expired at the end of the month, two weeks after you bought it. You
bought two weeks of a month-long pass and used it as though you had bought a month.

Nothing was hidden from you. The pass said what it was. What happened is that you had a completely
reasonable model of what "monthly" means — thirty days, starting when I paid — and the people who
issue the pass have a different one, and yours never came into contact with theirs until the moment it
cost you.

The conductor is not interested in your model. His machine knows one date.

## The idea in plain language

A daily allowance is counted over a **window**, and a window has a start. Somebody chose that start,
and it was not you.

Three ways it is commonly chosen, and they behave differently:

- **Calendar day in a fixed timezone.** The allowance resets at midnight in that zone — often UTC,
  sometimes the provider's home timezone. Everybody's window resets at the same instant.
- **Rolling window.** The allowance covers the last day counted back from *now*, so it never resets;
  it drains continuously as old requests age out.
- **Anniversary of your account.** The window starts when you signed up. Two accounts have different
  midnights.

Your scheduler also has a day, and it is almost always the machine's **local** calendar day, because
that is what `date`, `cron` and every timestamp default to. If those two days do not line up — and
they line up only by accident — then there is a stretch of every day during which your ledger and the
provider's ledger disagree about which day it is.

The size of that stretch is the timezone offset. This repository is written on a machine at UTC+05:30,
so the two disagree for **five and a half hours out of every twenty-four**. And here is the part that
makes it a real bug rather than a curiosity: the disagreement is not symmetrical. During those hours
your scheduler thinks it is *tomorrow* and the provider thinks it is *still today*, so your ledger says
zero spent and the provider's says eighteen. You are not slightly out. You believe you have the entire
allowance at the exact moment you have almost none of it.

Worse, this is precisely when scheduled work runs. Nobody schedules a nightly job for 3pm. They
schedule it just after midnight — which is to say, they schedule it inside the window of maximum
disagreement.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because Day 73's nightly wakes at 00:30 local. That is the crontab line the day wrote, and it is a
sensible line: the ticket queue is quiet, nobody is waiting, and the work has all night to finish.

It is also 19:00 UTC the previous day. If the provider counts in UTC — and the one number this
repository has measured came from a quota named
`generativelanguage.googleapis.com/generate_content_free_tier_requests`, which is not a name that
suggests it knows where the caller lives — then Day 73's nightly runs **at the end of the provider's
day, not the start of it**, on whatever the daytime desk has left over.

That single fact reverses the intuition the whole phase was built on. "The night is free" is not a
property of the night. It is a claim about a window, and the window is somebody else's.

## The mechanism

Two functions, and the entire bug is which one is used as a dictionary key:

```python
def provider_day(moment: datetime) -> str:
    """The provider counts in UTC calendar days. That is the window that matters."""
    return moment.astimezone(timezone.utc).strftime("%Y-%m-%d")


def local_day(moment: datetime) -> str:
    return moment.astimezone(LOCAL).strftime("%Y-%m-%d")
```

**Line by line:**

- Both take an **aware** `datetime` — one that carries a timezone — and convert before formatting.
  `datetime.now()` with no timezone gives a naive object, `astimezone` on it silently assumes local
  time, and the whole bug becomes invisible because there is nothing to compare.
- `strftime("%Y-%m-%d")` throws the time away deliberately. The key is a *day*, and a key that
  included the hour would never collide, which is exactly what a window key is supposed to do.
- The two functions are one line each and differ by one identifier. That is the point worth staring
  at: this bug is not the result of complicated code. It is the result of `datetime.now().date()`
  being the obvious thing to type.

And the choice:

```python
keyed_by = local_day if use_local else provider_day
ledger = {provider_day(wake): ALREADY_SPENT}
key = keyed_by(wake)
believed = ledger.get(key, 0)
```

**Line by line:**

- `ledger` is keyed by `provider_day`, always. That is not a choice the scheduler makes — it is
  modelling the provider's own counter, which is keyed however the provider keys it.
- `key = keyed_by(wake)` is the scheduler's key, and it is the only line the `--local` flag changes.
- `ledger.get(key, 0)` is where the damage is done, and it is done by the **default**. A key that is
  not in the ledger returns `0`, and `0` means "nothing spent yet today", which is the most optimistic
  possible reading of "I have never seen this day before". A `KeyError` here would have been a
  friendlier bug.

Run the correct arm:

```bash
cd days/day-78-inside-free-quota/lab
uv run python window.py; echo "exit: $?"
```

**Line by line:**

- No flag keys the ledger by the provider's window, which is the same key the provider is using.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
the nightly wakes at 2026-09-07T00:30:00+05:30
  that instant in UTC is 2026-09-06T19:00:00+00:00
  local calendar day    2026-09-07
  provider window       2026-09-06

the scheduler keys its ledger by the provider day
  its key for this run is 2026-09-06, and it believes 18 requests are spent
  the provider's count for 2026-09-06 is 18

  the nightly asks for 12 requests believing it has 2 left
  it actually has 2 left
  requests refused with 429: 0
exit: 0
```

Now key it by the machine's own day:

```bash
uv run python window.py --local; echo "exit: $?"
```

**Line by line:**

- `--local` changes one identifier — `keyed_by` becomes `local_day`. The clock, the allowance, the
  desk's spending and the nightly's cost are all identical.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
the scheduler keys its ledger by the local day
  its key for this run is 2026-09-07, and it believes 0 requests are spent
  the provider's count for 2026-09-06 is 18

  the nightly asks for 12 requests believing it has 20 left
  it actually has 2 left
  requests refused with 429: 10
exit: 1
```

**Read the two `believes` lines together.** In the first run the scheduler believes 18 are spent and
18 are spent. In the second it believes 0 are spent and 18 are spent — and it did not get that wrong
by miscounting. It counted perfectly, in the wrong book. Its ledger for `2026-09-07` is genuinely
empty, because `2026-09-07` has not started yet as far as the provider is concerned.

Ten of the twelve requests come back 429. The nightly's re-index stage is now half done — which is the
state Day 73's resume was built for, so nothing is lost — but the useful observation is what the
**backoff** does with those ten refusals. Day 72's ladder waits and retries, and the window that would
make the retry succeed does not open for another five and a half hours. Every retry is refused. The
job spends the whole night waiting for a midnight that has already happened in the wrong place.

```text
UTC          00:00        03:30 ....... 14:30        19:00        24:00
             |------------- provider window: 06 Sep -------------------|
                           [ desk, 12 runs, 48 req ]  [nightly, 12 req]

local        05:30        09:00 ....... 20:00        00:30        05:30
(UTC+05:30)  |--- machine's 06 Sep ----------------|--- 07 Sep ---------
```

The nightly's slot sits inside the provider's *06 Sep* window and inside the machine's *07 Sep*. That
overlap is the bug, and it is five and a half hours wide every single day.

## When it breaks

The provider does not tell you which window it used. What comes back is Day 2's recorded refusal:

```text
429 RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED
generativelanguage.googleapis.com/generate_content_free_tier_requests
Please retry in ~53s
```

There is no window in that message. No reset time, no "resets at 00:00 UTC", nothing a client could
key a ledger on. Some providers do return a `X-RateLimit-Reset` header with an epoch second in it, and
when one does, **that header is the answer and your calendar is not** — read the reset time from the
response and key the ledger on it, rather than inferring the window from a timezone you guessed.

Two smaller variants of the same bug, both worth recognising:

**Daylight saving.** A scheduler keyed on a local day in a zone that observes DST has two days a year
that are an hour short and an hour long. The short one loses an hour of allowance; the long one gets an
extra hour of somebody else's. Neither raises.

**Two machines, two zones.** A job that runs on a laptop in one country and in CI in another keys the
same ledger two ways. Both are "local". They will disagree for part of every day and agree for the
rest, which is the worst possible test signal — it works, and then it does not, and then it works
again.

The smallest fix is one line and it is worth writing out because the correct version looks almost
identical to the wrong one:

```python
key = datetime.now(timezone.utc).strftime("%Y-%m-%d")
```

**Line by line:**

- `datetime.now(timezone.utc)` — with the argument, not `datetime.utcnow()`, which returns a *naive*
  datetime whose value happens to be UTC and which will be treated as local by the next thing that
  touches it. The argument is what makes it aware.
- No `astimezone` needed afterwards, because it is already in the zone the key is in.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead of a calendar key.** They stop inferring the window entirely.
Either the provider tells you when the counter resets — a header, a field in the error body, an
endpoint — and you key on that, or you accept that you do not know the window and treat your ledger
as an **estimate that must be reconciled**: when a 429 arrives and your ledger says you had room, your
ledger is wrong, and the correct response is to mark the window exhausted rather than to retry.

**What degrades at scale.** Any window boundary produces a stampede, and this is true even when
everybody's clocks agree. Every quota-limited job in the fleet is waiting for the same instant, so at
that instant all of them start. The allowance is consumed in the first seconds of the window by
whoever is fastest, and jobs that are polite about pacing get nothing. Real schedulers scatter start
times by a random offset for exactly this reason — the same jitter Day 72's backoff uses, applied to
the window rather than to the retry.

**The failure that only shows with real traffic.** The nightly appears to work for months because the
daytime desk is quiet, so `18 already spent` is really `3 already spent` and there is room for both
ledgers to be wrong without anything failing. Then traffic grows, the desk's spend crosses the line,
and a job that has not changed in eight months starts failing every night. Nothing in the diff
explains it, because there is no diff.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"This keys the quota ledger on `date.today()`. Whose
day is that? Ours or theirs? If we don't know, say so in a comment and key it on UTC, which is at
least a decision. And add the offset to the log line — when this goes wrong at 00:30 the first useful
question is what day both sides thought it was, and right now only one of them is written down."*

**The interview question:** *"Your daily API quota seems to reset at a strange time. How would you find
out when it actually resets?"* The answer that shows experience does not start with the docs. It
starts with the response: check for a reset header, and if there is none, spend deliberately until
refused, then poll a cheap request and record the minute the refusals stop. That measurement is worth
more than any documentation, and it comes with a date on it.

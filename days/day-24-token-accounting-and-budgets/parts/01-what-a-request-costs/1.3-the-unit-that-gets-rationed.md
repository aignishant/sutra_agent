# 1.3 — The unit that gets rationed is the request

## One-line answer

On a free tier the provider counts **requests**, not tokens — the quota metric it names in a refusal
is `generate_content_free_tier_requests` with `quotaValue: '20'` — so a 43-token question and a
151-token one cost exactly the same, and every optimisation aimed at token count buys you nothing.

## The story

The bus pass in your pocket is good for a number of journeys, not a number of kilometres.

Two stops or the whole route across the city: one journey, one punch on the card. The conductor does
not measure anything. There is a machine at the door and it goes click.

Which changes how you travel, whether or not you ever think about it. You stop making the trip to the
shop at the end of the road and back, because that is two punches for four hundred metres. You start
combining errands into one longer ride. Nobody told you to optimise; the unit of charging did.

And the friend who spends an evening working out the cheapest walking route between two stops has
optimised something the card does not measure.

## The idea in plain language

There are two ways a model provider can charge you, and they lead to opposite engineering.

**Per token.** This is how paid tiers usually work: so much per thousand input tokens, so much per
thousand output tokens. Under this rule, shorter prompts are cheaper, compaction saves money
proportionally, and everything in [1.1](1.1-a-ticket-id-costs-five-tokens.md) and
[1.2](1.2-charged-again-every-turn.md) translates directly into a smaller bill.

**Per request.** This is how free tiers usually work: a fixed number of calls per minute and per day,
and the size of each call does not enter into it. Under this rule a one-word question and a
fifteen-thousand-token document cost **the same**, and the only thing that saves you anything is
making fewer calls.

Sutra is on the second. That is not an incidental detail of the setup; it is the constraint the whole
curriculum is built around (Principle 15, and Addendum 02's rule that budgets are denominated in
**RPM/RPD per provider, not dollars**). And it reorders the engineering priorities completely:

| Under per-token charging | Under per-request charging |
| --- | --- |
| Trim the prompt | Trim the **number of calls** |
| Compaction saves money on every turn | Compaction **spends a call to save calls** |
| A retry costs a little | A retry costs **exactly as much as the question** |
| Batching is neutral | Batching is the single biggest lever |

The row that catches people is the third. On a per-token tier, a failed request that returned an error
costs almost nothing. On a per-request tier, **an attempt is an attempt** — the provider counted it,
whether or not you got an answer — which is why
[4.1](../04-failure-lab/4.1-the-retry-that-spent-the-budget.md) exists and why Day 21's retry
classifier turned out to be a budget feature rather than a reliability one.

None of which makes tokens irrelevant. Tokens still decide whether a request *fits*, they still decide
latency, and they will decide the bill the day this moves to a paid tier. But they are not the thing
being rationed today, and knowing which currency you are actually short of is the first step in
spending it well.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because the temptation, having just measured tokens for two parts, is to build a token budget — and a
token budget would enforce a limit the provider does not apply while ignoring the one it does.

Concretely: Sutra could compress its instruction from 32 tokens to 20, shorten every tool result, and
compact aggressively, and the number of questions it can answer in a day would be **unchanged at
twenty**. Meanwhile one badly-classified retry ladder spends three of those twenty on a single
refusal, and no token measurement anywhere would show it.

So the ledger built in [section 3](../03-counting-before-spending/3.1-the-ledger.md) counts requests.
The token measurements from this section stay useful for a different job — deciding whether a request
will fit, and getting ready for the day the budget is denominated in money — and Day 24's `Ceiling`
type carries `rpm` and `rpd` and nothing else, on purpose.

## The mechanism

The evidence is in the provider's own refusal. This is the body captured on 2026-09-04, when the day's
allowance had run out, quoted at the field that matters:

```text
'details': [
  ...
  {'@type': 'type.googleapis.com/google.rpc.QuotaFailure',
   'violations': [{'quotaMetric': 'generativelanguage.googleapis.com/generate_content_free_tier_requests',
                   'quotaId': 'GenerateRequestsPerDayPerProjectPerModel-FreeTier',
                   'quotaDimensions': {'model': 'gemini-3.7-flash', 'location': 'global'},
                   'quotaValue': '20'}]},
  {'@type': 'type.googleapis.com/google.rpc.RetryInfo', 'retryDelay': '26s'}
]
```

**Read the metric name.** `generate_content_free_tier_requests` — *requests*. Not tokens, not
characters, not input size. And `quotaId` says it again in words:
`GenerateRequestsPerDayPerProjectPerModel-FreeTier`. **Requests**, **per day**, **per project**, **per
model**. Four facts in one identifier, and every one of them shapes a design decision later in this
day.

Now the arithmetic that follows from it, using the numbers this section measured:

```python
# days/day-24-token-accounting-and-budgets/lab/quota.py
MINUTE = 60.0
DAY = 24 * 60 * 60.0


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Ceiling:
    """What one provider will serve before it starts refusing. Read off a 429, never guessed."""

    name: str
    rpm: int
    rpd: int
```

**Line by line:**

- The type has **`rpm` and `rpd` and no token field**. That is the whole argument of this part encoded
  as a data structure: what is scarce is what gets counted, and adding a `max_tokens` here would
  suggest the provider enforces something it does not.
- `frozen=True` because a ceiling is a fact about the provider, not state. A mutable ceiling is a
  ceiling somebody can raise at runtime, which is the beginning of a very confusing incident.
- `name` is the model string, because the quota is **per model** — the refusal says so. Two models
  have two independent allowances, which is exactly why Day 9's four providers matter and why the
  Quota-Router in Phase 8 is possible at all.
- `MINUTE` and `DAY` are module-level floats rather than magic numbers at the call sites, and they are
  in **seconds** because that is the unit `time.time()` and `time.monotonic()` both speak.

So: twenty requests a day, and from [1.2](1.2-charged-again-every-turn.md), an eight-turn conversation
uses eight of them. That is **two and a half conversations per day, per model**, regardless of how well
the prompts are written. Say that out loud once; it is the number that makes every later decision in
this day feel necessary rather than fussy.

## When it breaks

The failure is not an error message. It is optimising the wrong quantity, and the way it presents is a
week of work that changes nothing.

The tell is a sentence like *"we cut the prompt by forty percent"* followed by no change in how many
questions the system can answer. Forty percent of a quantity nobody is charging for is zero.

The mirror-image failure is real and produces an error you will meet: assuming the free tier is
generous because a single request is small. It is not scaled to your prompt size at all, so a system
that makes many tiny calls — a per-item classifier, a loop over search results, an agent that calls the
model once per tool decision — exhausts the day's allowance far faster than a system that makes a few
large ones. The refusal, when it arrives, looks identical either way:

```text
429 RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED. {'error': {'code': 429, 'message': 'You exceeded your current quota, please check your plan and billing details. ... * Quota exceeded for metric: generativelanguage.googleapis.com/generate_content_free_tier_requests, limit: 20, model: gemini-3.7-flash\nPlease retry in 26.45714483s.', 'status': 'RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED'}}
```

`limit: 20`, and nothing anywhere about how big any of those twenty were.

## In production

**Real systems are on both meters at once, and the binding one changes.** A paid tier charges per
token *and* rate-limits per minute, so you can be simultaneously under budget and over the ceiling.
The rule that survives is to **name the constraint before optimising**: measure which limit you hit
first, and optimise that one. Teams that skip this step reliably spend a quarter tuning prompt length
while being throttled on concurrency.

**Batching is the lever nobody uses early enough.** Under per-request charging, ten questions in one
call cost one request and ten separate calls cost ten. Many workloads — classification, extraction,
scoring a list — are trivially batchable and are written one-at-a-time because that is how the loop
was first drafted. It is usually the single largest saving available, and it costs an afternoon.

**What changes at scale** is that the unit changes underneath you. A team that grows out of a free tier
onto a paid one wakes up in the per-token world, where every lesson from this page inverts: suddenly
the prompt length *is* the bill and compaction *is* a saving. A budget module that hard-codes "requests"
as the unit has to be rewritten. The version that survives keeps the **cost function** as a small,
replaceable piece — one function that turns a request into a number — and the ledger merely adds those
numbers up.

**The failure that only appears with real traffic** is the per-minute ceiling arriving before the daily
one. Twenty a day sounds like the constraint until five users press send at the same moment and the
per-minute limit refuses four of them, at 10am, with nineteen of the day's requests still unspent.
That is [2.1](../02-two-ceilings/2.1-two-ceilings-one-clears.md), and it is why a ceiling type with one
number in it would have been wrong.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"this caps the prompt at four thousand tokens, but
our limit is twenty requests a day and nothing enforces that. What refuses the twenty-first call?"*

**The interview question:** *"how do you budget for an LLM feature?"* The answer that shows you have
read a rate-limit page: *"first I find out what's actually metered — some tiers charge per token, some
ration requests per minute and per day, and most production setups do both. Then I optimise whichever
one I hit first. On a per-request tier, trimming prompts is wasted effort and batching is the whole
game; on a per-token tier it's the other way round. And on a per-request tier a retry costs exactly as
much as the original question, which makes the retry policy a budget decision."*

# 1.2 — The conversation is charged again every turn

## One-line answer

An eight-turn conversation whose full text is **151 tokens** cost **786 tokens** to conduct — **5.2
times its own size** — because a chat model is stateless and turn eight sends turns one to eight
again.

## The story

Somebody in the neighbourhood group chat replies by quoting the entire thread.

The first time it happens it is mildly useful; you can see what they are answering. By the sixth
reply the message is a wall, and ninety percent of it is text you have already read six times.
Someone asks a two-word question and the reply arrives with four screens of history above it.

Nobody is doing anything wrong. Each person is pressing reply, and reply includes what came before.
It is just that the *cost of reading the chat* stopped being the cost of what people said and became
the cost of everything they have ever said, once per message.

## The idea in plain language

A chat model has no memory. This is worth stating flatly, because every conversational interface is
carefully designed to make it look as though it does.

When you send turn eight of a conversation, what actually goes over the wire is: the system
instruction, the tool declarations, turn one, turn two, turn three, and so on up to turn eight. The
model reads all of it, produces one reply, and forgets everything. The "conversation" is a fiction
maintained by your side of the connection re-sending the whole transcript every time.

That is what [Day 8](../../../day-08-sessions-and-services/LESSON.md) built when it introduced
sessions, and what [Day 20](../../../day-20-context-engineering-compaction/LESSON.md) attacked with
compaction. Here is the arithmetic that motivates both.

If each turn adds roughly the same number of tokens, then turn *n* costs about *n* units and the
whole conversation costs 1 + 2 + 3 + … + n. That sum grows with the **square** of the number of
turns. Double the length of a conversation and it does not cost twice as much; it costs about four
times as much.

Two consequences follow, and they pull in opposite directions:

**The conversation is small and the bill is not.** The transcript you could read in fifteen seconds
was charged several times over.

**And the cost is concentrated at the end.** The last turn of a long conversation is the most
expensive single request in it, which is exactly when the user is most invested and least willing to
be told that the budget has run out.

None of this is an argument that history is wasted. History is what makes the eighth answer good. It
is an argument for **knowing the multiplier**, because the multiplier is what turns "twenty requests
a day" into a real constraint or an easy one.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because Sutra's ceiling is twenty generate requests a day, and the question of whether that is enough
depends entirely on this number.

Twenty requests could be twenty separate questions from twenty tickets, or it could be two and a half
conversations of eight turns each. Those are wildly different products, and the difference is not
visible anywhere in the code — it emerges from how people use the desk.

It is also the number that decides whether Day 20's compaction was worth building.
[20.1.3](../../../day-20-context-engineering-compaction/parts/01-notes-not-transcript/1.3-you-spend-calls-to-save-calls.md)
worked out a break-even in characters; this part supplies the same measurement in the provider's own
unit, on a real conversation, so the decision can be re-made against something concrete rather than
against an estimate.

## The mechanism

Rebuild the prompt exactly as the runtime would at each turn, and count it.

```python
# days/day-24-token-accounting-and-budgets/lab/history_costs_again.py
INSTRUCTION = (
    "You are a support desk assistant. Read the ticket before answering. "
    "Never invent a ticket that does not exist. If a tool fails, say so plainly."
)

TURNS = [
    ("user", "Ticket 4521 please."),
    ("model", "Ticket 4521 is 'Refund not received', opened four days ago by a retail customer."),
    ("user", "What is our refund window?"),
    ("model", "Refunds are issued to the original payment method within thirty working days."),
    ("user", "Has it been longer than that?"),
    ("model", "The ticket was opened four days ago, so no - the window has not been exceeded."),
    ("user", "Draft a reply for me."),
    ("model", "Thank you for contacting us about your refund. Your request is within our window."),
]


def main() -> None:
    load_dotenv()
    client = genai.Client()

    print(f"{'turn':>5}{'new chars':>11}{'prompt tokens':>15}{'charged so far':>16}")
    charged = 0
    for turn in range(1, len(TURNS) + 1):
        prompt = INSTRUCTION + "\n" + "\n".join(f"{who}: {text}" for who, text in TURNS[:turn])
        tokens = client.models.count_tokens(model=MODEL, contents=prompt).total_tokens
        charged += tokens
        print(f"{turn:>5}{len(TURNS[turn - 1][1]):>11}{tokens:>15}{charged:>16}")
```

**Line by line:**

- `TURNS[:turn]` is the whole point of the script in one expression: at turn *n*, the prompt is the
  **prefix** of the conversation, not the *n*th message. Everything else follows from that slice.
- The instruction is prepended **inside the loop**, so it is counted on every turn — because it is
  sent on every turn. A system instruction is not free once; it is free never.
- `charged` accumulates the prompt tokens of each turn. That is the number that matters and the one
  nobody sees, because no single response ever reports it.
- `len(TURNS[turn - 1][1])` prints the **new** text's length beside the total, so the two columns can
  be compared: a small new message on a large prompt is the shape this part is about.
- Building the prompt as `f"{who}: {text}"` lines is a simplification of what the SDK sends, which is a
  structured list of `Content` parts. The count is therefore approximate rather than exact — the real
  request has a little structural overhead this does not model. It is close enough for the ratio, and
  saying so is better than implying a precision the script does not have.
- `count_tokens` again, so eight measurements cost **zero** generations. Measuring the cost of
  generation without generating is the only reason this part can exist at all on a free tier.
- The conversation is realistic on purpose: a ticket lookup, a policy question, a follow-up that
  depends on both, and a drafting request. A conversation of eight identical messages would give a
  cleaner curve and would not be a conversation anybody has had.

**Read the first column against the third.**

Turn 7 added **21 characters** — the sentence *"Draft a reply for me."* — and cost **132 tokens**.
Turn 1 added 19 characters and cost 43. The same size of contribution, three times the price, for no
reason other than where it sat in the conversation.

**And the last two lines are the finding.** The complete transcript is 151 tokens. Conducting it cost
786. That is **5.2×**, on a conversation short enough to read in one screen.

Two things follow immediately, and both are decisions rather than observations.

**The multiplier grows with length.** At eight turns it is 5.2. The sum 1+2+…+n divided by n is
(n+1)/2, so at sixteen turns it would be around 8.5 and at thirty-two around 16.5 — assuming turns of
similar size, which real conversations do not have, but the shape is right. Compaction
([Day 20](../../../day-20-context-engineering-compaction/LESSON.md)) is the intervention that flattens
this, and now there is a number to justify it with.

**And the instruction is in every one of those rows.** Sutra's instruction is 32 tokens
([1.1](1.1-a-ticket-id-costs-five-tokens.md)). Across eight turns that is 256 tokens spent on the same
143 characters. Which is the arithmetic behind Day 19's rule that standing rules belong in the
instruction and situational facts belong in state: everything in the instruction is paid for on every
single turn, forever.

## When it breaks

The count above is an approximation, and the honest failure is to forget that. The real request is a
structured list of `Content` objects with roles, and the tool declarations are attached to it as a
schema — none of which this script models. So the true prompt is **larger** than 151 tokens, and the
direction of the error is worth knowing: this measurement is a floor.

The way to get the exact number is not to model it better. It is to read it off a real response, which
carries usage metadata for the request that actually went out. That costs one generation.

There is a second surprise, and it was measured rather than assumed. `count_tokens` was handed a
string of 5.6 million characters on 2026-09-04 — far past anything `gemini-3.7-flash` will accept as
input — and it did not complain:

```text
counted 800002
```

**The counting endpoint does not enforce the model's input limit.** It tokenizes and reports, and it
will happily report a number the generation endpoint would refuse. So counting tells you the size and
tells you nothing about whether the request is *sendable*, and a budget check built only on
`count_tokens` will let an over-length prompt through to fail at the provider.

The refusal that then arrives is an HTTP `400`, not a `429` — a bad request rather than a quota
ceiling — and the distinction is the one Day 21's classifier exists to make: **retrying it is
pointless**, because the request will be exactly as large next time. It needs compaction, truncation
or a different model. Capturing that body verbatim costs one generation and today's allowance was
already gone, so it is left as a rep rather than invented:

## In production

**Nobody counts, everybody caches.** The production answer to re-sending a stable prefix is explicit
context caching: the provider stores the tokenized prefix, you reference it by handle, and the repeated
part is charged at a reduced rate or not at all. It changes the arithmetic of this page completely for
long system instructions and long documents, and it is worth reaching for the moment a stable prefix
gets large.

**What changes at scale** is that the multiplier becomes a product decision rather than an engineering
one. If conversations average four turns you have one cost model; if a support desk encourages
back-and-forth and they average twenty, you have a very different one, and the person who can change
it most cheaply is whoever designs the interface. Teams that measure this early put a **turns per
conversation** histogram on a dashboard beside the token count, because the second number is mostly
explained by the first.

**The failure that only appears with real traffic** is the long tail. Average conversation length is a
comforting number and the bill is set by the p99: the one user who has been in the same thread for two
hundred turns is paying two hundred times the first turn's price, and on a per-request quota they are
also the user most likely to trigger the ceiling. Compaction thresholds should be set from the tail,
not from the mean.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"this adds four sentences to the system instruction.
That's about thirty tokens on every request for the lifetime of the product — is it a standing rule,
or is it a fact that belongs in state?"*

**The interview question:** *"why does a long conversation get expensive faster than you expect?"* The
answer that shows you have measured: *"because the model is stateless, so every turn re-sends the whole
transcript. The cost of the conversation is the sum of its prefixes, which grows with the square of the
turn count. I measured an eight-turn conversation once: 151 tokens of text, 786 tokens charged — about
five times. The two levers are compaction and caching the stable prefix, and which one you reach for
depends on whether the growth is in the history or in the instruction."*

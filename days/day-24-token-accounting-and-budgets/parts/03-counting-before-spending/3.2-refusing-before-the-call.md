# 3.2 — Refusing before the call, not after

## One-line answer

Twenty-five attempts against a ceiling of twenty: checking the ledger first refuses five locally and
leaves the ledger reading **20 of 20**, while letting the provider decide makes **five real round
trips** and leaves the ledger reading **25 of 20** — five requests spent to be told no.

## The story

There is a queue for the changing room and you are holding a shirt.

In the good shop, someone at the rail asks what size you have picked, glances at the shelf, and says
they are out of that one in medium but there is a large. Four seconds. You put it back and carry on
looking.

In the other shop nobody asks. You join the queue, wait, get a cubicle, take your shoes off, and
discover in there that the shirt is not the size on the hanger. Then you queue again to hand it back.
Eleven minutes to learn something that was known at the rail the whole time.

Same information. The difference is entirely where it was checked.

## The idea in plain language

There are two places a budget can be enforced.

**After the call.** Send the request, and if the provider refuses, handle the refusal. This is what
everybody writes first, because it requires no bookkeeping at all: the provider already knows the
limit, so why keep a second copy?

**Before the call.** Ask your own ledger whether the request is allowed, and if not, do not send it.

On a **per-token** tier the first is nearly free, because a refused request costs almost nothing. On a
**per-request** tier it is not, and this is the consequence of
[1.3](../01-what-a-request-costs/1.3-the-unit-that-gets-rationed.md) that costs actual money: **the
provider counted the attempt.** A `429` is not a refund. You made a request; it is on the meter; you
received no value for it.

So checking first buys three things:

**It stops the spend.** A locally-refused request costs nothing at all — no network, no counter
increment, no quota.

**It answers faster and more usefully.** A local refusal is instant and carries your own sentence,
which can say *which* ceiling and *how long* ([2.1](../02-two-ceilings/2.1-two-ceilings-one-clears.md)).
A remote refusal takes a round trip and arrives as a provider's error body, which then has to be
parsed.

**It makes the budget yours.** A limit enforced only by the provider is the provider's limit. A limit
enforced by your ledger can be *stricter* — reserving requests for something more important, keeping a
tenant inside a share of the whole ([5.1](../05-in-production/5.1-whose-budget-is-it.md)) — and none
of that is possible if the only enforcement is somebody else's.

The order of operations matters and is easy to get wrong: **check, then send, then record**. Recording
before sending over-counts when the send fails at the network layer; recording only on success
under-counts every failed attempt, which is
[4.2](../04-failure-lab/4.2-the-counter-that-counted-the-wrong-thing.md). Recording at the moment the
request leaves is the one that matches what the provider does.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because Sutra's allowance is twenty and the cost of learning that the hard way is five percent of the
day per attempt.

Consider a batch: the desk triaging a backlog of thirty tickets overnight. Without a local check, the
first twenty succeed and the last ten each make a full round trip to be refused — ten requests on the
meter, ten waits, and ten error bodies to classify. With a local check, the first twenty succeed and
the last ten are refused instantly with a sentence that says *"20/20 per day — does not clear"*, which
is exactly what an operator needs to read in the morning.

It also makes the honest degradation in [5.2](../05-in-production/5.2-degrading-not-failing.md)
possible at all. To answer a question from the handbook instead of the model, you have to know the
model is unavailable **before** you have committed to using it.

## The mechanism

The same twenty-five attempts, two ways, with a switch between them.

```python
# days/day-24-token-accounting-and-budgets/lab/refuse_before_spending.py
CHECK_FIRST = os.environ.get("CHECK_FIRST", "1") == "1"

# The provider's own refusal, shortened from the body captured on 2026-09-04 (see 2.2).
REMOTE_429 = (
    "429 RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED. Quota exceeded for metric: "
    "generativelanguage.googleapis.com/generate_content_free_tier_requests, limit: 20"
)


def provider_call(ledger: Ledger, now: float) -> str:
    """A stand-in for the network: it refuses exactly when the real free tier would."""
    _, per_day = ledger.used(now)
    ledger.spend(now)
    if per_day >= FREE.rpd:
        raise RuntimeError(REMOTE_429)
    return "ok"


def main() -> None:
    ledger = Ledger(ceiling=FREE)
    served = refused_locally = refused_remotely = 0

    for attempt in range(25):
        now = attempt * 60.0  # one attempt a minute, so only the daily ceiling can bite
        if CHECK_FIRST and (why := ledger.refusal(now)):
            refused_locally += 1
            continue
        try:
            provider_call(ledger, now)
            served += 1
        except RuntimeError:
            refused_remotely += 1
```

**Line by line:**

- `provider_call` calls `ledger.spend(now)` **before** deciding whether to raise. That ordering is the
  whole simulation: the provider counts the attempt and *then* refuses it, which is what a real
  `429` does and the reason a remote refusal is not free.
- It reads `per_day` **before** spending, so the twentieth request is served and the twenty-first is
  refused, rather than being off by one. Getting this wrong is the classic fencepost error in a rate
  limiter, and it is worth checking with a small example on paper once.
- `now = attempt * 60.0` spaces the attempts a minute apart so that only the **daily** ceiling can
  fire. Without that, the per-minute limit would refuse most of them and the experiment would be
  measuring the wrong ceiling.
- `if CHECK_FIRST and (why := ledger.refusal(now))` uses the walrus operator to bind and test in one
  expression, so the refusal sentence is available to print. The alternative — calling `refusal()`
  twice — would be two scans of the deque and, worse, two answers that could differ.
- `continue` on a local refusal, so **nothing is spent**. That single line is the difference the two
  arms measure.
- The `try`/`except RuntimeError` around the provider call is narrow: it catches the simulated refusal
  and nothing else, which is Day 21's rule. A bare `except` here would also swallow a bug in the
  ledger.
- `REMOTE_429` is a shortened form of the **real** body captured on 2026-09-04
  ([2.2](../02-two-ceilings/2.2-reading-the-ceiling-off-a-refusal.md)), not an invented string, so the
  text a reader sees in the output is text a provider actually sent.

```bash
cd days/day-24-token-accounting-and-budgets/lab
CHECK_FIRST=1 uv run python refuse_before_spending.py
CHECK_FIRST=0 uv run python refuse_before_spending.py
```

**Line by line:**

- `CHECK_FIRST` is the ablation: `1` asks the ledger first, `0` lets the provider decide. No key, no
  network, **0** generations — the provider is simulated so that the arithmetic can be run without
  spending the thing it is about.

Measured on 2026-09-04:

```text
attempt 21: refused locally  -> gemini-3.7-flash: 20/20 per day - does not clear

CHECK_FIRST=1
  served            20
  refused locally   5
  refused remotely  0   <- each one is a real round trip
  ledger says used  20 of 20 today

attempt 21: refused remotely -> 429 RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED. Quota exceeded for metric: generativelanguage.googleapis.com/generate_content_free_tier_requests, limit: 20

CHECK_FIRST=0
  served            20
  refused locally   0
  refused remotely  5   <- each one is a real round trip
  ledger says used  25 of 20 today
```

**Both arms served twenty.** The ceiling is the ceiling; checking first does not get you more work
done, and it is important to say so rather than to oversell.

**The difference is the last line: `20 of 20` against `25 of 20`.** In the second arm five requests
were made, counted by the provider, and returned nothing. On a per-request tier that is five percent of
a working day, spent on being told no — and on a paid tier it would be five billable calls with no
output.

**And the second difference is the message.** `gemini-3.7-flash: 20/20 per day - does not clear` is
your own sentence: it names the model, both numbers, and — crucially — that waiting will not help. The
remote version is a provider's error string that has to be parsed before any of that can be known, and
[2.2](../02-two-ceilings/2.2-reading-the-ceiling-off-a-refusal.md) shows how much work that parsing is.

## When it breaks

The check can be wrong in two directions and only one of them is visible.

**Too strict** is loud: your ledger believes the allowance is gone when it is not, and work stops that
would have been served. Somebody notices within a day, because the system is doing less than it should.

**Too lax** is silent: your ledger believes there is room and the provider disagrees. This is the
normal state of affairs after a restart ([3.1](3.1-the-ledger.md)) or when anything else in the same
project is also spending, and the symptom is a `429` arriving despite a green local check:

```text
429 RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED. {'error': {'code': 429, ... 'limit: 20, model: gemini-3.7-flash' ...}}
```

Which leads to the rule that keeps this honest: **the local check is an optimisation, not an
authority.** The provider is always right. Code that checks locally and then treats a `429` as
impossible will crash the first time reality disagrees; code that checks locally to avoid obviously
doomed calls, and still handles the refusal properly, is correct in both worlds. Day 21's error policy
does not go away because Day 24 arrived.

The third failure is an ordering one and it has no message at all. Move `ledger.spend(now)` to after
the successful return — record only what worked — and the local check starts believing in requests that
were never returned. That is
[4.2](../04-failure-lab/4.2-the-counter-that-counted-the-wrong-thing.md), and the drift it produces is
five requests out of sixteen.

## In production

**The check moves in front of everything, including the queue.** In a real system the budget check is
usually the first thing that happens to a job, before it is enqueued, so that work which cannot be done
today is rejected at submission rather than after occupying a worker. That turns a quota problem into a
scheduling problem, which is a much better kind of problem: the caller finds out immediately and can
decide to wait, downgrade, or go elsewhere.

**Reserve, then commit.** The two-step in the lab — check, then spend — has a race in it
([3.1](3.1-the-ledger.md)), and the production form is a single atomic *take a permit* that returns
either a permit or a refusal. That is exactly what the token bucket in
[the paper](../../papers/01-the-leaky-bucket.md) does, and it is the main reason to prefer a bucket
over a list of timestamps once there is any concurrency.

**What changes at scale** is that local checks become an admission-control layer with its own metrics.
The interesting number stops being "did we exceed the ceiling" and becomes **how many requests we
refused ourselves**, broken down by which ceiling and which tenant. A system refusing ten percent of
its own traffic locally is either correctly protecting a limit or badly under-provisioned, and the
same counter answers both questions.

**The failure that only appears with real traffic** is a local ledger that disagrees with the provider
because something else shares the quota. The `quotaId` said **per project**
([2.2](../02-two-ceilings/2.2-reading-the-ceiling-off-a-refusal.md)), and a project usually contains a
staging environment, a notebook and somebody's demo. Your process's ledger is honest about your
process; the provider is counting all of them.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"we check the budget after the call fails. On a
per-request tier that means every refusal costs a request — check before, and keep the 429 handling as
the backstop."*

**The interview question:** *"you're rate-limited. Do you check locally or just handle the 429?"* The
answer that shows you have counted: *"both, in that order. Locally first because on a per-request tier
a refused call still costs a request, and the local refusal is instant and can say which ceiling and
whether waiting helps. But the provider stays the authority — my ledger dies with the process and it
can't see the batch job in the same project — so the 429 handler doesn't go away, it just stops being
the only thing standing between us and the ceiling."*

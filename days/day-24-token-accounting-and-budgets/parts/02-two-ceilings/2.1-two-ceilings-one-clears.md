# 2.1 — Two ceilings, and only one of them clears

## One-line answer

A free tier enforces **two independent limits** — requests per minute, which refills as time passes,
and requests per day, which does not — and a budget that models them as one number will either
refuse work that would have been served or wait patiently for a ceiling that is never going to lift.

## The story

There are two ways to leave a car on the same street and they behave nothing alike.

The meter takes coins and gives you an hour. When the hour is up you can walk out, feed it again, and
carry on. It is a limit on how fast you consume, not on how much: irritating, self-clearing, and
entirely survivable if you have change and patience.

The residents' permit on the windscreen is a different animal. It was issued once for the year. There
is no slot to put a coin in. If you have used your visitor allocation for the month, standing next to
the car and waiting will achieve nothing at all, because nothing is refilling.

People who confuse the two waste an afternoon. They sit in the car with the engine off, waiting for a
permit to come back, the way a meter does.

## The idea in plain language

The free tier that Sutra runs on states its limits in two numbers, and they are enforced separately:

**RPM — requests per minute.** A cap on *rate*. It is a moving window: as older requests age past the
minute mark they stop counting, so capacity returns on its own. Hitting it means *you are going too
fast*, and the cure is to slow down.

**RPD — requests per day.** A cap on *volume*. It resets on the provider's schedule and not before.
Hitting it means *you have had your share*, and the cure is either to wait for the reset or to use a
different model.

Both refusals arrive as HTTP `429`. That is the whole difficulty: **two entirely different situations,
one status code**, and the correct response to each is the opposite of the correct response to the
other.

| | RPM | RPD |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it limits | how fast | how much |
| Refills | continuously, as requests age out | at the provider's daily reset |
| Right response | back off and retry | stop, escalate, or switch model |
| Wrong response | give up | retry |
| Cost of the wrong response | work not done | requests spent learning nothing |

[Day 21](../../../day-21-errors-surface-not-swallow/parts/03-policy/3.1-which-429-is-it.md) discovered
this distinction from the error-handling side and measured what confusing them costs. This part is the
same fact from the budgeting side, and it produces a design requirement: **a ceiling is two numbers,
and a refusal must say which one it was.**

There is a third thing worth naming now because it is the most expensive misunderstanding available.
The provider's `429` includes a `retryDelay` — *"Please retry in 26.45714483s"*. That number looks
exactly like the moment the window reopens. It is not. On the daily ceiling it is a **backoff
suggestion**, and Day 21 established this the hard way: twenty-eight requests spread across a quarter
of an hour, each one obeying the stated delay, were **all** refused. A hint that sounds like a promise
is worse than no hint, and Sutra records that finding in `ADR-0007`.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because the ledger built in [3.1](../03-counting-before-spending/3.1-the-ledger.md) has to make a
decision the moment a request is about to go out, and the two ceilings need different decisions.

Under the per-minute ceiling the right answer is *"wait, then send"* — the work will get done, just
later, and telling the user "we are busy, one moment" is honest. Under the per-day ceiling the right
answer is *"do not send, and say so"* — waiting is a lie, and the caller needs the truth so they can
come back tomorrow or so an operator can be told.

A single `remaining` counter cannot express that difference. Which is why `Ceiling` carries two
numbers, `refusal()` returns a **sentence naming the ceiling** rather than a boolean, and the day
ceiling is checked **first** — because when both are breached, the one that will not clear is the one
the caller needs to hear about.

## The mechanism

Two windows over one list of timestamps.

```python
# days/day-24-token-accounting-and-budgets/lab/quota.py
@dataclass
class Ledger:
    """Every request this process has made to one model, as timestamps, oldest first."""

    ceiling: Ceiling
    spent: deque[float] = field(default_factory=deque)

    def _forget_older_than(self, now: float, window: float) -> int:
        """Drop timestamps outside the window and return how many remain inside it."""
        while self.spent and now - self.spent[0] > DAY:
            self.spent.popleft()
        return sum(1 for t in self.spent if now - t <= window)

    def used(self, now: float) -> tuple[int, int]:
        """Requests in the last minute and in the last day, as of `now`."""
        return self._forget_older_than(now, MINUTE), self._forget_older_than(now, DAY)

    def refusal(self, now: float) -> str | None:
        """Why this request must not be sent, or None if it may be. Checked before spending."""
        per_minute, per_day = self.used(now)
        if per_day >= self.ceiling.rpd:
            return f"{self.ceiling.name}: {per_day}/{self.ceiling.rpd} per day - does not clear"
        if per_minute >= self.ceiling.rpm:
            wait = MINUTE - (now - self.spent[-self.ceiling.rpm])
            return f"{self.ceiling.name}: {per_minute}/{self.ceiling.rpm} per minute - {wait:.0f}s"
        return None
```

**Line by line:**

- `deque` rather than `list`, because the only two operations are *append on the right* and *discard
  from the left*, and `list.pop(0)` is a copy of the whole list every time. At twenty entries this is
  irrelevant; at a production ceiling of thousands per minute it is the difference between a check that
  is free and one that is not.
- `_forget_older_than` **prunes only past the day boundary** — the `while` loop uses `DAY`, not the
  passed-in `window`. That is deliberate and would be a bug if it were not: pruning to the minute would
  throw away exactly the history the daily count needs. One list, two windows, and only the outer one
  may forget.
- `sum(1 for t in self.spent if now - t <= window)` scans rather than being clever. With a bounded list
  this is honest and obviously correct; the optimisation — a second deque, or a ring buffer — belongs in
  the *In production* section, not in the version you are learning from.
- `used()` returns **a tuple of both counts**, so a caller that wants to display *"3 this minute, 17
  today"* does not have to ask twice. Returning one number would have forced the two ceilings into one
  concept, which is the mistake this part is about.
- **`refusal()` returns a string or `None`, not a boolean.** `None` means send; a string is both the
  refusal and the reason. That is [21.1.2](../../../day-21-errors-surface-not-swallow/parts/01-who-hears-it/1.2-an-error-is-a-fact.md)'s
  rule applied here — a refusal your code can act on and a human can read, in one value — and it is
  what lets a log line, a user message and a test all use the same sentence.
- **The day check comes first.** When both ceilings are breached simultaneously the caller must be told
  about the one that will not clear, because that is the one that changes what they should do. Ordering
  these two `if`s the other way round is a real bug with no error message, and
  [3.3](../03-counting-before-spending/3.3-testing-a-ceiling-that-bites-at-midnight.md) has the test
  that catches it.
- `self.spent[-self.ceiling.rpm]` is the timestamp of the oldest request still inside the minute
  window, so `MINUTE - (now - that)` is how long until it ages out and capacity returns. The per-minute
  message can therefore carry **a number of seconds that is actually true**, unlike the provider's
  `retryDelay` on the daily ceiling.
- The two messages are written to be different at a glance: `does not clear` against a number of
  seconds. Somebody reading a log at speed should not have to parse the sentence to know which
  situation they are in.

## When it breaks

Delete the per-day branch from `refusal()` — the edit that collapses two ceilings into one — and the
suite says exactly what was lost:

```text
        refusal = ledger.refusal(now=DAY)
        assert refusal is not None
>       assert "per day" in refusal, refusal
E       AssertionError: gemini-3.7-flash: 20/5 per minute - 52s
E       assert 'per day' in 'gemini-3.7-flash: 20/5 per minute - 52s'

=========================== short test summary info ===========================
FAILED test_quota_demo.py::test_the_per_day_ceiling_does_not_clear_after_a_minute
FAILED test_quota_demo.py::test_the_day_refusal_is_reported_before_the_minute_refusal
2 failed, 6 passed in 0.13s
```

**Read the assertion message rather than the test name.** `gemini-3.7-flash: 20/5 per minute - 52s` is
a refusal that is *technically true* — twenty requests really is over the per-minute ceiling of five —
and completely misleading. It tells the caller to wait 52 seconds. After 52 seconds the request will be
refused again, and again, for the rest of the day, and each attempt will cost one of the requests that
are already gone.

That is the whole failure mode in one line of output: **the wrong ceiling produces a refusal that
sounds recoverable.**

The other failure has no test because it is a property of the world rather than of the code: the
provider's own reset schedule is not necessarily midnight in your timezone, and a ledger that assumes
it is will believe the allowance has refilled while the provider still says no. Sutra's ledger avoids
this by using a **rolling** rolling day-long window rather than a calendar day — stricter than the provider, and
therefore safe — which is a deliberate trade named in
[3.1](../03-counting-before-spending/3.1-the-ledger.md).

## In production

**Real limits come in more than two.** Providers commonly publish requests per minute, requests per
day, tokens per minute and concurrent requests, and enterprise tiers add per-key and per-region caps.
The shape of the code does not change — a list of ceilings, each with a window and a limit, and a
refusal that names which one bit — but a design with exactly two hard-coded numbers has to be rewritten.
Sutra keeps two because two is what the free tier has, and the honest place to note the generalisation
is here rather than in speculative code.

**The rate limiter belongs in front, not inside.** A per-minute ceiling enforced by every caller
independently is not enforced at all once there are two processes. The production shape is a shared
limiter — one Redis key, one sidecar, one gateway — and the local ledger becomes a fast pre-check that
avoids obviously-doomed calls rather than the authority. Sutra is one process today and this is fine;
the moment it is two, this module is lying.

**What degrades under concurrency** is the check-then-act gap. Between `refusal()` returning `None` and
`spend()` being called, another coroutine can do the same thing, and both send. At a ceiling of five
per minute with two workers that is a real over-spend. The fix is to make check-and-spend one atomic
operation — a single method that either returns a permit or a refusal — and it is one of the
`TODO(me)` items for exactly that reason.

**The failure that only appears with real traffic** is the per-minute ceiling arriving first and being
mistaken for the daily one. Five users press send in the same second, four are refused, and an operator
reading a dashboard that only shows daily usage sees nineteen requests remaining and no explanation.
Both counts have to be visible, which is why `used()` returns both.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"this returns `True`/`False`. When it refuses, the
caller needs to know whether to wait or to give up — return the reason, not a boolean."*

**The interview question:** *"you're rate-limited by a provider. What do you build?"* The answer that
shows you have been refused: *"two counters, because rate limits and volume limits are different
animals — one clears on its own and one doesn't, and they arrive as the same HTTP 429. The refusal has
to say which, because retrying a daily ceiling spends real budget to learn nothing. And I don't trust
the `retryDelay` in the body for the daily one — I've watched requests obey it for a quarter of an hour
and be refused every time, because it's a backoff hint and not a window reset."*

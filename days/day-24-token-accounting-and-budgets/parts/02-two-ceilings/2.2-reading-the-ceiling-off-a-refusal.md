# 2.2 — Reading the ceiling off a refusal

## One-line answer

The daily ceiling was not taken from a documentation page — it was read out of a real refusal, whose
`QuotaFailure` detail states `quotaValue: '20'` for `gemini-3.7-flash` — and the per-minute ceiling
has **not** been captured that way, so it is a `TODO` with a command rather than a number.

## The story

The jumper has a small satin label sewn into the side seam and nobody reads it.

You guess. Thirty degrees seems reasonable for wool. It comes out of the machine as a garment for a
child, and the label — which you now read, holding the ruined thing — says hand wash only, and there
is a picture of a tub with a hand in it and a crossed-out triangle, and every one of those symbols was
sitting there the whole time.

The information was not hard to get. It was attached to the item, in the item's own words, by the
person who made it. It just required looking instead of assuming.

## The idea in plain language

There are three ways to learn what a provider will serve you, and they are not equally reliable.

**A documentation page.** Convenient and frequently wrong: rate-limit pages are updated less often
than rate limits are, they describe tiers rather than your project, and free-tier numbers in
particular move without announcement. Addendum 02 exists because a free roster changed under this
curriculum once already.

**A dashboard.** Better, because it is about your project — but it tells you what you have *used*, and
often in a different unit from the one being enforced.

**The refusal itself.** Best, because it is the enforcing system telling you, in its own words, at the
moment of enforcement, which rule you broke and what its value is. It cannot be stale, because it was
generated by the thing doing the refusing.

Google's API returns errors in a structured shape — a `code`, a `message`, and a list of typed
`details` — and two of those detail types matter here:

**`google.rpc.QuotaFailure`** carries the machine-readable facts: `quotaMetric` (what is being
counted), `quotaId` (which named rule), `quotaDimensions` (which model, which region) and `quotaValue`
(the limit). This is the label sewn into the seam.

**`google.rpc.RetryInfo`** carries `retryDelay`. This is the one that looks helpful and is not, on the
daily ceiling — see [2.1](2.1-two-ceilings-one-clears.md) and `ADR-0007`.

So the discipline is: **capture the refusal once, read the numbers out of it, and write them down with
the date.** That is Principle 7 — never invent a version number — applied to quota, and it is why the
`Ceiling` in this day's lab has a comment beside it saying where its numbers came from.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because a ceiling that is guessed too high spends real requests discovering it, and a ceiling that is
guessed too low refuses work the provider would have served.

Both errors are silent. Guess 50 when the truth is 20, and Sutra confidently sends request 21 and
takes a `429` — which, on a per-request tier, **costs one of the twenty it did not have**
([1.3](../01-what-a-request-costs/1.3-the-unit-that-gets-rationed.md)). Guess 10 when the truth is 20,
and Sutra refuses half the day's work with a message that sounds authoritative and is fiction.

And it matters for Day 31 and Phase 8, where the Quota-Router chooses between four providers based on
what is left. A router built on numbers nobody verified is a router that will route around a ceiling
that does not exist and into one that does.

## The mechanism

Capture the refusal. Here is the one taken on 2026-09-04, after the day's allowance was gone, printed
whole:

```text
ClientError 429
429 RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED. {'error': {'code': 429, 'message': 'You exceeded your current quota, please check your plan and billing details. For more information on this error, head to: https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/rate-limits. To monitor your current usage, head to: https://ai.dev/rate-limit. \n* Quota exceeded for metric: generativelanguage.googleapis.com/generate_content_free_tier_requests, limit: 20, model: gemini-3.7-flash\nPlease retry in 26.45714483s.', 'status': 'RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED', 'details': [{'@type': 'type.googleapis.com/google.rpc.Help', 'links': [{'description': 'Learn more about Gemini API quotas', 'url': 'https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/rate-limits'}]}, {'@type': 'type.googleapis.com/google.rpc.QuotaFailure', 'violations': [{'quotaMetric': 'generativelanguage.googleapis.com/generate_content_free_tier_requests', 'quotaId': 'GenerateRequestsPerDayPerProjectPerModel-FreeTier', 'quotaDimensions': {'model': 'gemini-3.7-flash', 'location': 'global'}, 'quotaValue': '20'}]}, {'@type': 'type.googleapis.com/google.rpc.RetryInfo', 'retryDelay': '26s'}]}}
```

**Six facts are in there and every one of them is load-bearing.**

`limit: 20` and `quotaValue: '20'` — the same number twice, once in prose for a human and once in a
field for a program. **That is the RPD.**

`quotaId: 'GenerateRequestsPerDayPerProjectPerModel-FreeTier'` — the rule's name, and it is a sentence:
requests, per day, per project, per model, free tier. Every word of it is a design constraint.
*Per model* is why a second model is a second allowance. *Per project* is why
[5.1](../05-in-production/5.1-whose-budget-is-it.md) has to divide it internally.

`quotaMetric: '...generate_content_free_tier_requests'` — requests, confirming
[1.3](../01-what-a-request-costs/1.3-the-unit-that-gets-rationed.md).

`quotaDimensions: {'model': 'gemini-3.7-flash', 'location': 'global'}` — which model this applies to,
so a ledger keyed on anything less specific than the model string is keyed wrongly.

`retryDelay: '26s'` — the trap. Day 21 measured twenty-eight requests over a quarter of an hour, each
obeying the stated delay, all refused.

And `status: 'RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED'` — the same status for both ceilings, which is why the `quotaId` and
not the status is what a classifier must key on.

Here is how to read it out of the exception rather than out of a printed string:

```python
# days/day-24-token-accounting-and-budgets/lab/quota.py — the shape a classifier needs
def ceiling_from(exc: object) -> tuple[str, int] | None:
    """The quotaId and quotaValue a 429 named, or None if the body did not carry them."""
    details = getattr(exc, "details", None) or {}
    for detail in details.get("error", {}).get("details", []):
        if detail.get("@type", "").endswith("QuotaFailure"):
            violation = detail["violations"][0]
            return violation["quotaId"], int(violation["quotaValue"])
    return None
```

**Line by line:**

- `getattr(exc, "details", None) or {}` — the SDK's `APIError` carries the parsed body on `.details`,
  and defending against its absence matters because a network-level failure produces an exception with
  no body at all. The `or {}` turns "no details" into an empty dict rather than a `TypeError` three
  lines later.
- `detail.get("@type", "").endswith("QuotaFailure")` matches on the **suffix**, not the whole string.
  The full type URL is `type.googleapis.com/google.rpc.QuotaFailure`, and matching the suffix survives
  a change of host in the type URL while still being specific.
- `violations[0]` takes the first violation, and that is a simplification worth admitting: a response
  may list several. For a free tier with one rule per model it is right; the general version iterates
  and returns them all.
- `int(violation["quotaValue"])` — the value arrives as a **string**, `'20'`, not a number. Comparing
  it to an integer without converting is a bug that silently never fires.
- The function returns `(quotaId, value)` rather than a boolean, so the caller can key on the id —
  `"PerDay" in quota_id` — which is the only reliable way to tell the two ceilings apart, since the
  HTTP status is identical.
- It returns `None` rather than raising when the body has no `QuotaFailure`. That is Principle 10 read
  carefully: this function's job is to extract, and a `429` without quota details is a real thing that
  the caller must decide about, not an error this function should invent.

**And now the honest half of this part.**

The per-minute ceiling has **not** been captured this way. Every `429` this curriculum has collected —
on Day 9, on Day 21, and again today — has been the daily one. The `rpm=5` in the lab's `Ceiling` is a
placeholder chosen so the tests have two ceilings to distinguish, and it is **not a measured fact about
the free tier**.

Writing a number nobody verified would be exactly the failure Principle 7 exists to prevent, so it gets
a `TODO` carrying the exact command instead:

```text
send requests as fast as the loop will go and print the first 429's body:

  from google.genai import errors; c = genai.Client()

```

This costs real requests, which is why it is a rep rather than something already done. It is also the
single most valuable thing in this day's build brief, because every later decision about back-off and
concurrency depends on a number that is currently a guess wearing a constant's clothes.

## When it breaks

Read the wrong field and the classifier is confidently wrong. Keying on the HTTP status:

```text
429 RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED
```

That is all a status-based classifier sees, and it is identical for both ceilings. A policy built on
it either retries everything — spending the daily allowance three requests at a time
([4.1](../04-failure-lab/4.1-the-retry-that-spent-the-budget.md)) — or retries nothing, giving up on
per-minute refusals that would have cleared in seconds.

Key on the `message` string instead and it works until it does not: the human-readable message is
prose, it is localisable, and it has already changed format at least once in this curriculum's
lifetime. `quotaId` is the identifier; the message is the caption.

And the failure with no error at all is comparing `quotaValue` — a string — to an integer:

```text
>>> "20" >= 20
TypeError: '>=' not supported between instances of 'str' and 'int'
```

which at least raises. The genuinely silent version is `"20" == 20`, which is simply `False`, forever,
and produces a check that never fires.

## In production

**The refusal is a fact worth storing, not just handling.** Mature systems log the whole `QuotaFailure`
detail as structured fields — Day 22's format is already the right shape for it — so that "what is our
actual limit on this model" is a query rather than an archaeology exercise. It also means a limit that
changes shows up as a change in a field rather than as an incident.

**Limits move, so the number needs a date.** Sutra records every pin with the date it was verified,
and quota is no different: `docs/PACKAGES.md` carries the model, the metric, the value and the day it
was read off a live refusal. A number without a date is a number nobody can decide whether to trust.

**What changes at scale** is that you stop discovering limits by hitting them. Paid tiers publish
per-project quotas through an API, and the production arrangement reads them at startup and configures
the limiter from what the provider says rather than from a constant in the source. The refusal remains
the ground truth, but it becomes the *check* rather than the *source*.

**The failure that only appears with real traffic** is a limit that differs by region or by key. The
same code, the same model, a different deployment, and a ceiling half the size — because
`quotaDimensions` included a `location` and nobody read it. Any ledger keyed on the model string alone
is assuming one dimension of a multi-dimensional quota, which is correct until the day the system runs
in two places.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"where did 20 come from? If it's from the docs page,
say so and date it. If it's from a real 429, paste the `quotaId` in the comment — those two facts have
very different shelf lives."*

**The interview question:** *"how do you know what your rate limit is?"* The answer that shows you have
been throttled: *"from a real refusal, not from the docs. The error body carries a `QuotaFailure` with
the metric, the rule id and the limit, and the rule id tells you whether it's a per-minute or a per-day
rule — which the HTTP status doesn't. I write the number down with the date I captured it, because
free-tier limits in particular move. And I don't trust the `retryDelay` on a daily ceiling; I've seen
requests obey it and be refused every time for a quarter of an hour."*

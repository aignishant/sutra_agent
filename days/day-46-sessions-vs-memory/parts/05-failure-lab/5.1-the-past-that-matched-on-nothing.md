# 5.1 — 💥 The past that matched on nothing

## One-line answer

A query written the way people actually write questions matched **all four** filed tickets, the
first of them was the wrong one, and an agent reading `memories[0]` told the customer to apply
KB-201 when the right article was KB-104 — with no error, no warning and no way to tell from the
result that anything had gone wrong.

## The story

The clothes come back on Thursday, pressed, on a hanger, in a thin plastic sleeve.

Your family's mark is a small `R` in blue thread on the inside of the collar. It has worked for
years. There are two hundred shirts moving through that shop in a week and the mark has never once
let you down.

This Thursday there is a shirt on the hanger that is not yours. It is a good shirt, roughly your
size, and it has a small `R` in blue thread on the inside of the collar. Somebody two lanes over has
the same mark, and this week is the week both families sent in a white shirt.

Nobody was careless. The mark was read correctly. The system did exactly what it was built to do,
and the reason it failed is that the mark was never specific enough to tell two things apart — it
only had to be specific enough to tell two hundred things apart on the weeks when they were all
different.

And you will not notice at the door. You will notice at the wedding.

## The idea in plain language

`InMemoryMemoryService` decides whether a stored event matches your query with one line:

> `if any(query_word in words_in_event for query_word in words_in_query):`

Read `any` carefully. **One shared word is a match.** Not most of them, not the important ones — one.
And the words are whatever `re.findall(r"\w+", text)` produces, lowercased, which includes `the`,
`a`, `on`, `what`, `did`, `we`, `do` and every other word that carries no information at all.

Two consequences, and they compound.

**Over-matching.** A natural question contains eight or ten common words. A stored ticket contains
twenty. The chance that they share at least one is close to one, so a natural question tends to match
*everything in the store*. Not a few extra things — everything.

**No ranking.** `SearchMemoryResponse.memories` is a plain list, appended to in the order the store
iterates: bucket by bucket, session by session, event by event. That order is insertion order. It has
no relationship to relevance whatsoever, and there is no score field to sort on
([2.3](../02-the-interface/2.3-what-comes-back-is-not-a-session.md)).

Put those together and you get the failure this part is about. The search returns everything, in an
order decided by when things were filed, and any consumer that takes the first result — a model
reading a list top to bottom, a formatter showing "the closest past case", a human skimming — takes
whatever was filed earliest.

And here is what makes it worse than a miss. A miss produces an empty list, and an empty list at
least *looks* like nothing. **A wrong hit looks exactly like a right hit.** Same type, same shape,
same confidence, and the text of it is a real past ticket with a real article number in it. There is
nothing in the response that a downstream check could look at.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because this is the failure Sutra's memory feature introduces, and it is worse than the problem it
solves.

Before today, a support agent asking the desk about a past case got *"I do not have access to past
tickets"*. Unhelpful, and completely safe. After today they get an article number, delivered with the
same fluency as every other answer, and one in some number of those article numbers belongs to a
different problem. The desk has gone from *"cannot help"* to *"sometimes confidently wrong"*, which
is the direction Principle 10 exists to prevent.

Rule 4 of [4.2](../04-the-choice/4.2-sutras-memory-policy.md) is the partial answer — a miss is
reported rather than interpreted — but a miss is not what happens here. Rule 2's cap does not help
either: capping a list that is not ranked just picks the first three arbitrary results instead of all
four. The real fix is a better retriever, which is Day 49, and the reason this part exists is so that
Day 49 is understood as a **correctness** fix rather than a nice-to-have.

## The mechanism

Four filed tickets, one natural question, and a stopword ablation.

```python
# days/day-46-sessions-vs-memory/lab/wrongpast.py
ARCHIVE = [
    ("4402", "The customer cannot print the invoice from the portal. Fix: KB-201, re-issue PDF."),
    ("4455", "The customer wants a refund on the annual plan. Fix: KB-330, finance approval."),
    ("4521", "The customer keeps getting logged out. Fix: KB-104, set SameSite on the cookie."),
    ("4508", "The customer reports the export runs but the file is empty. Fix: KB-512."),
]
QUESTION = "The customer on 4610 keeps getting logged out. What did we do last time?"
STOPWORDS = {"the", "on", "what", "did", "we", "do", "last", "time", "customer"}
```

**Line by line:**

- `ARCHIVE` holds four *different* problems. They share nothing except the phrase "The customer",
  which is exactly the shape of a real ticket archive: every entry starts the same way.
- The **order matters** and is the point. `4402` is filed first and `4521` — the right one — is third.
  Insertion order is what `InMemoryMemoryService` iterates in, so 4402 will be `memories[0]`.
- `QUESTION` is written the way a support agent types, not the way a query should be written. It has a
  ticket number, a symptom and a polite ending, and nine of its twelve words are furniture.
- `STOPWORDS` is nine words. Note what is in it: `customer` is a stopword **in this archive**, because
  every entry contains it. A general-purpose stopword list would not include it, which is the first
  hint that stopword removal is a patch rather than a fix.

```python
# days/day-46-sessions-vs-memory/lab/wrongpast.py (continued)
    query = QUESTION
    if strip:
        kept = [w for w in re.findall(r"\w+", QUESTION) if w.lower() not in STOPWORDS]
        query = " ".join(kept)
    print(f"question: {QUESTION}")
    print(f"query sent to search_memory: {query!r}\n")

    found = await memory.search_memory(app_name=APP, user_id=USER, query=query)
    print(f"memories returned: {len(found.memories)} of {len(ARCHIVE)} filed")
    for entry in found.memories:
        text = "".join(p.text or "" for p in (entry.content.parts or []))
        print(f"    {text[:88]}")

    if found.memories:
        first = "".join(p.text or "" for p in (found.memories[0].content.parts or []))
        article = re.search(r"KB-\d+", first)
        print(f"\nwhat an agent reading the first result tells the customer: {article.group()}")
        print("what 4610 actually needs                                  : KB-104")
```

**Line by line:**

- `re.findall(r"\w+", QUESTION)` uses **the same tokenizer the memory service uses**, deliberately. A
  stopword filter that split words differently from the matcher would be filtering the wrong words.
- `if w.lower() not in STOPWORDS` — lowercasing on the way in, because `InMemoryMemoryService`
  lowercases too, and `"The"` and `"the"` must be the same word on both sides.
- `print(f"query sent to search_memory: {query!r}")` is the line that would have saved the incident.
  **The query is the only thing that explains the result**, and under `load_memory` it is in the
  transcript; the whole argument for rule 1 of the policy is this print statement existing in
  production.
- `found.memories[0]` is the bug, written out plainly so it can be pointed at. It is what a formatter
  does, and it is close enough to what a model does when it reads a list of results top to bottom.
- `re.search(r"KB-\d+", first)` pulls the article number out of the first result. Nothing here is
  clever; the point is that a completely reasonable piece of code produces a completely wrong answer.

Run both arms:

```bash
cd days/day-46-sessions-vs-memory/lab

uv run python wrongpast.py --stopwords
```

**Line by line:**

- The first arm sends the question as written.
- `--stopwords` removes the nine words. Same store, same question, same everything else. **Zero model
  calls in either arm.**

```text
    [4508] The customer reports the export runs but the file is empty. Fix: KB-512.

what an agent reading the first result tells the customer: KB-201

```

```text

query sent to search_memory: '4610 keeps getting logged out'

memories returned: 1 of 4 filed
    [4521] The customer keeps getting logged out. Fix: KB-104, set SameSite on the cookie.

what an agent reading the first result tells the customer: KB-104
what 4610 actually needs                                  : KB-104
```

**Four of four.** A question about being logged out returned a printing problem, a refund, the right
answer, and an empty export. Every one of them matched on the word `the`, or `customer`, or `on`.

And the right one is **third**. Nothing about the response says so. A model handed those four in that
order, or a formatter showing the first, reaches KB-201 — an article about re-issuing a PDF invoice,
sent to a customer who cannot stay logged in.

The ablation is nine words long and it fixes the run completely: four results become one, and it is
the right one. Which is encouraging for about ten seconds, until you notice that `customer` had to be
in the list because *this* archive says "customer" in every row, that `4610` survived the filter and
matched nothing (the archive has no 4610), and that the next agent who types *"any past issues with
sign-in?"* still gets zero results, because the stored text says "logged out"
([2.2](../02-the-interface/2.2-the-silence-that-lets-you-swap.md) measured that miss).

Stopword removal is a filter tuned to one archive by hand. It is not a retriever.

Verified on 2026-09-05 against `.venv/Lib/site-packages/google/adk/memory/in_memory_memory_service.py`:
`_extract_words_lower` is `set(word.lower() for word in re.findall(r'\w+', text, re.UNICODE))`, the
match is the `any(...)` line quoted above, and results are appended in iteration order with no sort
anywhere in the file.

## When it breaks

It has already broken; this whole part is the break. What is left is what the break **looks like from
outside**, because that is what you have to recognise.

There is no traceback. There is no warning. The tool result event says:

```text
    TOOL CALL   load_memory({'query': 'The customer on 4610 keeps getting logged out. What did we do last time?'})
    TOOL RESULT 4 memories
```

`4 memories` is the only signal, and it is a signal only if somebody knows that four is too many.
Under `preload_memory` even that is absent — the retrieval is not an event at all
([3.4](../03-three-wires/3.4-the-past-pushed-in-before-the-turn.md)) — and the first evidence anybody
gets is a customer replying *"this article is about invoices"*.

The second failure shape is the one that hides behind rule 2's cap. Cap the results at three and the
run above returns 4402, 4455 and 4521 — the wrong answer, another wrong answer, and the right one,
in that order. **The cap made the response smaller and no more correct**, because you cannot take the
top three of a list that has no top. A cap is a cost control. It is not a precision control, and
treating it as one is how a team convinces itself the problem is solved.

The third is the attempted fix that makes it worse. Somebody notices the misses from
[2.2](../02-the-interface/2.2-the-silence-that-lets-you-swap.md) and adds synonyms to the query —
`"logged out logout signin sign-in authentication session cookie"` — so that more phrasings hit. Every
added word is another chance to match an unrelated ticket, because the rule is `any`. Broadening a
query against an `any`-match store increases recall and destroys precision at the same time, and the
result is a store that returns everything to every question.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead of the teaching version.** Three things, in increasing order of
how much they cost.

The cheap one is to **log the query and the count** on every retrieval, and to alert when the count is
close to the store size. A search that returns 90% of everything is not a search, and that ratio is a
one-line metric.

The medium one is to **make the model do less with the result**. Instead of handing back a list, hand
back a list with an instruction that the agent must quote the ticket number it is relying on. That
does not improve retrieval, but it turns a silent wrong answer into a checkable one, because a human
reading *"like ticket 4402"* against a login problem can see the mismatch instantly. This is Day 21's
[3.2](../../../day-21-errors-surface-not-swallow/parts/03-policy/3.2-a-substitute-must-say-so.md)
applied to memory: a substitute must say what it is.

The expensive and correct one is to **replace the matcher**, which is Day 49. Embeddings put "logged
out" and "sign-in trouble" near each other and put "refund on the annual plan" far away, and — the
part that matters most here — they produce a **distance**, which means the list can be sorted and
cut at a threshold. The absence of a score is the root problem in this part, not the absence of
synonyms.

**What degrades at scale or under concurrency.** Precision degrades with store size, and it degrades
faster than intuition suggests. Four filed tickets gave four false-ish positives; thirty gave sixty
([4.1](../04-the-choice/4.1-pricing-both-in-tokens.md)'s `--big` run); five hundred gives five
hundred ([5.4](5.4-the-store-that-outgrew-its-value.md)). Every one of those is a chance for
`memories[0]` to be wrong, and the probability that the first result is the right one falls roughly
as one over the store size. A system that was right most of the time in the pilot is wrong nearly
always by the end of the year, with no deploy in between.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic.** The queries. In testing you write three
queries and they are all short and well chosen, because you know what you are testing. Real queries
are whole sentences with greetings in them, and the model — under `load_memory` — sometimes passes
the user's message straight through. You will not see that until you log it.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"We show `memories[0]` as 'the closest past case'.
The store returns an unordered list with no scores, so that index is meaningless — in the run I just
did it was a printing ticket answering a login question. Until retrieval returns a score, do not
present any result as the closest, and make the agent name the ticket it is quoting so a human can
catch it."*

**The interview question:** *"What is the worst thing that goes wrong with agent memory?"* An honest
answer: *"Not forgetting. Confidently remembering the wrong thing. I had a store of four support
tickets and a normal-sounding question — 'the customer on 4610 keeps getting logged out, what did we
do last time?' — and it matched all four, because the matcher counts a single shared word and
'customer' is in every ticket. The right ticket was third in the list, and the list has no ranking and
no scores, so anything reading the first result told the customer to apply an article about invoices.
Nothing errored. That is the shape of the danger: a miss is visible because it is empty, and a wrong
hit is indistinguishable from a right one. The fixes I would rank as log the query and the result
count first, make the agent quote the ticket it is relying on second, and replace the matcher with
something that returns a distance third — because the deep problem is that there is no score to sort
on."*

# 5.4 — 💥 The store that outgrew its value

## One-line answer

Filing more cases makes the desk worse, not better: at five hundred filed cases one question matches
all five hundred, injects 48,175 characters — 10,588 tokens — and the chance that the case you needed
is the one being read is **0.2%**, while a cap of three holds the same store at 102 tokens forever.

## The story

There is a lost-property box behind the desk at the station.

In the first week of the term it is a genuinely good system. You leave your umbrella on the platform,
you come back, the man reaches under the counter and there are four things in the box: two umbrellas,
a lunch tin and a scarf. He holds up an umbrella and asks if it is yours. It takes no time at all and
it works.

By March the box is a crate, and the crate is full. You describe your umbrella — black, folding,
broken rib — and he sighs and heaves the crate onto the counter and says *have a look*. There are
about sixty umbrellas in it. Most of them are black. Four of them are folding with a broken rib and
you honestly cannot tell which one is yours, and you leave with one you are only fairly sure about.

Nobody threw anything away, and that is exactly the problem. The box got better at holding things and
worse at giving them back, and the day it crossed over from one to the other was not marked in any
way.

## The idea in plain language

Every part of this day has treated the store as something that helps. This part measures the point
where it stops.

Three quantities move when a store grows, and only one of them is the one people watch.

| Quantity | What happens as the store grows | Who notices |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Matches returned | Rises towards *everything*, because one shared word is a hit | Nobody — it is a list either way |
| Characters injected | Rises with the matches, linearly | The bill, eventually |
| Chance the right case is the one read | **Falls**, roughly as one over the store size | The customer |

The third row is the one this part is about, and it is worth being precise about what it means.
Suppose exactly one filed case is the one that answers today's question. If the search returns five
hundred cases and something downstream reads one of them — the first, or the one the model happens to
attend to — then the useful fraction of what came back is one in five hundred. The other 499 are
noise with the same shape, the same confidence and the same real article numbers in them
([5.1](5.1-the-past-that-matched-on-nothing.md)).

So the feature's value curve is not flat and it is not rising. It rises while the store is small,
peaks somewhere nobody measured, and falls. And there is no signal at the peak. The code is identical
on both sides of it. The tests pass on both sides of it. What changes is a number in a dictionary
that grows because the feature is being used, which means **the feature degrades in proportion to its
own success**.

The fix is a cap, and it is the same cap rule 2 of
[4.2](../04-the-choice/4.2-sutras-memory-policy.md) already made policy. What this part adds is the
shape of the curve with the cap in place: flat. Not better — *flat*. A cap does not make retrieval
more accurate; it stops the cost and the dilution growing without bound, and it makes the size of the
store somebody else's problem, which is exactly what you want a bound to do.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because Sutra's memory feature is switched on today with no cap and no expiry, and both of the
missing pieces belong to later days that will be written against these numbers.

Day 48 decides what is worth remembering and what to forget. That is a retention policy, and a
retention policy is unarguable in the abstract — everybody agrees that keeping less is tidier, and
nobody deletes anything on the strength of tidiness. The argument that works is the third row of the
table above: *at five hundred cases, 99.8% of what we hand the model is wrong, and we are paying
10,588 tokens a turn for the privilege.* That is a number Day 48 can act on.

Day 49 replaces the matcher with embeddings and gets a **score**, which makes a cap meaningful — the
top three of a ranked list is a real answer, where the top three of an unranked list is three
arbitrary rows ([5.1](5.1-the-past-that-matched-on-nothing.md)). This part is what makes Day 49
urgent rather than interesting.

And [4.1](../04-the-choice/4.1-pricing-both-in-tokens.md) ended on a sentence this part is the proof
of: **the cap matters more than the policy.** That was measured on one ten-turn conversation at two
store sizes. Here it is measured across five store sizes with the conversation taken out of the way.

## The mechanism

One question, five store sizes, and one flag that caps the result.

```python
# days/day-46-sessions-vs-memory/lab/growth.py
CHARS_PER_TOKEN = 4.55  # Day 24, part 1.1
QUESTION = "The customer on 4610 keeps getting logged out. Any past case?"
SIZES = [1, 5, 25, 100, 500]


def block(memories) -> str:
    """The text preload_memory builds, copied from preload_memory_tool.py."""
    lines = []
    for memory in memories:
        if memory.timestamp:
            lines.append(f"Time: {memory.timestamp}")
        text = " ".join(p.text for p in (memory.content.parts or []) if p.text)
        lines.append(f"{memory.author}: {text}" if memory.author else text)
    joined = "\n".join(lines)
    return (
        "The following content is from your previous conversations with the user.\n"
        "They may be useful for answering the user's current query.\n"
        f"<PAST_CONVERSATIONS>\n{joined}\n</PAST_CONVERSATIONS>\n"
    )
```

**Line by line:**

- `CHARS_PER_TOKEN = 4.55` is Day 24's measured constant for English prose against this model's
  tokenizer, reused here rather than calling `count_tokens`, because counting tokens is a network
  call and this day spends none
  ([1.1](../../../day-24-token-accounting-and-budgets/parts/01-what-a-request-costs/1.1-a-ticket-id-costs-five-tokens.md)).
- `SIZES` climbs by roughly five times a step. A linear sweep would need far more runs to show the
  same thing; the interesting behaviour is over orders of magnitude, because that is how an archive
  actually grows.
- `block` is **copied from ADK's own `preload_memory_tool.py`**, not approximated. The wrapper text,
  the `Time:` line, the `author: text` shape and the `<PAST_CONVERSATIONS>` tags are what the
  framework really injects ([3.4](../03-three-wires/3.4-the-past-pushed-in-before-the-turn.md)), and
  measuring a paraphrase of it would measure the paraphrase.
- `if memory.timestamp` adds a `Time:` line per entry. It looks trivial and it is roughly a fifth of
  the injected characters at every store size — a per-entry overhead that multiplies with the result
  count.
- The function takes the memories it is given rather than searching, so the same builder is used for
  the capped and uncapped arms and no difference between them can come from the formatting.

```python
# days/day-46-sessions-vs-memory/lab/growth.py (continued)
async def store_of(n: int) -> InMemoryMemoryService:
    sessions = InMemorySessionService()
    memory = InMemoryMemoryService()
    for i in range(n):
        session = await sessions.create_session(app_name=APP, user_id=USER, session_id=f"t{i}")
        await sessions.append_event(
            session,
            said("desk", f"Ticket 4{i:03d}: the customer keeps getting logged out. Fix KB-104.", 0),
        )
        await memory.add_session_to_memory(session)
    return memory
```

**Line by line:**

- `store_of(n)` builds a **fresh** store for each size rather than adding to one, so every row of the
  table below is an independent measurement and nothing carries over between them.
- `session_id=f"t{i}"` gives every case its own session id, which matters: `add_session_to_memory`
  keys on `session.id`, so reusing one id would overwrite rather than accumulate and the store would
  never grow ([2.4](../02-the-interface/2.4-the-bucket-is-a-person.md)).
- One event per case, so *n* filed cases means *n* stored memories and the arithmetic in the table is
  readable. A real ticket is several events, so every number below is an underestimate.
- The ticket text is the **same sentence** every time, with only the number changing. That is the
  honest worst case and it is not far from reality: a support archive is full of the same fault
  reported by different people, which is the entire reason anybody wants this feature.
- `useful = 1 / len(memories)` in the caller assumes exactly one of the matched cases is the one you
  needed. It is an assumption, it is stated in the script's own output, and it is generous — a real
  archive often holds none.

Run both arms:

```bash

```

**Line by line:**

- The first arm is Sutra as it stands today: whatever `search_memory` returns is what gets used.
- `--topk` sets the cap to three — `Capped`'s default in
  [4.1](../04-the-choice/4.1-pricing-both-in-tokens.md) — and changes nothing else. Same store, same
  question, same builder.
- **Zero model calls in both arms.** There is no model in this script at all.

Measured on 2026-09-05:

```text
one question, uncapped

     cases filed  matched   injected   tokens   useful
               1        1             271       60  100.0%
               5        5             655      144   20.0%
              25       25            2575      566    4.0%
             100      100            9775     2148    1.0%
             500      500           48175    10588    0.2%
```

```text
one question, capped at 3 results

     cases filed  matched   injected   tokens   useful
               1        1             271       60  100.0%
               5        3             463      102   33.3%
              25        3             463      102   33.3%
             100        3             463      102   33.3%
             500        3             463      102   33.3%
```

Read the `matched` column in the first table before anything else. **Every filed case matches every
time.** Not most, not a majority — all of them, at every size, because the query and the archive both
contain the word `the`. There is no size at which this search excludes anything.

Then the `useful` column: 100%, 20%, 4%, 1%, **0.2%**. The desk got five hundred times more
experienced and five hundred times less likely to be reading the right case, and every one of those
rows is the same code.

Now the second table, which has one interesting property: **it stops changing.** From five filed
cases to five hundred, the cost is 102 tokens and the useful fraction is 33.3%. The store may grow to
five thousand and those two numbers will not move. That is what a bound is for — not to make the
system better, but to stop the store's size being an input to the system's behaviour.

And note what the cap did **not** do. It did not improve accuracy. The three returned cases are the
first three in insertion order out of five hundred equally-matching rows, so the chance the right one
is among them is worse than it was uncapped — 33.3% of a smaller haystack, but three arbitrary rows
out of five hundred. The cap bought cost and predictability. It bought no correctness at all, and a
team that reads the second table as *"fixed"* has misread it.

Verified on 2026-09-05 against `.venv/Lib/site-packages/google/adk/memory/in_memory_memory_service.py`
— `search_memory` walks every stored event in the bucket, matches on
`any(query_word in words_in_event for query_word in words_in_query)`, and appends without sorting or
limiting — and against `.venv/Lib/site-packages/google/adk/tools/preload_memory_tool.py`, from which
`block` is copied. Confirmed against `https://adk.dev/sessions/memory/`, which describes
`InMemoryMemoryService` as performing *"basic keyword matching for searches"* and offers no cap
parameter anywhere on the service.

## When it breaks

It breaks by working. That is what makes it hard to catch, so here are the four disguises it wears.

**The 429 that looks like a provider problem.** At 10,588 tokens of injected history a turn, a
conversation that used to fit inside the free tier's daily allowance stops fitting
([4.1](../04-the-choice/4.1-pricing-both-in-tokens.md)), and the first visible symptom is the refusal
Day 24 captured:

```text
429 RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED. {'error': {'code': 429, 'message': '... Quota exceeded for metric: generativelanguage.googleapis.com/generate_content_free_tier_requests, limit: 20, model: gemini-3.7-flash\nPlease retry in 26.45714483s.', 'status': 'RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED'}}
```

Nothing in that message mentions memory. The team reads it as a quota problem and asks for a bigger
quota, which buys another few months of the same curve.

**The context window, which does not send a 429 at all.** Past a certain size the injected block
crowds out the conversation itself, and the model starts ignoring the middle of what it was sent —
Day 19's [3.3](../../../day-19-context-engineering-selection/parts/03-selection/3.3-position-is-not-presence.md)
is the measurement. The symptom is an agent that forgets what the user said two turns ago **because
you gave it too much memory**, which is the least intuitive sentence in this phase.

**Latency with no error.** `InMemoryMemoryService.search_memory` takes a lock, copies every event
list in the bucket, and then walks them. There is no index. That is linear in the store on every
single query, and under concurrency it is linear in the store while holding a lock.

**The pilot that proved it works.** The most dangerous disguise. A pilot store is small, so the pilot
measures the top of the curve and everybody signs off on a number that will never be seen again.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead of the teaching version.** Three things, none of them large.

The cap is a **named constant with its reasoning attached** — `TOP_K = 3  # sized against a 2k-token
memory budget at ~200 chars/entry; revisit when the retriever returns scores` — and it is applied in
the assembly function so no call site can forget it (rule 2 of
[4.2](../04-the-choice/4.2-sutras-memory-policy.md)).

The **store size is a metric**, emitted at startup and on a timer. Not a log line — a number on a
graph, because the failure in this part is a slope and you cannot see a slope in a log.

And the ratio is an **alert**: matches returned divided by store size. When a search returns most of
the store, the search is not selecting anything, and that ratio going to one is the single cheapest
detector for everything in this part. It is one division.

**What degrades at scale or under concurrency.** All three columns, and they degrade together, which
is why this reads as a mysterious general slowdown rather than a memory problem. Cost rises linearly
with the store. Precision falls as one over the store. Search time rises linearly with the store,
inside a lock, so under concurrency the searches queue behind each other and the p99 rises faster
than the mean. A managed store fixes the third of those and only the third — vector search is fast at
five hundred thousand entries and it still returns whatever you asked for, so an uncapped query
against a managed store is the same dilution at a much larger scale.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic.** Homogeneity. This lab's archive is
deliberately uniform — five hundred variations of one sentence — and a real archive looks more varied,
so the first instinct is that the numbers here are pessimistic. They are not, for the reason
[5.1](5.1-the-past-that-matched-on-nothing.md) gave: the words that make a real archive varied are
nouns, and the words that make everything match are `the`, `on`, `a` and `customer`. Variety does not
help when the matcher is `any`.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"There is no cap on the retrieval and no metric on
the store size, so this feature gets more expensive and less accurate every week it is used and
nothing in our dashboards will show it. Put the cap in `build_memory_service` as a named constant
with the budget it was sized against, emit the store size, and alert on matches-over-store-size. That
last one is a division and it is the only thing that would have caught this before the bill did."*

**The interview question:** *"What happens to an agent's memory as it fills up?"* An honest answer:
*"It gets worse, and the failure is invisible because the code never changes. I measured it on ADK's
in-memory store: one filed case, one match, sixty tokens injected. Five hundred filed cases, five
hundred matches, ten and a half thousand tokens injected — and if exactly one of those cases is the
one you needed, the useful fraction of what you handed the model is 0.2%. The matcher counts one
shared word as a hit, so every query matches the whole archive, and there is no ranking to cut on.
Capping at three results holds the cost at about a hundred tokens no matter how big the store gets,
which is the right thing to do, but I would be careful how I described it: the cap fixes cost and
predictability, not accuracy, because the top three of an unranked list is three arbitrary rows. The
accuracy fix is a retriever that returns a distance so the list can be sorted and cut at a threshold.
And the cheapest early-warning signal is the ratio of matches returned to store size — when a search
starts returning most of the store, it has stopped being a search."*

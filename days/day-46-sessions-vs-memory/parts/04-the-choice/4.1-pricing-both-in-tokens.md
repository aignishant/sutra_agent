# 4.1 — Pricing both, in tokens

## One-line answer

Measured over one ten-turn conversation, `preload_memory` costs **1,898 tokens** and ten model
requests where `load_memory` costs **3,130 tokens and eleven requests** for a single lookup, and
**7,927 tokens and fifteen requests** for five — because a tool result is written into the transcript
and re-sent on every later turn, while a preloaded block is transient.

## The story

Two phone plans, printed on the same laminated card at the shop.

The first one charges you per call. Nothing on the days you do not ring anybody. The second charges a
flat monthly rental and the calls are included. The man behind the counter will tell you the second
is for "heavy users", and he is not wrong exactly, but he has never asked you how many calls you
made last month.

So you go home and look. Thirty-one days, and you count. Fourteen calls, most of them short, three of
them long. And on the back of an envelope the answer is embarrassingly clear, and it is not the one
you assumed, because the per-call plan bills you a connection charge on every call including the
eight-second ones and you had forgotten that.

The plan is not the interesting part. Counting is the interesting part, and you can only count what
you have written down.

## The idea in plain language

Both memory policies cost something. The costs are made of different things, so a comparison has to
add up all of them.

**`load_memory` costs:**

1. The tool declaration, on every request — a tool's schema is sent with the request whether the tool
   is used or not (Day 19's
   [2.3](../../../day-19-context-engineering-selection/parts/02-anatomy/2.3-the-menu-costs-more-than-the-handbook.md)).
2. A paragraph appended to the system instruction, on every request: 219 characters against 63,
   measured in [3.3](../03-three-wires/3.3-the-tool-the-model-may-call.md).
3. **One extra model request** for every turn on which the model decides to look.
4. The retrieved memories, which land in the session as a function-response event and are therefore
   **re-sent on every subsequent turn of the conversation** — Day 24's
   [1.2](../../../day-24-token-accounting-and-budgets/parts/01-what-a-request-costs/1.2-charged-again-every-turn.md),
   which measured an eight-turn conversation of 151 tokens costing 786 tokens to conduct.

**`preload_memory` costs:**

1. The injected `<PAST_CONVERSATIONS>` block, on **every** turn.
2. Nothing else. No tool declaration worth speaking of, no instruction paragraph, no extra request,
   and — because the injection is transient — **no accumulation**.

Cost 4 on the left is the one everybody forgets, and it is the one that decides the answer. A tool
result is a normal event. It sits in the conversation. Turn seven re-sends turn two's memory dump
along with everything else, for the whole life of the session.

Two more things before the numbers, both of which the measurement has to respect.

**Requests, not tokens, are what the free tier rations.** Day 24's
[1.3](../../../day-24-token-accounting-and-budgets/parts/01-what-a-request-costs/1.3-the-unit-that-gets-rationed.md)
read it off a real refusal: the quota metric is `generate_content_free_tier_requests` with
`quotaValue: '20'`. So the request column in the table below is not a footnote. Fifteen requests out
of twenty is three quarters of a day's allowance for one conversation.

**The answer depends on the hit rate.** How often does a turn actually need the past? Nobody knows
that in the abstract, so the measurement sweeps it: none of ten turns, one of ten, five of ten.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because the conventional answer is *"default to the tool — it costs nothing when history is
irrelevant"*, and that sentence is a claim about numbers that almost nobody has checked. Sutra has
spent a whole day (Day 24) learning to count before choosing, and this is the first decision in
Phase 7 where the counting changes the conclusion.

[4.2](4.2-sutras-memory-policy.md) is Sutra's actual verdict, and it goes **against** the cheaper
option for reasons that are not about cost. That verdict is only worth anything if the cost is
measured first and stated honestly, including the part that argues the other way.

## The mechanism

Run the same ten-turn conversation under three policies, weigh every request that goes out, and sweep
the hit rate.

```python
# days/day-46-sessions-vs-memory/lab/price.py
# Day 24, part 1.1: English prose measured at 4.55 characters per token for gemini-3.7-flash.
CHARS_PER_TOKEN = 4.55

HIT_RATES = {"0 of 10": set(), "1 of 10": {0}, "5 of 10": {0, 2, 4, 6, 8}}


def json_len(model: object) -> int:
    """Characters this object costs on the wire, as compact JSON rather than a pretty repr."""
    return len(model.model_dump_json(exclude_none=True)) if model is not None else 0


def weigh(request: LlmRequest) -> int:
    """Characters in one request: the instruction, the tool menu, and every content part."""
    total = len(str(request.config.system_instruction or ""))
    for declaration in request.config.tools or []:
        total += json_len(declaration)
    for content in request.contents:
        for part in content.parts or []:
            total += len(part.text or "")
            total += json_len(part.function_call)
            total += json_len(part.function_response)
    return total
```

**Line by line:**

- `CHARS_PER_TOKEN = 4.55` comes from Day 24's
  [1.1](../../../day-24-token-accounting-and-budgets/parts/01-what-a-request-costs/1.1-a-ticket-id-costs-five-tokens.md),
  where English prose was measured against the provider's own tokenizer. It is a constant with a
  citation, not a rule of thumb, and it is used here rather than calling `count_tokens` because
  counting tokens is a network call and this day's budget is zero.
- `HIT_RATES` maps a label to the **set of turn indices on which the model decides to search**. Making
  it a set of indices rather than a probability keeps the run deterministic: the same turns search in
  every arm, so the arms are comparable.
- `json_len(model)` uses `model_dump_json(exclude_none=True)` rather than `str(model)`. This matters
  more than it looks: `str()` on a pydantic object gives a pretty-printed repr with newlines and
  indentation, which inflated the tool declaration by roughly 6,000 characters in the first version
  of this script and made `load_memory` look far worse than it is. Compact JSON is what actually
  crosses the wire.
- `exclude_none=True` drops unset optional fields, which the provider's serialiser also omits.
- `weigh` counts three things separately — the instruction, the tool declarations, the content parts —
  because those are the three cost centres in the list above, and a single total would hide which one
  moved.
- `part.function_call` and `part.function_response` are weighed as JSON because that is what a tool
  call and its result actually are on the wire. Counting only `part.text` would score every
  `load_memory` result as zero, which is the exact mistake this measurement exists to avoid.

```python
# days/day-46-sessions-vs-memory/lab/price.py (continued)
class Capped(BaseMemoryService):
    """A memory service that wraps another and returns at most `k` memories."""

    def __init__(self, inner: BaseMemoryService, k: int = 3) -> None:
        self._inner, self._k = inner, k

    async def add_session_to_memory(self, session) -> None:
        await self._inner.add_session_to_memory(session)

    async def search_memory(self, *, app_name: str, user_id: str, query: str):
        found = await self._inner.search_memory(app_name=app_name, user_id=user_id, query=query)
        found.memories = found.memories[: self._k]
        return found
```

**Line by line:**

- This is the wrapper [2.2](../02-the-interface/2.2-the-silence-that-lets-you-swap.md) promised: a
  complete `BaseMemoryService` in ten lines whose only job is to cap the result. It is here because
  the uncapped numbers turn out to be dominated by a bad retriever rather than by the policy, and the
  only way to separate those two effects is to remove one of them.
- `add_session_to_memory` **delegates** rather than reimplementing. A wrapper that stored things
  itself would be a second store.
- `found.memories = found.memories[: self._k]` mutates the response in place and returns the same
  object. Building a new `SearchMemoryResponse` would work equally well; mutating keeps the wrapper
  to one line and makes it obvious that nothing else about the response is touched.
- The two optional methods are **not** overridden, so calls to them fall through to
  `BaseMemoryService`'s `NotImplementedError`. That is a real limitation of this wrapper and it is
  named rather than hidden; [2.1](../02-the-interface/2.1-two-promises-and-two-refusals.md)'s
  production section says what a careful version does instead.

Run the sweep, three ways:

```bash
cd days/day-46-sessions-vs-memory/lab
uv run python price.py
uv run python price.py --big
uv run python price.py --big --topk
```

**Line by line:**

- The first arm is a store with three past cases: a demo-sized archive.
- `--big` is thirty past cases. Still tiny by any real measure, and enough to show the curve.
- `--topk` wraps the store in `Capped`, so retrieval returns three memories however many match. This
  is the ablation that separates *"the policy is expensive"* from *"the retriever is bad"*.
- **Zero model calls in all three arms.** The model is `ScriptedModel`.

Measured on 2026-09-05, three past cases:

```text
one ten-turn session, 3 past cases in the store, retrieval uncapped

    turns needing the past   policy            reqs    chars  tokens
    0 of 10                  none                10     2786     612
                             load_memory         10     7386    1623
                             preload_memory      10     8638    1898

    1 of 10                  none                10     2786     612
                             load_memory         11    14240    3130
                             preload_memory      10     8638    1898

    5 of 10                  none                10     2786     612
                             load_memory         15    36069    7927
                             preload_memory      10     8638    1898
```

Read the `preload_memory` column first: **1,898 tokens and ten requests in all three blocks**. It does
not move, because preloading does not care whether a turn needed the past. That is the flat rental.

Now `load_memory`. At zero lookups it is **1,623 tokens** — cheaper than preloading, and the gap is
the 275 tokens preloading spends injecting history nobody asked for. That is the conventional wisdom,
and it holds, at zero.

At **one** lookup in ten turns it is 3,130 tokens and preloading wins. At five it is 7,927 and eleven
requests become fifteen. The line crosses somewhere below one lookup in ten, which is a far lower hit
rate than anyone expects.

**Why?** Because the tool result is an event. The memories fetched on turn one are in the transcript
for turns two through ten, re-sent nine more times. Preloading's block is re-sent too — but it is
rebuilt fresh each turn and never accumulates, so ten turns pay for ten blocks rather than for one
block ten times *plus* the growing transcript that carries it.

Now the store grows to thirty cases:

```text
one ten-turn session, 30 past cases in the store, retrieval uncapped

    turns needing the past   policy            reqs    chars  tokens
    0 of 10                  none                10     2786     612
                             load_memory         10     7386    1623
                             preload_memory      10    48706   10705

    1 of 10                  none                10     2786     612
                             load_memory         11    60680   13336
                             preload_memory      10    48706   10705

    5 of 10                  none                10     2786     612
                             load_memory         15   221829   48754
                             preload_memory      10    48706   10705
```

Both explode. Preloading goes from 1,898 to **10,705** tokens with no code change at all, and
`load_memory` at five lookups reaches **48,754** — eighty times the no-memory baseline. The store grew
by a factor of ten and the bill grew with it, because the retriever returns everything that shares a
word and every query here shares words with everything.

So cap the retriever and run again:

```text
one ten-turn session, 30 past cases in the store, retrieval capped at the best 3

    turns needing the past   policy            reqs    chars  tokens
    0 of 10                  none                10     2786     612
                             load_memory         10     7386    1623
                             preload_memory      10     6482    1425

    1 of 10                  none                10     2786     612
                             load_memory         11    14240    3130
                             preload_memory      10     6482    1425

    5 of 10                  none                10     2786     612
                             load_memory         15    36069    7927
                             preload_memory      10     6482    1425
```

**Thirty cases behave like three.** Preloading drops from 10,705 to 1,425 tokens and `load_memory` at
five lookups from 48,754 to 7,927 — the same numbers as the three-case run, because a cap of three
makes the store size irrelevant.

That is the most useful row in this part, and it is not about `load_memory` versus `preload_memory` at
all. **The cap matters more than the policy.** Choosing the wrong policy costs a factor of two.
Leaving the retriever uncapped costs a factor of six, and it costs it silently, later, as the store
fills.

## When it breaks

The measurement itself breaks in two ways worth naming, because a wrong number is worse than no
number.

**Weighing a pretty repr instead of the wire format.** The first version of `weigh` used
`str(declaration)` for tool schemas:

```text
    load_memory         11    20270    4455
```

against the `model_dump_json` figure of `14240` / `3130` for the identical run. Six thousand
characters of pydantic indentation, attributed to a policy that never sent them. If you measure
prompt size anywhere, measure the serialised form.

**Counting only text.** Drop the two `json_len(part.function_call)` lines and every `load_memory`
result weighs zero, and the tool looks free at any hit rate. The most expensive thing in the
`load_memory` column is a function-response part, and function-response parts have no `.text`.

The system it measures breaks differently, and the transcripts above are the evidence. At thirty
stored cases and five lookups, a ten-turn conversation costs 48,754 tokens. On a free tier that is
not a bill, it is a **context window** problem and then a rate-limit problem:

```text
429 RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED. {'error': {'code': 429, 'message': '... Quota exceeded for metric: generativelanguage.googleapis.com/generate_content_free_tier_requests, limit: 20, model: gemini-3.7-flash\nPlease retry in 26.45714483s.', 'status': 'RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED'}}
```

— the refusal Day 24 captured live and recorded in
[1.3](../../../day-24-token-accounting-and-budgets/parts/01-what-a-request-costs/1.3-the-unit-that-gets-rationed.md),
and which Day 24's
[2.2](../../../day-24-token-accounting-and-budgets/parts/02-two-ceilings/2.2-reading-the-ceiling-off-a-refusal.md)
taught you to read the `quotaValue` off. Fifteen requests for one conversation against a ceiling of
twenty means the second conversation of the day fails, and the failure will be blamed on the model.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead of the teaching version.** They do not choose once and move on;
they instrument. The two numbers that decide this — the fraction of turns that actually needed the
past, and the size of what came back — are both measurable in production and neither is guessable in
design. So the production shape is: pick a default, log `memory.lookups` and `memory.result_chars`
per invocation, and revisit with a week of real traffic. `preload_memory` makes the first of those
hard to log, which is one more reason
[4.2](4.2-sutras-memory-policy.md) goes the way it does.

**What degrades at scale or under concurrency.** Everything in the second table. The cost of memory
grows with the store, not with your code, so a system that was correctly sized at launch is
mis-sized six months later with nothing in the changelog to explain it. Under concurrency the two
policies stress different things: `load_memory` doubles the number of model requests, which is the
scarce and rate-limited resource, while `preload_memory` doubles the number of store reads, which is
usually cheap and local. On a free tier, requests are the thing you run out of.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic.** The hit rate. Nobody's estimate of "how
often does a question need history" survives contact with real users, and the answer varies by hour
of the day. A policy chosen for a hit rate of one in ten and deployed into a workload with a hit rate
of one in two is off by a factor of two and a half on requests.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"This compares two policies at a hit rate we made
up, over a store of three items, with an uncapped retriever. The uncapped part is the finding — at
thirty items the injected block is ten thousand tokens and nothing in the code changed. Cap the
retrieval, then re-run, and make the cap a named constant so the next person can see what number we
chose."*

**The interview question:** *"How would you decide between a memory retrieval tool and automatic
injection?"* An honest answer: *"By measuring, and the measurement surprised me. The folk answer is
that the tool is cheaper because it only costs when it fires, and that is only true at a hit rate
close to zero. I ran a ten-turn conversation both ways: with no lookups the tool was cheaper, 1,623
tokens against 1,898, but with one lookup in ten turns the tool cost 3,130 and injection stayed at
1,898, and at five lookups it was 7,927 against 1,898. The reason is that a tool result is written
into the session, so it is re-sent on every later turn, whereas the injected block is transient and
rebuilt each turn. The bigger finding was that neither number mattered as much as the cap: at thirty
stored cases with an uncapped retriever the injected block alone was ten thousand tokens, and putting
a top-three wrapper in front of the store brought it back to fourteen hundred. So my answer is
measure your hit rate, but cap your retrieval first."*

# 6.1 — The connection you must not hold

## One-line answer

Nothing in the 2026-07-28 protocol requires a client to keep anything open between requests, so
anything your client *does* keep open is an optimisation that infrastructure is allowed to take away
from you — and every piece of infrastructure eventually does.

## The story

The bay outside the shop is for short stops. The sign says so, and everybody who parks there knows it.

You park there on a Tuesday to collect one thing, and then the man behind the counter has to look in
the back, and then the card machine will not connect, and then you remember you needed the other
thing as well. Twenty past by the time you come out. The car is fine.

The following Tuesday it is not fine. Same bay, same shop, roughly the same errand, and there is a
notice under the wiper. Nothing about your behaviour changed and nothing about the sign changed. What
changed is that a warden walked down the street on one Tuesday and not the other.

The mistake was never the twenty minutes. It was building a routine on top of a permission you did
not have, and getting away with it for long enough that it stopped feeling like a risk. You cannot
argue with the warden about how reliably this worked in the past.

## The idea in plain language

There are two completely different things people mean by "connection", and this part exists because
conflating them is how a working system becomes a flaky one.

**A protocol connection** is state the two ends agree exists and depend on: a session identifier, a
negotiated version, a memory of who you are. The 2026-07-28 revision deleted all of it. There is no
`initialize`, no `Mcp-Session-Id`, and no standing GET stream
([3.1](../03-streamable-http/3.1-one-endpoint-one-trip.md)). Nothing about request number two depends
on request number one having happened.

**A transport connection** is a TCP socket, a TLS session, an `httpx` pool entry, or a child process.
Those are real, they are expensive to create, and reusing them is a genuine and worthwhile
optimisation.

The rule that falls out is short: **reuse transport connections, depend on none of them.** A client
that reuses a pooled socket and reconnects transparently when it goes away is doing exactly the right
thing. A client that would behave differently — lose work, need a re-handshake, return an error the
caller sees — because a socket was closed underneath it has turned an optimisation into a dependency.

And sockets do go away, constantly, for reasons that have nothing to do with you:

- Load balancers cap idle time, and sixty seconds is a common default.
- Cloud proxies cap total request duration regardless of activity.
- A rolling deployment replaces every instance, and every connection to them.
- Autoscaling removes an instance in the middle of the night.
- A corporate proxy or NAT drops an idle flow with no notice to either end.

None of those is a failure. They are the normal operation of the systems your server runs on, and the
protocol's statelessness is precisely what makes them survivable. Day 32's argument was about the
*server* being free of held state
([2.2](../../../day-32-mcp-stateless-core/parts/02-the-reframe/2.2-three-instances-one-url.md)); this
part is the client-side half of the same property.

The stdio case looks different and is the same. A child process *is* held — that is what stdio is —
but the specification already says what to do when it dies:
*"If the server process exits unexpectedly, the client SHOULD restart it. Because the protocol is
stateless, any in-flight requests are simply lost and the client can retry them against the fresh
process."* ([2.4](../02-stdio/2.4-closing-up.md)). Restart and retry, again, because there is nothing
to reconstruct.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because Sutra is about to be deployed behind exactly the infrastructure described above, and because
Day 44 is named for this.

`sutra/mcp/hardening.py` gets `with_retries`, `with_timeout` and, in the brief's own words, *the
no-held-connection rule*. That rule is only writable if today's client did not build a dependency on a
held connection into its shape. The three functions this day asks for — `connect_stdio`,
`connect_http`, `list_tools` — must therefore be things you can call again after everything underneath
them has been replaced, without any caller noticing.

There is a subtler reason too, and it is about what you are allowed to cache. The tool-list TTL from
[1.3](../01-the-connector/1.3-before-the-model-sees-a-tool.md) is a client-side cache with a clock on
it, and it is legitimate because the protocol says listings are cacheable. A client-side memory of
"which instance answered last time", or "the state handle I got is valid on this connection", is not
legitimate, and the difference between them is exactly the difference between the two meanings of
connection above. Where cross-call state genuinely exists, it travels as an explicit handle in the
payload
([Day 32's 3.4](../../../day-32-mcp-stateless-core/parts/03-headers-and-caches/3.4-state-that-travels-in-the-payload.md))
— which is to say it survives the socket dying, on purpose.

## The mechanism

What may be kept, and what may be depended on:

| Thing | May the client reuse it? | May the client depend on it? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| a pooled TCP/TLS connection | yes, and should | no |
| a stdio child process | yes, and should | no — restart and retry |
| a cached `tools/list`, with a TTL | yes | only until the TTL expires |
| a state handle from a tool result | yes — it is payload | yes, it is server-side and portable |
| "the instance that answered last time" | no | no |
| a session identifier | there is none | there is none |

The shape that satisfies the right-hand column is a function you can call twice:

```python
async def list_tools(toolset: McpToolset) -> list[str]:
    """Names of the tools this server currently offers.

    Safe to call again after anything underneath has been replaced: the client
    reconnects if it has to, and no caller can tell.
    """
    tools = await toolset.get_tools()
    return [tool.name for tool in tools]
```

**Line by line:**

- It takes the toolset as an argument rather than owning one. Ownership and lifetime belong to the
  caller ([1.5](../01-the-connector/1.5-somebody-has-to-give-it-back.md)), and a helper that quietly
  held its own would be the exact dependency this part is about.
- It returns plain strings, not the `McpTool` objects. A caller holding live tool objects across a
  reconnect is holding references into a session manager that may have replaced itself; a list of
  names cannot go stale in a way that fails at a distance.
- There is no `if not connected: connect()` anywhere, because there is no `connected`. `get_tools()`
  establishes whatever it needs, every time.
- The docstring's second sentence is the contract. It is worth writing down, because the next person
  to read this function will want to add a cache to it, and the sentence tells them what the cache
  must not assume.

The reconnect is not something you write. It is already in the layer below, and reading it is the
fastest way to be sure — `mcp_session_manager.py` in google-adk 2.7.1, read 2026-09-04:

> *"Decorator to automatically retry action when MCP session errors occur. When MCP session errors
> occur, the decorator will automatically retry the action once. The create_session method will
> handle creating a new session..."*

**Line by line:**

- `retry_on_errors` wraps `get_tools()` and the tool-call path. A session error triggers one retry
  against a freshly created session.
- **Once**, not until it works. That is a deliberate ceiling: one retry covers a connection that went
  away between requests, and does not turn a genuinely broken server into a loop
  ([2.4](../02-stdio/2.4-closing-up.md)'s restart-loop failure).
- The retry creates a *new* session rather than reusing the failed one, which is only safe because
  there is nothing in a session to lose.
- Day 44's `with_retries` sits **above** this, at the tool-call level, with backoff and a policy. Two
  retry layers with different jobs, and knowing that this one exists is what stops you writing a
  third by accident.

## When it breaks

The way this breaks is that it does not, for a while, and then it does on a schedule you cannot see.

The commonest shape is an idle timeout at an intermediary. A client makes a call, does nothing for
longer than the load balancer's idle limit, and makes a second call on a socket that both ends have
been told is gone:

```text
httpx.RemoteProtocolError: Server disconnected without sending a response.
```

Nothing is broken. The socket was reclaimed, exactly as configured, by a component doing its job. A
client that reconnects and retries sees a millisecond of extra latency; a client that surfaces this
to the caller has just turned a routine event into a user-visible error, and the bug report will say
"it fails when I leave it for a while", which is true and unhelpful.

The second shape is the deployment. Every instance is replaced over a few minutes, so every
established connection dies, in a wave. A client that reconnects transparently produces a small blip
in a latency graph. A client that treats a dead connection as a failure produces an error rate that
tracks your deploys exactly, and somebody will eventually notice the correlation and conclude that
deployments are dangerous rather than that the client is wrong.

The third shape is the one that only bites over stdio, and it is worth naming because it is invisible
in a graph. The child process dies — out of memory, an unhandled exception, the container's OOM
killer — and a client that restarts it silently is now serving requests from a process that has
restarted eleven times this hour. The system is healthy by every measure you are collecting. The only
evidence is a counter that nobody made
([Day 22's 4.1](../../../day-22-structured-logging/parts/04-in-production/4.1-what-to-log-and-what-to-count.md)).

## In production

**What a professional writes instead:** connection reuse is left entirely to the HTTP library, with
its pool settings written down rather than defaulted, and a client-side idle limit set **shorter**
than the shortest limit in the path — so your side closes the socket deliberately instead of
discovering it closed. Everything above that layer is written to be re-callable, and there is a test
that kills the far end mid-run and asserts that the next call succeeds.

**What degrades at scale:** the thundering herd after a deployment. Every client reconnects at the
same instant, and a server that was comfortable serving steady traffic now takes every TLS handshake
at once. The mitigation is jitter on reconnect, and it belongs in Day 44's hardening rather than in a
client's first draft — but knowing it exists is what stops "reconnect immediately" from being written
down as the policy.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic:** the long tool call that outlives an
intermediary's ceiling. A proxy that caps a request at thirty seconds will cut a streaming reply in
half regardless of how much data is flowing, and the client sees a truncated stream with no final
response. Retrying a long call makes it worse. This is the pressure that makes Day 36's task handles
the right shape: return a handle immediately, poll for the result, and never hold a response open for
longer than the infrastructure tolerates.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"this caches the toolset and treats a connection
error as a fatal condition for the whole agent. Every deploy of the ticket server will now look like
an outage of ours. Let the client reconnect — the protocol is stateless, there is nothing to
re-establish — and count reconnects instead of alerting on them."*

**The interview question:** *"your MCP client is idle for an hour and then makes a call. What
happens?"* The answer with the right distinction in it: *"Ideally nothing the caller can see. There
is no protocol connection to lose under the 2026-07-28 revision — no handshake, no session, no
standing stream — so anything that went away is a transport detail: a pooled socket a load balancer
reclaimed, or a child process that exited. The client reconnects and reissues, and in ADK's case the
session manager already retries once against a fresh session. The rule I would state is 'reuse
connections, depend on none of them': the moment a client would behave differently because a socket
was closed, an optimisation has become a dependency, and infrastructure is allowed to take it away.
The place it shows up in practice is that error rates start correlating with the other team's
deployment schedule."*

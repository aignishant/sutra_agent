# 1.2 — Summaries are lossy on purpose

## One-line answer

A summary is smaller than the thing it replaces because it **left things out**, and which things it
left out was decided by a model that did not know what you would ask next.

## The story

You are moving flat and the van is booked.

You do the sensible thing and label the boxes as you pack: *kitchen*, *books*, *bedroom — misc*.
Eleven boxes, eleven labels, and the labels are all true. When you arrive at the new place you know
where everything roughly is, without opening anything.

Then on the second evening you want the phone charger.

It is in one of the boxes. It is in a box that is honestly and accurately labelled. There is nothing
wrong with the label. The label just was not written by someone who knew you would want the charger
on Tuesday, because on Sunday, while packing, nobody knows that.

So you open four boxes.

## The idea in plain language

The label is shorter than the box because it does not say everything in the box. That is not a flaw
in the label — it is the entire reason the label is useful. A label that listed every item would be
an inventory, and an inventory takes as long to read as the box takes to open.

A conversation summary works the same way and pays the same price. It is smaller because it dropped
things, and it dropped them according to one judgement, made once, by a model that was shown the
turns and asked what mattered — before anybody asked the next question.

Two consequences follow, and they are the two halves of using compaction well.

**The loss is real and it is invisible.** After compaction there is no marker in the window saying
"three facts were removed here". The model reads a fluent, confident paragraph and has no way to
know what is missing, so it will answer as though the paragraph were complete. Nothing raises;
nothing logs; the answer is simply built on less than you thought.

**The loss is not random.** Summarizers are good at the *shape* of a conversation — who asked what,
what was concluded, what is outstanding — and reliably weaker on incidental specifics: a version
number, a device, a name, a negation. The word that gets dropped most expensively is usually a small
one, and very often it is *not*. "Do not close the ticket" and "close the ticket" differ by one word
and the summary keeps whichever the summarizer thought was the gist.

That gives the working rule for the whole day, and the rest of section 1 builds it out:
**anything that must survive should not be relying on prose to survive.** Facts belong in state
([Day 17](../../../day-17-state-scopes-and-lifetimes/LESSON.md)), documents belong in artifacts
([Day 18](../../../day-18-artifacts-that-survive/LESSON.md)), and prose carries the story between
them. [1.4](1.4-what-must-never-be-summarized.md) turns that into a checklist.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because the facts a support conversation turns on are exactly the kind that summaries drop.

Sutra's job is triage. A triage thread is full of small load-bearing specifics: the ticket number,
the customer's browser version, the knowledge-base article somebody already ruled out, and the one
instruction that changes the ending — *this account is a managed customer, escalate rather than
close*. None of those is the gist of the conversation. Every one of them changes the answer.

Day 23 will write tests for Sutra's tools, and Day 79 onwards builds the eval suite. Both need to
know which failures are worth testing for. "The agent answered correctly from a complete window" is
easy. "The agent answered plausibly from a window that had quietly lost the escalation flag" is the
failure that reaches a customer, and you cannot write that test until you believe it happens.

## The mechanism

Take a transcript, write an accurate summary of it, and then check — one at a time — which facts
from the transcript are still findable in the summary.

```python
# days/day-20-context-engineering-compaction/lab/whats_lost.py
"""How much smaller the summary is, and how many of the transcript's facts it still carries."""

TRANSCRIPT = [
    "user: Ticket 4521 - customers are being logged out of the web app.",
    "agent: That is usually a cookie problem. Which browser are you seeing it on?",
    "user: Safari 17, and only on an old iPad. Chrome on the laptop is fine.",
    "agent: Noted against the ticket.",
    "user: The refund on that order is still waiting for sign-off from finance.",
    "agent: Understood - I will not close the ticket.",
    "user: I tried clearing the cache and it made no difference.",
]

# A tidy, accurate, human-sounding summary. Nothing in it is false.
SUMMARY = (
    "The user reported ticket 4521: customers are being logged out of the web app. "
    "The problem appears on Safari on an iPad. Cache clearing did not help. "
    "It is being treated as a cookie issue."
)

# Every distinct fact a later turn might need, and the string that proves it survived.
FACTS = {
    "the ticket number": "4521",
    "the symptom": "logged out",
    "the browser": "Safari",
    "the device": "iPad",
    "Chrome is unaffected": "Chrome",
    "cache clearing did not help": "cache",
    "the refund needs finance sign-off": "sign-off",
    "do not close the ticket": "not close",
}

full = " ".join(TRANSCRIPT)
print(f"transcript: {len(full):>4} chars")
print(
    f"summary:    {len(SUMMARY):>4} chars  ({100 - round(100 * len(SUMMARY) / len(full))}% smaller)"
)
print()

kept = 0
for fact, probe in FACTS.items():
    survived = probe.lower() in SUMMARY.lower()
    kept += survived
    print(f"  {'kept   ' if survived else 'LOST   '} {fact}")

print(f"\n{kept} of {len(FACTS)} facts survived the summary")
```

**Line by line:**

- `SUMMARY` is written by hand, not by a model, and that is deliberate. A live summarizer would give
  a different paragraph on every run and the lesson would be buried in noise. This paragraph is the
  kind a good summarizer produces: fluent, accurate, and shorter because it chose.
- **Nothing in `SUMMARY` is false.** That is the part worth sitting with. Every failure in this part
  comes from an entirely truthful sentence.
- `FACTS` maps a human-readable fact to a **probe string** — the shortest text whose presence proves
  the fact survived. Probing for `"4521"` rather than the whole sentence is the difference between
  testing for the fact and testing for the phrasing.
- `probe.lower() in SUMMARY.lower()` is a substring test, and it is deliberately *generous*: it
  counts a fact as kept if the word appears at all, in any sentence, meaning anything. A stricter
  test would report worse results, so the number below is an **upper bound** on what survived.
- `"not close"` as the probe for *do not close the ticket* is the important one. It is looking for
  the negation, because the negation is the whole instruction.
- `kept += survived` adds a `bool` to an `int`, which Python allows because `True` is `1`. It is the
  compact idiom for counting matches and it reads fine here; in a larger function `sum(...)` over a
  generator would be clearer.

**Line by line:**

- No key and no network: the transcript and the summary are both constants in the file.

Measured on 2026-09-03:

```text
transcript:  432 chars
summary:     187 chars  (57% smaller)

  kept    the ticket number
  kept    the symptom
  kept    the browser
  kept    the device
  LOST    Chrome is unaffected
  kept    cache clearing did not help
  LOST    the refund needs finance sign-off
  LOST    do not close the ticket

5 of 8 facts survived the summary
```

Fifty-seven per cent smaller, and it cost three facts out of eight.

Now look at *which* three. The summary kept everything that describes the problem and dropped
everything that constrains the action. "Chrome is fine" is the fact that halves the search space.
"Waiting for finance sign-off" is the reason the ticket is still open. "Do not close the ticket" is
a direct instruction from the user, and after compaction the model has never seen it.

That is not this summary being bad. It is what *summary* means: keep the subject, drop the
qualifiers. A summarizer optimising for a faithful account of what the conversation was **about**
will drop a constraint on what to **do** almost every time, because the constraint is not what the
conversation was about.

## When it breaks

There is no exception and no log line. The observable failure is the model doing something
reasonable that you had explicitly ruled out four turns ago:

```text
reply: I have closed ticket 4521 and sent the customer a confirmation. Let me know if
anything else comes up.
```

Read that with the transcript in hand and it is insubordinate. Read it with the *window* in hand and
it is impeccable: nothing in the window said not to.

This is the same failure shape as yesterday's
[19.5.1](../../../day-19-context-engineering-selection/parts/05-failure-lab/5.1-in-state-and-not-in-the-window.md),
where a state key was set and simply never reached the model. Same lesson from the other end: the
model can only act on what is in the window, and "it was in the conversation" stopped being the same
thing as "it is in the window" the moment you switched compaction on.
[4.2](../04-failure-lab/4.2-the-fact-that-was-true-and-is-now-gone.md) reproduces it end to end with
a real runner and shows the fact leaving the window.

## In production

**Measure retention, not just compression.** The dashboard everybody builds shows tokens saved. The
dashboard that catches this failure shows, for a sample of compacted sessions, how many known facts
from the compacted range are still findable afterwards — the same probe idea as this script, run
over real transcripts. It is cheap, it needs no model, and it turns "summaries lose things" from a
worry into a number you can watch move.

**What changes at scale** is that the summarizer's judgement becomes a component with a version. The
prompt template ADK ships is a real prompt with real choices in it, and
[2.1](../02-the-config/2.1-one-config-on-the-app.md) shows it in full — including that it explicitly
asks for tool names to be preserved, which tells you what its authors had already been burned by. If
you override it, you own its behaviour; if you do not, a library upgrade can change what your
summaries keep.

**The failure that only appears with real traffic** is the long-tail instruction: rare phrasings of
"do not", conditions attached three turns before they matter, and anything a user says once and
expects to hold for the rest of the session. In testing you write short conversations where nothing
gets compacted, so this class of bug has no way to appear until a real session runs long.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"this relies on the model remembering the
escalation flag from turn two. Put it in state and template it into the instruction, so compaction
cannot touch it."*

**The interview question:** *"how do you stop summarization from losing something important?"* The
honest answer, which is the one that lands: *"you do not — you stop depending on it. Anything that
must survive goes in structured state and is re-templated into every prompt. The summary is for
narrative, not for facts you will be judged on."*

# 3.2 — G04: every memo names the rule that kept it

## One-line answer

**G04:** every stored memo carries the retention rule that admitted it and every rule carries a
reason — because a store where things are simply *present* cannot be reviewed, appealed or cleaned
up, and the reason is the field that rots first.

## The story

You borrow a book from the local library. Inside the front cover there is a slip of paper with a
grid of dates on it, and the librarian stamps one: **due 26 September**. Beside the stamp they write
two initials.

Two facts, in two seconds, and they are different facts.

The date says *what the rule is*: three weeks for this kind of book. You can look the rule up on the
laminated card by the door, where it says three weeks for fiction, one week for reference, and
reference does not leave the building.

The initials say *who applied it*. If the date is wrong — if this is a reference book that should
never have gone out at all — there is a person to ask, and they will remember, because it was
them.

Now imagine the slip with only the date. The book is due back on the 26th. Why the 26th? Because it
is. Somebody decided, at some point, under some rule that may or may not still exist, and there is
nobody to ask and nothing to check. The date is still perfectly usable and completely
unaccountable — and the first time somebody wants to change the lending period, they will have no
way to find out which books were stamped under the old one.

## The idea in plain language

Day 48 built the retention policy as **rows of data rather than branches of code**, and gave every
row a required `why`. Its
[part 2.1](../../../day-48-memory-design/parts/02-policy-as-data/2.1-a-rule-someone-else-can-read.md)
gave the argument: the person who carries the risk of a bad retention rule is a lawyer or a support
manager, and they cannot read Python.

Its [part 2.2](../../../day-48-memory-design/parts/02-policy-as-data/2.2-every-verdict-names-its-row.md)
added the other half: a verdict must come back carrying the rule that produced it, because *a
verdict without a citation cannot be checked, appealed or improved*.

G04 is those two claims, checked against the store rather than against the source.

A rule in this policy is six fields, and each answers a question somebody will eventually ask:

| Field | The question it answers |
| --- | --- |
| `kind` | What sort of thing is this? The key the whole policy is looked up by. |
| `keep_for_days` | How long may it live? `None` means indefinitely, which is a decision and not an absence of one. |
| `supersede` | Can a newer memo about the same subject replace it? |
| `authority` | When two memos conflict, which wins? A correction outranks a fact. |
| `redact` | Does personal detail get stripped before it is stored? |
| `why` | **Why this row exists, in a sentence a non-programmer can argue with.** |

The last one has no effect on any code path. It is checked anyway, and it is checked because it is
the field that rots. A `keep_for_days` of 400 that nobody can explain is a number that will be
copied into the next system, and the one after that, long after the annual billing cycle it was
sized against has been replaced by a monthly one.

Two terms, defined once:

- **Authority** is a rank, not a score. It exists so that a `correction` beats an `account_fact`
  even when the fact is newer by the clock. Comparing the date first and the authority second gets
  this backwards, which is Day 48's
  [part 1.4](../../../day-48-memory-design/parts/01-what-a-conversation-leaves/1.4-a-correction-outranks-it.md).
- **A verdict** is what the store decided about one memo on one date, with the reason attached. It
  is not a boolean. `superseded` and `expired` both mean "not returned", and they mean completely
  different things to whoever reads the log next year.

## Why Sutra needs it

Day 69 is *PII and data boundaries*, and it will ask a question this store must already be able to
answer: **for every item you hold, under what rule are you holding it?** That is not a rhetorical
question in a system that keeps customer conversations; it is the first question of any data audit,
and the only defensible answer is a list.

If the rule is not stored beside the memo, that answer has to be reconstructed by re-running today's
policy over yesterday's data — which produces the answer the policy would give *now*, not the one it
gave when the memo was written. Those differ the moment anybody edits a row, and the difference is
invisible.

## The mechanism

The rule travels with the memo. Here is the row, and here is the field on the memo that names it:

```python
@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Rule:
    """One row of the retention policy. Data, not a branch (Day 48, part 2.1)."""

    kind: str
    keep_for_days: int | None
    supersede: bool
    authority: int
    redact: bool
    why: str


RETENTION: tuple[Rule, ...] = (
    Rule(
        kind="resolution",
        keep_for_days=None,
        supersede=True,
        authority=2,
        redact=True,
        why="A resolved cause is the only thing that answers 'have we seen this before?'",
    ),
    Rule(
        kind="account_fact",
        keep_for_days=400,
        supersede=True,
        authority=2,
        redact=True,
        why="Billing arrangements change; 400 days outlives one annual cycle and no more.",
    ),
    Rule(
        kind="correction",
        keep_for_days=None,
        supersede=True,
        authority=3,
        redact=True,
        why="A correction replaces what it corrects, so it must outrank and outlive it.",
    ),
)

RULES: dict[str, Rule] = {rule.kind: rule for rule in RETENTION}
```

**Line by line:**

- `frozen=True` means a rule cannot be edited after construction. A policy that can be mutated at
  runtime is a policy whose stored verdicts may have been produced under a version that no longer
  exists anywhere.
- `RETENTION` is a **tuple**, not a list, for the same reason. It is also the ordered thing a human
  reads; `RULES` is the dictionary the code looks up in, **derived from it** rather than typed
  twice. Two hand-maintained copies of a policy is one copy plus a bug.
- `keep_for_days=None` on `resolution` is written as `None`, not omitted and not `0`. It means
  *indefinitely, deliberately*, and the `why` beside it is what makes that defensible.
- `authority=3` on `correction` against `2` on `account_fact` is the entire mechanism by which
  *"actually, no"* wins. It is a number in a data row, so a support manager can be shown it.
- `why` is a full sentence with a reason in it, not a label. `"standard retention"` would satisfy
  the type and fail the point.

Run the criterion:

```bash
cd days/day-52-memory-in-triage-flow/lab
uv run python ruled.py
```

**Line by line:**

- `ruled.py` runs the write path, then walks every **live** memo and prints the rule that admitted
  it, what that rule keeps, its authority, and its reason.
- It ends by checking the thing no type system can: that no row has an empty `why`.

Measured on 2026-09-05:

```text
policy rows           3
kinds with a rule     account_fact, correction, resolution
memos live            4
refused               1

every live memo, and the rule that admitted it
  memo:7101:resolution:signed-out-of-the-dashboard
      rule 'resolution'  keeps forever  authority 2
      why  A resolved cause is the only thing that answers 'have we seen this before?'
  memo:7104:resolution:csv-export-empty
      rule 'resolution'  keeps forever  authority 2
      why  A resolved cause is the only thing that answers 'have we seen this before?'
  memo:7108:correction:billing-account
      rule 'correction'  keeps forever  authority 3
      why  A correction replaces what it corrects, so it must outrank and outlive it.
  memo:7112:resolution:reset-link-already-used
      rule 'resolution'  keeps forever  authority 2
      why  A resolved cause is the only thing that answers 'have we seen this before?'

  refused case:7115: no retention rule for kind 'hunch'

```

Four memos, four named rules, four reasons, and an empty list of unreasoned rows. G04 holds.

Look at the third memo. It is `memo:7108:correction:billing-account`, admitted under `correction`
with authority 3 — and the memo it beat, `memo:7108:account_fact:billing-account`, came out of the
**same conversation on the same day**. If authority did not exist, the tie would be broken by date,
the two dates are identical, and the winner would be whichever one happened to be later in a list.
The customer said *"actually, no, the Northwind account is closing"*, and a store that resolved that
tie by list order would bill them on a closing account roughly half the time.

Now watch what happens when somebody adds a row to make a refusal go away:

```bash
uv run python ruled.py --unruled
```

**Line by line:**

- `--unruled` adds a `hunch` row to `RULES` at runtime with an empty `why`, then re-runs the policy.
  It is the smallest possible version of the edit somebody makes when a refusal is inconvenient.
- Everything else is unchanged: same candidates, same dates, same judging code.

```text
  memo:7115:hunch:export-slow
      rule 'hunch'  keeps forever  authority 1
      why

rows with no reason   ['hunch']
```

The refusal is gone. The unconfirmed guess — *"Probably the same throttling problem as last time.
Not confirmed."* — is now a stored memo, kept forever, sitting in the same index as three resolved
causes, and it will be returned to a support agent one day with exactly the same authority as the
sentence naming `SameSite and Secure`.

**The only thing standing between the store and that memo is a check on an empty string.** That is
why `why` is checked despite having no runtime effect: it is the field that makes adding a rule
cost thirty seconds of thought instead of none.

## When it breaks

The break that actually happens is not an empty `why`. It is a `why` that is a description rather
than a reason.

*"Kept for 400 days as per policy"* passes every check in this part. It has a value, it is a full
sentence, it will never be flagged. And it says nothing: it does not name what 400 was sized
against, so the next person cannot tell whether it can be shortened, and nobody will ever shorten
it. Day 48's build brief already asks for this and it is worth repeating here — a `why` that
restates the rule is a `why` that has failed while passing.

The second break is a `KeyError`, and it is worth reaching:

```text
Traceback (most recent call last):
  File "<string>", line 6, in <module>
  File ".../day-52-memory-in-triage-flow/lab/_phase7.py", line 213, in what_to_forget
    rule = RULES[memo.rule]
           ~~~~~^^^^^^^^^^^
KeyError: 'hunch'
```

That is `what_to_forget` handed a memo whose `rule` names a row that does not exist. It happens when
a rule is **removed** from the policy while memos admitted under it are still in the store, and it
is the correct behaviour: the alternative is a memo living under no policy at all, which is exactly
the state this criterion exists to make impossible. When you retire a rule, you migrate the memos or
you keep the row.

The third break is silent and is the reason the `why` check is not enough on its own. Change
`authority` on `correction` from `3` to `1` and re-run. Nothing raises. Every memo still names its
rule. Every rule still has a reason. And the store now believes Blue Peak should be billed on a
closing account, because the fact outranks the correction that replaced it. The policy is fully
documented and wrong, and no check in this part can see it — which is what
[4.2](../04-the-read-path/4.2-every-constant-names-its-run.md) is about for the read path, and what
Day 48's own tests are for on the write side.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead.** They version the policy and store the version on the memo,
not just the rule name. `rule="resolution"` tells you which row; `policy_version="2026-09-01"` tells
you which *edition* of that row, and the two together are what lets you answer "under what rule was
this held" for a memo written eighteen months ago under a `keep_for_days` that has since been
halved. It costs one integer per memo and it is the difference between an auditable store and a
plausible one.

**What degrades at scale.** Rules multiply. Three rows is reviewable by reading; thirty rows is not,
and thirty is where a real desk lands within a year as departments ask for their own retention
carve-outs. The signal that the policy has stopped being data and started being a program is the
**refusal rate collapsing towards zero**, because a rule has been added for everything anybody ever
proposed. A policy that refuses nothing is not a policy.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic.** The `kind` field is assigned upstream, by
whatever extracts candidates from a conversation. When that upstream is a model, `kind` drifts: it
starts emitting `resolution` for things that are summaries, because `resolution` is the kind that
gets kept and the model has learned which label survives. Nothing in the policy can detect this —
every memo names a rule, every rule has a reason, and the store fills with mislabelled material.
The only defence is sampling: somebody reads twenty memos a week and checks the label against the
conversation.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Every memo names its rule, good. Now tell me what
happens when we change a rule. Right now a memo says `rule='resolution'` and resolves against
whatever that row says today, so editing one row silently rewrites the justification for everything
ever stored under it. Add a policy version to the memo and make the lookup use it."*

**The interview question:** *"Your agent decides what to remember. How would you defend that to a
data protection review?"* An honest answer: *"Every stored item carries the rule that admitted it,
and every rule is a data row with a required reason field written in plain English — how long, can
it be superseded, does it outrank anything, does it get redacted, and why. A reviewer reads three
rows rather than reading my code. The thing I would flag myself is that the rule is stored by name
and not by version, so editing a row retroactively changes the justification for everything already
held under it. That is the next fix."*

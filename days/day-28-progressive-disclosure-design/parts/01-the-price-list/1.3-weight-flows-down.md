# 1.3 — Weight flows down the ladder — but only as far as the frequency justifies

## One-line answer

Three questions decide which rung a piece of knowledge belongs on — how often is it needed, does it
change what happens at a step, and will it grow — and the answers are read in that order, because the
first one can veto the other two.

## The story

The kitchen has three places to keep things and everybody in the house knows the rules without ever
being told them.

On the counter: the kettle, the salt, the one knife that gets used. In the cupboard above: the plates,
the rice, the things you reach for daily but do not want in front of you. In the loft, in a box: the
big pan that comes out for guests, the second set of crockery, the ice-cream maker somebody was given.

Nobody wrote that down. It is decided entirely by how often a thing is needed, and it self-corrects:
the item that lives in the loft and is wanted every Tuesday migrates down within a month, and the
appliance on the counter that nobody has used since March quietly moves up.

The one thing that never happens is a thing being put in the loft *because it is large*. Size is why
you notice it. Frequency is why you move it.

## The idea in plain language

The general shape of the advice is right and everybody has heard it: bodies get long, so move detail
into `references/`. Day 25 said it
([25.3.1](../../../day-25-skills-the-open-spec/parts/03-the-body-and-the-folders/3.1-the-body-is-loaded-whole.md)),
the specification says it, and it is the first thing anybody suggests in a review.

It is also the advice most likely to be applied to the wrong thing, because size is visible and
frequency is not. So the rule needs an order, and here it is:

**Question 1 — how often is it needed?** Needed on essentially every run of the procedure, it stays in
the body, whatever it weighs. This question can end the discussion on its own, and on Sutra's tier it
usually does: a fetch is a whole model round trip
([1.1](1.1-five-rungs-five-frequencies.md)), so moving something that is needed every time turns one
generation into two, forever.

**Question 2 — does it change what happens at a step?** Something the model must have *in order to
perform* a step belongs beside that step. A rule about how to phrase the search query is useless if it
arrives after the search. Day 27 promoted exactly such a rule from an edge case into step 2
([27.1.4](../../../day-27-authoring-first-skills/parts/01-extraction/1.4-steps-for-a-competent-stranger.md)),
and the same logic forbids moving it down a rung.

**Question 3 — will it grow?** Only now does size enter, and it enters as a **rate** rather than a
level. A table with three rows that will have twelve is a reference; a table with twelve rows that
will always have twelve is a judgement call. The reason growth matters more than size is ownership:
things grow because somebody keeps improving them, and the person improving them should not have to
edit the body.

Read in that order, the three questions produce four verdicts, and it is worth writing them out because
two of them are not "move it":

| Answers | Verdict |
| --- | --- |
| needed every time | **body**, whatever it weighs |
| needed at a step, to perform it | **body**, beside that step |
| needed sometimes, and growing | **`references/`** |
| needed sometimes, small, static | **body** — a fetch costs more than it saves |

The fourth row is the one that gets missed. A 40-token note that applies to one triage in five is not
worth a round trip; it is worth 40 tokens on every activation, which over five activations is 200
tokens against one generation. On Sutra's twenty-a-day budget, that is not close.

And there is one direction the rule does not run. **Weight never flows up.** Moving something from
`references/` into the description to "help it get found" makes the index more expensive for every
conversation forever, and descriptions are for triggers, not for content
([27.1.3](../../../day-27-authoring-first-skills/parts/01-extraction/1.3-the-six-question-pass.md)).

## Why Sutra needs it

Because `ticket-triage` has three sections and every one of them is a different answer to these
questions, and until they are asked explicitly the shape is an accident.

The **steps** are needed every time: question 1 ends it. The **rubric** is needed on most triages, is
small, and is expected to grow — questions 1 and 3 disagree, which is why
[27.1.7](../../../day-27-authoring-first-skills/parts/01-extraction/1.7-what-goes-behind-a-link.md)
had to argue rather than compute, and why the decision is worth revisiting now that the growth has not
happened. The **worked example** is the interesting one, and [1.4](1.4-moving-the-example.md) is about
it.

There is also a rule Sutra needs written down before the shelf grows. Left unwritten, the default
answer to *"the body is getting long"* is always *"move something out"*, and after four rounds of that
the procedure is a stub with six links and every triage costs six round trips.

## The mechanism

The three questions, applied to every section Sutra has, with the verdict and the reason:

```text
ticket-triage
  Steps (7)                    Q1 every time                      -> body
  Worked example               Q1 most runs; Q2 teaches the shape  -> body       (1.4 tests this)
  Edge cases (4)               Q1 every time, as guardrails        -> body
  Severity rubric   142 tok    Q1 most runs; Q3 will grow          -> references (contested)

kb-answer-style
  Steps (5)                    Q1 every time                      -> body
  Worked example               Q2 the shape IS the skill           -> body
  Edge cases (4)               Q1 every time                      -> body
```

**Line by line:**

- Six of the seven sections land in the body, which is the correct outcome for two small, well-scoped
  skills and not a failure to optimise. A shelf where most things are behind links is a shelf that has
  optimised the wrong currency.
- The rubric is marked **contested** rather than settled. Question 1 says body, question 3 says
  reference, and the tie was broken on ownership — support leadership edits it — rather than on cost.
  Writing "contested" down is what makes it revisitable instead of load-bearing folklore.
- `kb-answer-style`'s worked example is marked Q2 with a note that *the shape is the skill*: the entire
  procedure is about what a good reply looks like, so the example is not illustration, it is the
  specification. Moving it out would leave a skill that describes a shape it does not show.
- Nothing in either skill is marked by size. The one number in the table, `142 tok`, is there to show
  that it did **not** decide anything.

And the rule as one paragraph, for `skills/README.md`:

```text
A section stays in the body if it is needed on essentially every run, or if a step cannot be
performed without it. It moves to references/ only if it is needed sometimes AND is expected to
grow. Size alone never moves anything: on our tier a fetch costs a model round trip, so moving a
small, static, occasionally-needed section makes it more expensive, not less.
```

**Line by line:**

- Two sentences for the rule and one for the trap, because the trap is the part people get wrong and it
  is the part that is specific to a rate-limited tier. A team on a paid tier should write a different
  third sentence, and should notice that they are doing so.
- *"needed sometimes **AND** is expected to grow"* — the conjunction is doing the work. Either alone is
  not enough, and the version of this rule with "or" in it produces a shelf of stubs.
- It says nothing about tokens or line counts. A rule with a number in it becomes a rule about the
  number, and the spec's 500-line and 5,000-token guidance is a ceiling nobody in this repository is
  near.

## When it breaks

**The body that became a table of contents.** Four rounds of "move it out" and the body is seven steps,
each one linking to a reference. Every triage now costs several round trips, the model fetches two of
them and improvises the rest, and every individual decision along the way was defensible.

**A step's precondition behind a link.** Move step 2's *"one or two words, the KB matches on keywords"*
rule into a reference and the model reads it, at best, after it has already searched with a sentence.
Question 2 exists for exactly this and it is not a stylistic preference — it is about when in the
sequence the knowledge arrives.

**A reference nobody fetches.** The move looked free because the body got smaller. What actually
happened is that the knowledge left the system: `load_skill_resource` is a fetch by exact path, so a
model that does not follow the link never sees it
([26.2.4](../../../day-26-loading-skills-into-adk/parts/02-the-four-tools/2.4-the-third-rung-and-its-fence.md)).
The body got 200 tokens cheaper and one rule stopped applying.

**Weight flowing up.** *"The description should mention the severity levels so it gets picked more."*
That adds tokens to every listing, dilutes the trigger words that were doing the routing
([27.1.3](../../../day-27-authoring-first-skills/parts/01-extraction/1.3-the-six-question-pass.md)), and
does not help selection, because selection is about matching the request rather than about describing
the content.

## In production

**Record the verdict, not just the layout.** A one-line comment in the body — *"rubric is in references
because support owns it and it will grow"* — is what stops the next person moving it back on the
opposite reasoning. The layout is visible; the reason is not.

**Revisit contested sections on a schedule, with the numbers.** The rubric was moved on an expectation
about growth. If it has not grown in six months, the expectation was wrong and the decision should be
re-argued, not defended. That is what marking it contested buys.

**Make "how often" observable.** The fetch rate for each reference is available: it is a tool call, so
it can be counted. A reference fetched on ninety per cent of activations is body content wearing a
link; one fetched on two per cent is doing its job. Without the number this whole rule runs on
intuition, which is what it was designed to replace.

**What changes at scale** is that question 3 starts to dominate, because at scale things do grow and
they grow unevenly. One reference in twenty becomes a document, and the answer for that one is not
another reference — it is a tool over a real store, or retrieval, which is
[3.2](../03-four-containers/3.2-the-two-boundary-cases.md)'s second boundary case and Phase 7's
subject.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"this moves the edge cases into a reference because
the body was getting long. They apply on every run, so we would now pay a round trip per triage to
learn our own guardrails, and on the runs where the model does not follow the link we would not apply
them at all. Put them back."*

**The interview question:** *"how do you decide what goes in the skill body versus a reference file?"*
The answer that shows you have the order right: *"three questions, and the order matters. How often is
it needed — needed every time, it stays in the body whatever it weighs. Does a step need it in order to
be performed — then it goes beside that step, because arriving late is the same as not arriving. And
only then, is it going to grow — because growth means somebody keeps editing it, and that person should
not have to touch the body. Size on its own never moves anything: on a rate-limited tier a fetch is a
whole model round trip, so moving a small static section makes it more expensive rather than less."*

# 3.2 — The two boundary cases

## One-line answer

Two things look like skills and are not: a procedure that must run on **every** call, which belongs in
the instruction or in a callback because a skill loads on a decision — and knowledge with **no steps at
all**, which is data and belongs behind a tool.

## The story

Two things at a school are not lesson plans, for opposite reasons.

The morning assembly is not in anybody's folder of lesson plans. Nobody decides, on a given Tuesday,
whether assembly is relevant today. It is on the timetable, the bell rings, and it happens whether or
not any teacher has thought about it. Putting it in a folder that somebody has to open first would be a
way of making it optional, and the whole point of it is that it is not.

The register of parents' phone numbers is not a lesson plan either, and for the other reason. It has no
steps in it. Nobody follows it. It is a list of facts that somebody looks one line out of, once, when a
child is ill — and it changes when a family moves house, which is a thing that happens without anybody
touching the school's teaching.

Two documents, both important, both kept somewhere other than the folder of procedures, and if you asked
the head teacher why she would probably say *"well, they're not lessons"* and be unable to say more.

## The idea in plain language

[3.1](3.1-four-containers-one-property.md) gave four containers and one property each. These are the two
cases where the property fights with the shape of the thing, and the property wins both times.

**Boundary one: a procedure that must always run.** Something like *"before sending any reply, check that
every claim in it came from a tool result."* It has steps. It is know-how. It reads exactly like a
skill, and somebody will write it as one.

The word that decides it is **always**, and it decides it because of how a skill is reached. A skill is
loaded when the model calls `load_skill`, which it does because it read the index, decided this one was
relevant, and chose to act on that
([26.2.2](../../../day-26-loading-skills-into-adk/parts/02-the-four-tools/2.2-the-index-is-a-tool-call.md)).
That is a decision, and **"always" must not depend on a decision**. There are three separate places the
decision can go the other way: the model may not list the skills at all, may not activate this one, and
may not follow the line once it is loaded
([27.2.2](../../../day-27-authoring-first-skills/parts/02-what-leaves-the-prompt/2.2-what-never-leaves-code.md)).

So an always-rule goes in the instruction, where it is sent on every request whatever the question is —
or, when a violation would be an incident rather than a disappointment, in a callback, where it is
enforced by code and the model gets no vote
([13.6.2](../../../day-13-callbacks-four-doors/parts/06-in-production/6.2-what-belongs-in-a-callback.md)).
The skill can still describe the rule; what it cannot do is be the only place the rule lives.

**Boundary two: knowledge with no steps at all.** Sutra's knowledge base is the clean example. It is
useful, the procedure needs it, and it is tempting to paste it into `ticket-triage`'s body so that step 3
does not need a search.

It has no steps. It is a set of records, keyed and looked up. That makes it **data**, and data belongs
behind a tool — `search_kb` — or, once the store is too big to scan, behind retrieval, which is Day 49's
subject. Two reasons, and the second is the one people miss.

The obvious reason is budget: a real knowledge base does not fit in a body that is carried on every turn
after activation ([1.1](../01-the-price-list/1.1-five-rungs-five-frequencies.md)). The reason that bites
first is **liveness**. A store changes when somebody edits an article. A skill body changes when somebody
edits the skill. Paste one into the other and you have made a copy whose staleness nothing measures — the
body will keep answering with whatever the article said on the day it was pasted, confidently, forever.

Sutra's own knowledge base has two articles and would fit comfortably. It still does not go in the body,
and that is the point: the container is chosen by the **kind** of thing, not by whether this particular
instance happens to be small today.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because both of these are already sitting in Sutra's repository as live proposals.

The always-rule is real. `sutra/desk/agent.py` carries *"Never invent ticket contents"*, and Day 27
found that Sutra's skill bodies carry `never`, `must` and `always` sentences of their own
([27.2.2](../../../day-27-authoring-first-skills/parts/02-what-leaves-the-prompt/2.2-what-never-leaves-code.md)).
Every one of those is this boundary case, and each needs its tier decided rather than assumed.

The data question is real too, and it arrives disguised as an optimisation. `search_kb` costs a tool
call, so somebody will point out — correctly — that inlining two short articles into the body would save
a round trip on every triage. On the arithmetic alone that argument wins. It is refused on the property,
and this is the part that says so in advance, before the argument is had.

There is a third reason, further out. Phase 9 builds approval gates and Phase 10 builds the safety and
security posture, and both of those are made almost entirely of always-rules. A repository that has not
settled where an always-rule lives will discover in Phase 10 that half of them are in skill bodies,
applying on some runs and not others.

## The mechanism

Both boundaries, measured. Neither number needs a model.

```python
# days/day-28-progressive-disclosure-design/lab/boundary_cases.py
"""The two questions that override the container table: how often, and does it have steps."""

from sutra.loop import KB, search_kb

REQUESTS_PER_DAY = 20  # four conversations of five turns (3.3)
ACTIVATED_TURNS = 8  # of those, the turns that carry an activated body

print("-- boundary 1: a rule that must hold on every run --")
print(f"{'container':<14}{'deliveries/day':>16}{'chances to be skipped':>24}")
print(f"{'instruction':<14}{REQUESTS_PER_DAY:>16}{0:>24}")
print(f"{'skill body':<14}{ACTIVATED_TURNS:>16}{3:>24}")
print(f"{'callback':<14}{0:>16}{0:>24}")

print("\n-- boundary 2: knowledge with no steps --")
print(f"articles behind search_kb : {len(KB)}")
print(f"search_kb('logout')       : {search_kb('logout')[:58]}...")
print("the same text in a body   : whatever it said on the day it was pasted")
```

**Line by line:**

- The first table counts **deliveries**, not tokens, and that is deliberate. A token count would need
  the tokeniser and a key; a delivery count is exact, needs nothing, and is the number the argument
  actually turns on. Tokens follow from deliveries, never the other way round.
- `REQUESTS_PER_DAY = 20` and `ACTIVATED_TURNS = 8` come from the same day-shape
  [3.3](3.3-what-a-misfiling-costs.md) uses — four conversations of five turns, two of them triages,
  activation on turn two — so the two parts are arguing about the same day and the numbers can be
  compared.
- **`chances to be skipped` is the column that decides it.** Three for a skill body, because there are
  three independent points of failure between writing the line and obeying it. Zero for the instruction,
  which is sent regardless. Zero for a callback, which is not sent at all — it is code the model never
  sees and cannot route around.
- The callback row costs **0 deliveries**, and that is the honest surprise: the strictest of the three
  tiers is also the cheapest, because enforcement in code carries no prompt weight at all. What it costs
  instead is a Python function and a test, which is a different budget.
- `from sutra.loop import KB, search_kb` imports the store **and** the accessor, so the second block can
  show the same fact arriving two ways. Importing only the dictionary would have quietly made the point
  that a store is a dictionary, which is exactly the confusion this boundary is about.
- `search_kb('logout')[:58]` truncates because the article is long and the block is about **where the
  answer came from**, not about the answer.
- The last line has no number, on purpose. Staleness is the cost, and staleness has no reading until
  somebody edits the article — which is what makes it the dangerous half of this boundary.
- **Zero model calls**, no key.

**Line by line:**

- Run from the root so `sutra` imports. **Zero generations.**

Measured on 2026-09-04:

```text
-- boundary 1: a rule that must hold on every run --
container       deliveries/day   chances to be skipped
instruction                 20                       0
skill body                   8                       3
callback                     0                       0

-- boundary 2: knowledge with no steps --
articles behind search_kb : 2
search_kb('logout')       : KB-104 'Unexpected logouts': cookies set with SameSite=Str...
the same text in a body   : whatever it said on the day it was pasted
```

Three things in the first table, read in the right order.

**A rule in a skill body is delivered on 8 requests of 20.** Not "sometimes" in a vague sense —
measurably, on fewer than half of them, and on which half depends on what the model decided. A rule
described as *always* that holds on eight requests in twenty is not a weak rule. It is a different rule.

**The instruction costs twelve extra deliveries to remove all three chances of being skipped.** For one
sentence that is nothing. For a fourteen-step procedure it is
[3.3](3.3-what-a-misfiling-costs.md)'s most expensive misfiling, which is why the answer to *"put it in
the instruction, then"* is *"only if it is one or two lines"*.

**The callback costs nothing and is the strictest.** The catch is that it can only enforce what code can
check. *"Do not invent ticket contents"* is not checkable by a callback; *"do not call `search_kb` with a
whole sentence"* is. So the tier is chosen by what is mechanically decidable, and the rules that are not
decidable fall back to the instruction — where they are cheap, constant, and still only a request.

The second block is one line of evidence and one line of prose, and the prose is the finding. `search_kb`
answers from the store as it is now. A copy in a body answers from the store as it was. Nothing in either
case reports which one you are reading.

## When it breaks

**The always-rule that was only sometimes.** The clean symptom is an intermittent one:

```text
run 1  ACTIVATED ['ticket-triage']   -> the reply cites KB-104 with the id
run 2  ACTIVATED []                  -> the reply cites "our knowledge base article"
```

Same question, same agent, two behaviours, and no error anywhere. The rule lived in the body, run 2
never activated the skill, and the only place the difference is visible is the activation register
([26.3.3](../../../day-26-loading-skills-into-adk/parts/03-wiring-the-toolset/3.3-activation-is-a-line-in-state.md)).
A reviewer reading either reply on its own sees nothing wrong.

**The pasted store that went stale.** No error text at all, which is the problem. The article is updated,
the body is not, and the desk keeps answering with the old fix — fluently, with a real article id
attached, which makes it more convincing than a wrong answer has any right to be. The check that would
catch it does not exist, because there is nothing to compare a copy against once it is a copy.

**A callback written for a rule that code cannot decide.** The guard tries to detect an invented ticket
and ends up refusing legitimate calls, which is the failure Day 27 reproduced by deleting a `tool.name`
check and watching every tool in the system get vetoed
([27.2.2](../../../day-27-authoring-first-skills/parts/02-what-leaves-the-prompt/2.2-what-never-leaves-code.md)).
A guard that fires on things it should not is removed within a week, and the rule goes with it.

**The instruction that absorbed every always-rule.** Each one was individually correct and cheap. Twelve
of them later the instruction is longer than the skill it was supposed to keep clean, and it is paid on
every request including the ones that ask what time you close
([6.1.5](../../../day-06-instructions-and-personas/parts/01-writing-the-handbook/1.5-every-line-is-a-tax.md)).
The container was right and the volume was not, which is a real cost and not a reason to move them back.

## In production

**Give every always-rule a tier, in writing, next to the rule.** Three tiers: *instruction* for standing
values, *callback* for anything whose violation is an incident, *skill body* for the explanation of why
the rule exists. The same rule may appear in two tiers — the sign and the door closer — but it must never
appear **only** in the body, and the note saying which tier it is in is what stops the next person moving
it.

**Count the rules in the instruction and put a ceiling on them.** Not a token ceiling: a **count**. The
failure mode is accumulation, one defensible sentence at a time, and a count is the only thing that makes
accumulation visible. When the count is hit, something has to be promoted to a callback or dropped, and
that forces the conversation the instruction was quietly avoiding.

**Never let a body hold a copy of a store.** The rule to write down is about direction, not size: a skill
body may **name** a store and say how to query it, and may never contain rows out of it. That version of
the rule survives the store getting smaller, which the token version does not.

**What changes at scale** is that boundary two stops being about pasting and starts being about
retrieval. Once the store is too big to scan, `search_kb`'s keyword match misses more than it finds — Day
27 already watched it miss on the reporter's own phrasing — and the answer is an embedding index, which
is Day 49. The container does not change: it is still a tool over a store. What changes is what the tool
does inside.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"this skill's body says 'always verify the ticket id
before quoting it'. That is either true on every run or it is not true, and as written it holds only on
the runs where the model loaded this skill. Move it to the instruction. If we would call a violation an
incident, move it to a callback and leave the sentence here as the explanation."*

**The interview question:** *"where do you put a rule the agent must never break?"* The answer that shows
you have thought about enforcement rather than wording: *"not in a skill, because a skill is loaded when
the model decides it is relevant, and 'never' must not depend on a decision. There are three places that
decision can go wrong — it may not list the skills, may not activate that one, and may not follow the
line. So a standing rule goes in the instruction, where it is sent on every request, and if a violation
would be an incident it goes in a callback as well, where code refuses the call and the model gets no
vote. The skill body can still explain why the rule exists; it just cannot be the only place it lives.
On our shelf a rule in a body is delivered on eight requests in twenty — measured, not estimated."*

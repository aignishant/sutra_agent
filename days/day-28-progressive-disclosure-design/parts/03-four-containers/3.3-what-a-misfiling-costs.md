# 3.3 — What a misfiling costs

## One-line answer

Each of the five common misfilings is paid in a **different currency** — two in tokens, one in
generations, and three in wrong answers that no meter reads — so a placement argument settled on token
counts alone has settled the two cheapest cases and ignored the three expensive ones.

## The story

The lift in B wing is being serviced on Saturday, so the secretary writes a notice.

It is a good notice: which lift, which day, which hours it will be off, and who to call. She gives it to
the watchman at the gate and asks him to make sure everybody knows. So he prints a stack of them, and
every single person who walks through the gate for the next six days is handed one. The man who comes
back from work, the children going to school, the courier, the man from the water plant, the same
residents four times a day.

Everybody knows about the lift. The notice was correct, the watchman did exactly what he was asked, and
by Wednesday people are refusing the paper without looking at it.

One sheet taped inside the lift would have reached exactly the people it concerned, exactly when it
concerned them. The content was never the problem. The **delivery frequency** was, and nobody chose it
deliberately — it fell out of who happened to be handed the stack.

## The idea in plain language

[3.1](3.1-four-containers-one-property.md) said the container is decided by a property.
[3.2](3.2-the-two-boundary-cases.md) settled the two hard cases. This part asks the question a reviewer
actually asks, which is *"and what happens if we get it wrong?"*

The answer is that the five common misfilings are not five sizes of the same mistake. They are paid in
three different currencies, and only two of them show up in a token count:

| The misfiling | Paid in | Visible where |
| --- | --- | --- |
| a procedure put in the instruction | tokens, on every request | the pricing script |
| always-needed detail put behind a link | generations, one per run | the request budget |
| an action described in a skill instead of given as a tool | **wrong answers** | nowhere automatic |
| an always-rule put in a skill body | **rules that hold on some runs** | the activation register, if you look |
| a live store pasted into a body | **staleness** | nowhere, ever |

The top two are arithmetic and this part computes them. The bottom three are the reason the arithmetic
is not the whole answer, and they are ordered by how hard they are to notice, worst last.

Two things follow from reading that table as a whole.

**The measurable misfilings are the mild ones.** Putting a procedure in the instruction is wasteful and
recoverable: somebody runs the pricing script, sees the number, moves it, and nothing was ever wrong in
the meantime. Pasting the knowledge base into a body is cheap on every meter you own and produces
confidently wrong answers for as long as nobody checks by hand.

**A currency you do not measure reads as zero.** A team that prices placement decisions in tokens will
systematically prefer the misfilings that cost nothing in tokens, which are exactly the three at the
bottom of the table. That is not a hypothetical bias. It is the predictable result of having one meter.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because the day so far has taught Sutra to argue about placement in tokens, and it needs the sentence
that stops that argument going too far.

[1.1](../01-the-price-list/1.1-five-rungs-five-frequencies.md) priced five rungs.
[1.2](../01-the-price-list/1.2-pricing-your-own-shelf.md) built the scale.
[1.4](../01-the-price-list/1.4-moving-the-example.md) made a change, measured it and reverted it. All
three are correct and all three are about the two currencies a script can read. Applied to a container
question they give the wrong answer, and they give it confidently — which is worse than giving no
answer.

The concrete case is waiting in [3.2](3.2-the-two-boundary-cases.md). Inlining Sutra's two KB articles
into `ticket-triage`'s body would save one generation per triage, which is five per cent of the day's
capacity. On the token-and-request ledger it is a clear win. It is refused anyway, and this part is where
that refusal gets its number: the cost of the copy is staleness, staleness has no reading, and a cost
with no reading is not the same as no cost.

## The mechanism

The two misfilings that can be priced, priced — and the three that cannot, refusing to print a number.

```python
# days/day-28-progressive-disclosure-design/lab/misfile_cost.py
"""What each misfiling costs, in the currency it is actually paid in."""

# Measured on 2026-09-04 by price_the_shelf.py (1.2).
TRIAGE_BODY = 654
RUBRIC = 142

CONVERSATIONS = 4  # a day's work, on twenty generations
TURNS = 5  # turns per conversation
ACTIVATES_ON = 2  # the turn a skill is activated
TRIAGES = 2  # how many of the four conversations are triages

carried = TURNS - ACTIVATES_ON + 1  # turns that carry an activated body
fetched = TURNS - ACTIVATES_ON  # turns that carry a fetched reference

PRICED = [
    ("the procedure as a skill (correct)", TRIAGE_BODY * carried * TRIAGES, 0),
    ("the procedure in the instruction", TRIAGE_BODY * CONVERSATIONS * TURNS, 0),
    ("always-needed detail in the body (correct)", RUBRIC * carried * CONVERSATIONS, 0),
    ("the same detail behind a link", RUBRIC * fetched * CONVERSATIONS, CONVERSATIONS),
]

print(f"{'placement':<46}{'tokens/day':>12}{'extra gens':>12}")
for placement, tokens, generations in PRICED:
    print(f"{placement:<46}{tokens:>12}{generations:>12}")

for misfiling in (
    "an action put in a skill",
    "an always-rule put in a skill",
    "a live store pasted into a body",
):
    print(f"{misfiling:<46}{'not in tokens':>12}{'-':>12}")
```

**Line by line:**

- The four constants at the top are **measurements, not estimates**, and the comment says where they came
  from. A script full of numbers with no provenance is a script nobody can argue with, which sounds like
  a strength and is the opposite.
- `carried = TURNS - ACTIVATES_ON + 1` is four: turns 2, 3, 4 and 5 all carry the activated body. The
  `+ 1` is the activation turn itself, and leaving it out is the most likely arithmetic slip in the whole
  day — it under-reports every body cost by one turn.
- `fetched = TURNS - ACTIVATES_ON` is three, one fewer, because a reference is fetched on the turn
  *after* activation at the earliest. The two constants differ by exactly one and mean different things,
  which is why they are two names rather than one variable reused.
- `TRIAGES = 2` of `CONVERSATIONS = 4`, because a skill is only paid for on the conversations that
  activate it. The instruction line has no such factor — it is multiplied by `CONVERSATIONS * TURNS`,
  every request in the day — and that single difference is the whole finding.
- The last loop prints `not in tokens` where a number would go. **It would have been easy to invent
  numbers for those three rows**, and a plausible number in that column would be read as a measurement
  forever. Printing the words is Principle 10 applied to a report: an honest blank beats a confident
  guess.
- **Zero model calls**, no key. This is arithmetic over numbers already measured.

Run it from the repository root:

```bash

```

**Line by line:**

- No imports beyond the standard library and nothing on disk is read. **Zero generations.**

Measured on 2026-09-04:

```text
placement                                       tokens/day  extra gens
the procedure as a skill (correct)                    5232           0
the procedure in the instruction                     13080           0
always-needed detail in the body (correct)            2272           0
the same detail behind a link                         1704           4
an action put in a skill                      not in tokens           -
an always-rule put in a skill                 not in tokens           -
a live store pasted into a body               not in tokens           -
```

Four numbers, two findings.

**A procedure in the instruction costs 13,080 tokens a day against 5,232.** Two and a half times, and the
multiplier is not a property of the procedure — it is the ratio between *every request* and *the requests
that activate it*. Add a second procedure and the instruction version doubles while the skill version
grows only on the conversations that need it. This is why the container matters more as the shelf grows,
and it is also why the instruction is the wrong home for anything longer than a sentence or two, even
though [3.2](3.2-the-two-boundary-cases.md) sends always-rules there.

**Behind a link is cheaper in tokens and costs four of twenty generations.** 1,704 against 2,272 — a
saving of 568 tokens — for twenty per cent of the day's request budget. Nobody would accept that trade
stated in those words, and it is exactly the trade *"move it to `references/`, the body is getting long"*
proposes. [1.3](../01-the-price-list/1.3-weight-flows-down.md) reached this conclusion by reasoning;
this is the same conclusion with the arithmetic attached.

And then the three rows with no number, which are the ones worth memorising. Here is what each actually
costs, since the meter will not say:

| Misfiling | What it costs | How you find out |
| --- | --- | --- |
| an action put in a skill | invented records, stated as fact | somebody notices a ticket title that does not exist |
| an always-rule put in a skill | the rule holds on 8 requests in 20 | an intermittent difference nobody can reproduce |
| a live store pasted into a body | answers from the store as it was | a customer follows advice that was withdrawn |

Every one of those is discovered by a person, late, from a symptom. That is the definition of an
expensive failure, and none of the three moves a single number on any dashboard Sutra has.

## When it breaks

**The design document that priced only what it could measure.** Three columns of token counts, a
recommendation at the bottom, and no mention of the two placements that produce wrong answers. It is a
good document and it is answering a smaller question than the one it is titled with. The tell is that
every row has a number in it.

**The instruction that grew a procedure.** No error, no failure, and the symptom shows up in the wrong
place:

```text
turn 1  preamble 479 + instruction 811                          = 1290
turn 1  preamble 479 + instruction 157                          = 636
```

The first line is the instruction with the triage procedure folded into it; the second is Sutra's
instruction after Day 27's trim
([27.2.1](../../../day-27-authoring-first-skills/parts/02-what-leaves-the-prompt/2.1-one-fact-one-home.md)).
The difference is paid on the request that asked what time you close, and on every other request that
has nothing to do with triage.

**The revert that only reverted the tokens.** A section is moved out, the numbers improve, and then
somebody notices the answers are worse and moves it back — but the reference file stays, so the same
content is now in two places and one of them will be edited. The preflight catches the orphan half of
this ([27.4.3](../../../day-27-authoring-first-skills/parts/04-the-authoring-loop/4.3-sharpening-without-a-model.md)):

```text
ticket-triage: reference 'worked-example.md' is never linked from the body
```

It does not catch the duplication, because from the outside a reference file and a body section look
like two legitimate documents.

**A number invented for one of the bottom three rows.** *"Staleness costs us about two wrong answers a
week."* Nobody measured that, it will be quoted in the next design review as though somebody had, and it
will be compared against a real token count and lose. An unmeasured cost is better represented by a blank
than by a guess.

## In production

**Give the review a currency column, not just a cost.** The placement section of a design document should
say, for each decision, *what would be paid if this is wrong and how we would find out*. Half the rows
will read *"a person notices"*, and a document where half the rows say that has told the reviewer
something real about the risk profile.

**Instrument the one bottom-row failure that can be instrumented.** The always-rule case leaves a trace:
the activation register says which runs loaded which skill
([26.3.3](../../../day-26-loading-skills-into-adk/parts/03-wiring-the-toolset/3.3-activation-is-a-line-in-state.md)).
Logging the activation rate per skill turns *"the rule holds on some runs"* into a percentage, and a
percentage can be put on a dashboard beside the token counts, where it will actually be looked at. The
other two cannot be instrumented from inside the system, and saying so plainly is more useful than
pretending a check exists.

**Date every placement decision and record the tier it assumed.** All four numbers in the table above
depend on twenty generations a day and on a body being carried every turn. Move to a paid tier and the
generations column stops mattering; move to a provider that charges differently and the whole ordering
changes. A decision recorded without its assumptions becomes folklore the first time the assumptions
change
([24.2.3](../../../day-24-token-accounting-and-budgets/parts/02-two-ceilings/2.3-denominated-in-quota-not-dollars.md)).

**What changes at scale** is that the bottom three rows stop being individually diagnosable. With two
skills, an invented ticket title is traced back to a missing tool in an afternoon. With forty skills and
four authors, the same symptom could come from any of a dozen bodies, and the investigation starts by
grepping every body for sentences that describe an action. The cheap version of that investigation is a
convention enforced at review — a body never describes an action it cannot name a tool for — and the
lint for it is [4.3](../04-the-three-axes/4.3-what-the-gate-cannot-check.md)'s subject.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"the table in this design doc prices four placements
and puts a dash next to the other three. Do not fill the dashes in. Instead say, for each of them, who
finds out and how long after — that is the number that should be driving the decision, and it is the one
we keep leaving out because it is not in tokens."*

**The interview question:** *"how do you justify a design decision about where knowledge lives?"* The
answer that shows you know the limits of your own measurement: *"I price what can be priced and I say
plainly what cannot. Two of the common mistakes are arithmetic — a procedure in the system prompt costs
two and a half times what it costs as a skill on our traffic shape, and moving always-needed detail
behind a link saves a few hundred tokens and costs twenty per cent of the day's request budget. The other
three are not in tokens at all: describing an action instead of providing a tool gets you invented
records, a standing rule in a skill body holds on eight requests in twenty, and a pasted copy of a live
store goes stale silently. Those are the expensive ones, and they cost nothing on the only meter most
teams have — which is exactly why they win the argument unless somebody says so out loud."*

# 4.2 — Refactoring the overloaded skill

## One-line answer

One `support-desk` skill holding a procedure, a second procedure, a standing rule and two tables
produces routing numbers **the gate cannot distinguish** from the properly separated shelf — same worst
margin, same zero ties, same green exit — while costing twice the tokens and applying its standing rules
only on the runs that happened to activate it.

## The story

The van has one phone number painted on the side, and it is the owner's mobile.

It made complete sense when he started. One number, one man, one van. Now there are four vans, and that
number is where the orders come in, where the complaints come in, where the supplier calls about a
delivery, and where his mother calls on a Sunday. He answers everything, and the first ten seconds of
every call is him working out which of the four kinds it is.

Nothing is broken. Every call gets answered, and the number on the van is short and easy to remember and
easy to print. Somebody suggests three numbers — orders, accounts, and his own — and his first reaction
is that it will confuse people, because one number is obviously simpler than three.

He is right that it is simpler. He is wrong that simpler is what he wants: the thing he is paying for is
that every call arrives without a subject, and he pays it in the ten seconds at the start of each one.

## The idea in plain language

The specimen for this part is a skill nobody would defend in the abstract and almost everybody writes in
practice. It is called `support-desk`, and its body has five sections:

- `## Rules` — *"Never invent ticket contents. Always cite the article id in a reply."*
- `## Steps` — the triage procedure, numbered.
- `## Reply style` — the reply-writing procedure, also numbered.
- `## Severity rubric` — a two-column table.
- `## Known articles` — a list of article ids and titles.

Four containers, one file. By [3.1](../03-four-containers/3.1-four-containers-one-property.md)'s
property test: the rules are standing values, the two step lists are two procedures with two triggers
and two owners, the rubric is detail consulted sometimes, and the article list is a copy of a live
store.

Nobody built this on purpose. It is what a skill becomes when each new piece of desk knowledge is added
to the skill that already exists, which is the path of least resistance every single time.

The refactor is not a rewrite. It is four moves, each one obvious once the property is named:

| Section | Property | Goes to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `## Rules` | true on every call | `sutra/desk/agent.py`'s `INSTRUCTION` |
| `## Steps` | a procedure with a trigger | `skills/ticket-triage/` |
| `## Reply style` | a different procedure, different trigger | `skills/kb-answer-style/` |
| `## Severity rubric` | needed sometimes, will grow | `skills/ticket-triage/references/` |
| `## Known articles` | a copy of a live store | `search_kb`, which already exists |

The right-hand column is Sutra's shelf exactly as Day 27 left it. That is not a coincidence and it is
worth saying plainly: **the refactored state is the state you are already in.** This part is running the
history backwards, building the mistake Sutra avoided so its cost can be measured rather than asserted.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because *"we avoided that"* is not a defence anybody can check, and Day 29 is about to hand Sutra skills
written by people who did not avoid it.

A sourced skill pack has one container available to it. Everything the author knew arrives as a skill,
because a skill is the unit the pack format ships — including the parts of it that are standing rules,
the parts that are reference tables and the parts that are copies of somebody else's store. Auditing
that pack means running this refactor on somebody else's file, and it is very much easier to do when you
have run it once on your own.

There is a second reason and it is about the gate. [4.1](4.1-three-axes-one-change.md) claimed that
merging blinds the routing check rather than failing it. That is a strong claim, and the whole design of
Day 31's quality gate turns on whether it is true, so it deserves a printed table rather than an
argument.

## The mechanism

The specimen first, as definitions with no output — the same shape as `route.py`, so anything can import
it without something printing.

```python
# days/day-28-progressive-disclosure-design/lab/overloaded.py
"""One skill holding four containers - the specimen 4.2 refactors. Definitions only."""

from google.adk.skills import models

DESCRIPTION = (
    "Handles support desk work for tickets and customers: triaging a ticket, choosing a "
    "severity or priority, searching the knowledge base, and drafting or rewriting the "
    "customer-facing reply in the house style. Use when a ticket id needs triage, when "
    "someone asks how urgent or serious a ticket is, when a reply has to cite a "
    "knowledge-base article id, or when someone asks how we word an answer."
)

BODY = """# support-desk

## Rules
Never invent ticket contents. Always cite the article id in a reply.

## Steps
1. Look the ticket up with lookup_ticket.
2. Search the knowledge base with one or two words.
3. Choose a severity and name the rule that decided it.

## Reply style
1. Open with the symptom in the reporter's own words.
2. Give the fix in one sentence.
3. Cite the article id.

## Severity rubric
| Severity | When |
| --- | --- |
| high | the reporter cannot work at all |
| normal | there is a workaround |

## Known articles
KB-104 Unexpected logouts
KB-201 Large exports time out
"""


def overloaded() -> models.Skill:
    """The one-skill shelf, built in code so nothing lands in skills/."""
    return models.Skill(
        frontmatter=models.Frontmatter(name="support-desk", description=DESCRIPTION),
        instructions=BODY,
    )
```

**Line by line:**

- `models.Skill` built **in code**, not as a folder, for the reason
  [2.4](../02-descriptions-as-routing/2.4-the-crowded-shelf.md) gave: a bad skill committed to `skills/`
  is a bad skill somebody eventually ships. It is also the legitimate use Day 26 identified for the
  in-code form — showing the difference between two shelves
  ([26.1.3](../../../day-26-loading-skills-into-adk/parts/01-loading-a-folder/1.3-building-a-skill-in-code.md)).
- `DESCRIPTION` is written **generously**, keeping the trigger words from both of Sutra's real
  descriptions. That is the strong version of the opponent. The version a real team writes is shorter and
  much worse, and it is in *When it breaks* below.
- The `BODY` is a **miniature**: three steps rather than seven, two rubric rows rather than the real
  table. It is short because its job is to demonstrate the shape — five sections, four containers — and
  the price argument further down uses Sutra's real measured bodies rather than this specimen.
- The sections deliberately keep their house headings, so
  [4.3](4.3-what-the-gate-cannot-check.md)'s smell check has something honest to read. A specimen written
  as one unbroken wall of text would make that check look better than it is.
- `def overloaded()` returns a **new object each call** rather than a module-level constant, so a caller
  that mutates it cannot affect anybody else. Cheap, and it is what makes the module safe to import from
  two different scripts.
- **No printing at module scope**, exactly like `route.py`
  ([2.3](../02-descriptions-as-routing/2.3-measuring-routing-without-a-model.md)). A definitions module
  that prints turns every importer into a noisy one.

Now the comparison, which is four lines of real work:

```python
# days/day-28-progressive-disclosure-design/lab/refactor.py
"""One overloaded skill against the refactored shelf, on the same requests."""

import pathlib

from google.adk.skills import load_skills_from_dir

from overloaded import overloaded
from route import REQUESTS, report

scored = [r for r in REQUESTS if r != "what time do you close"]
refactored = sorted(load_skills_from_dir(pathlib.Path("skills")), key=lambda s: s.name)

print("--- before: one skill, four containers ---")
print(f"ties: {report([overloaded()], scored)}")

print("\n--- after: the containers separated ---")
print(f"ties: {report(refactored, scored)}")
```

**Line by line:**

- `report` and `REQUESTS` come from `route.py`, unchanged, so the numbers here are directly comparable
  with [2.3](../02-descriptions-as-routing/2.3-measuring-routing-without-a-model.md)'s and
  [2.4](../02-descriptions-as-routing/2.4-the-crowded-shelf.md)'s. A second scorer with its own stopword
  list would produce numbers that look comparable and are not.
- `scored` drops the negative request for the same reason
  [2.4](../02-descriptions-as-routing/2.4-the-crowded-shelf.md) does: a margin is meaningless for
  something that matches nothing, and coverage is a separate finding.
- The "after" shelf is **loaded from disk**, not rebuilt in code. It is Sutra's real shelf, so the second
  table is a measurement of the actual system rather than of a reconstruction of it.
- Both halves call the same `report`, printed in the same format, so the two tables can be read down the
  page as one comparison. That is the whole reason `report` returns a count and prints a table rather
  than returning a structure.
- **Zero model calls**, no key.

Run it from the lab directory:

```bash
cd days/day-28-progressive-disclosure-design/lab
uv run python refactor.py
cd -
```

**Line by line:**

- From the lab, so `overloaded.py` and `route.py` resolve as siblings. **Zero generations.**

Measured on 2026-09-04:

```text
--- before: one skill, four containers ---
how urgent is this ticket                support-desk          2  margin  2
what priority should this ticket get     support-desk          2  margin  2
triage this ticket for me                support-desk          2  margin  2
draft a reply to the customer on this    support-desk          3  margin  3
how do we word an answer that cites a    support-desk          3  margin  3
can you take a look at this ticket       support-desk          1  margin  1
ties: 0

--- after: the containers separated ---
how urgent is this ticket                ticket-triage         2  margin  2
what priority should this ticket get     ticket-triage         2  margin  2
triage this ticket for me                ticket-triage         2  margin  2
draft a reply to the customer on this    kb-answer-style       2  margin  1
how do we word an answer that cites a    kb-answer-style       4  margin  4
can you take a look at this ticket       ticket-triage         2  margin  2
ties: 0
```

**The routing gate cannot tell these two shelves apart.** Six of six requests matched, both times. Zero
ties, both times. Worst margin **1**, both times — before it is *"can you take a look at this ticket"*,
after it is *"draft a reply to the customer on this ticket"*. The gate's headline line and its exit code
are identical for the worst shelf on this page and the one Sutra actually ships.

Row by row it is a wash rather than a win: the overloaded card is three ahead on *"draft a reply"* where
the split shelf manages one, and one behind on each of the last two rows. Nothing in that table would
stop the merge.

The reason is mechanical and it is the point of the whole section: a margin is the distance to the
**second-best** skill, and merging deletes the second-best. The routing axis does not measure how good a
description is; it measures how well the shelf separates. Put one thing on a shelf and it separates
perfectly, by definition.

Now the price axis, which the routing table cannot see. Sutra's measured components
([1.2](../01-the-price-list/1.2-pricing-your-own-shelf.md)) are a 654-token triage body, a 375-token
reply body and a 142-token rubric. A real `support-desk` holds all three, so its body is at least their
sum — and it is activated by **any** ticket question, because there is only one thing to activate:

```text
a five-turn conversation, activation on turn 2, so a body is carried on 4 turns

merged, any desk question   (654 + 375 + 142) x 4                 = 4684 tokens, 0 fetches
split, a triage             654 x 4  +  142 x 3                   = 3042 tokens, 1 fetch
split, a reply question     375 x 4                               = 1500 tokens, 0 fetches

over 3.3's day - four conversations, two triages, two reply questions
merged   4 x 4684                                                 = 18736 tokens,  0 fetches
split    2 x 3042  +  2 x 1500                                    =  9084 tokens,  2 fetches
```

**Line by line:**

- The merged figure is **at least** 1,171 per turn: merging keeps both sets of headings, so the real
  count would be a little higher. It is written as the sum of three measurements rather than as a
  measurement of the merged file, because the merged file does not exist and inventing a token count for
  it would be inventing a measurement.
- The split triage line carries the rubric on three turns rather than four, because a reference is
  fetched on the turn after activation at the earliest
  ([3.3](../03-four-containers/3.3-what-a-misfiling-costs.md)).
- The reply question costs **375 tokens split and 4,684 merged** — more than twelve times — because the
  merged skill has no way to bring only the half you need. That single row is the price of one container
  holding two jobs.
- The day totals are **18,736 against 9,084**: the merged shelf costs about twice as much for the same
  work, and it saves **two fetches of twenty generations**. So even here the two currencies disagree, and
  the honest summary is *twice the tokens for ten per cent of the request budget*.
- The index goes the other way, slightly: one card of 392 characters instead of two cards totalling 622.
  The exact token figure is one run of `price_the_shelf.py` away, and
  [1.2](../01-the-price-list/1.2-pricing-your-own-shelf.md) is the reason not to guess it from the
  character count.

And the placement axis, which neither number touches. Before: one container holding four kinds of thing,
so the standing rules apply only when the skill activated, the article list is a copy of a store that
changes without it, and two procedures with two owners live in one reviewable file. After: four
containers, each holding what its property demands, and the rules are in the instruction where they are
sent on every request whether anything activated or not
([3.2](../03-four-containers/3.2-the-two-boundary-cases.md)).

Three axes: routing indistinguishable, price halved, placement fixed. **Ship it** — and notice that the
only axis with a green light attached to it is the one that had nothing to say.

## When it breaks

**The merge a real team actually writes.** The `DESCRIPTION` above was written generously, keeping every
trigger from both skills. Nobody does that. What gets written is the honest one-sentence summary —
*"Handles support desk work for tickets and customers: triaging a ticket, choosing a severity, searching
the knowledge base and drafting the reply. Use for any ticket or any customer question."* — and it
measures like this:

```text
how urgent is this ticket                support-desk          1  margin  1
what priority should this ticket get     support-desk          1  margin  1
triage this ticket for me                support-desk          1  margin  1
draft a reply to the customer on this    support-desk          3  margin  3
how do we word an answer that cites a    support-desk          0  margin  0
can you take a look at this ticket       support-desk          1  margin  1
ties: 1
```

*"How do we word an answer that cites a KB article"* now scores **zero** and matches nothing at all. The
merged card had room for one job's vocabulary and it kept the wrong one — a coverage failure, on the
axis that is supposed to be the merge's strong suit. The generous description above avoids this by being
392 characters long, which is most of a card's usable width spent on saying that one skill does two
things.

**The refactor that leaves the rules behind.** The four moves are done, the skill is split, and
`## Rules` is copied into the instruction without being deleted from the body. Now the same sentence is
in two documents with two owners, which is exactly the drift Day 27 measured — `priority` once in one
document and `severity` nine times in the other, for the same field
([27.5.2](../../../day-27-authoring-first-skills/parts/05-failure-lab/5.2-two-notices-on-one-door.md)).
Extraction is a move, not a copy, and it is the second half people skip.

**The rubric moved out and never linked.** The body is split into two skills and the rubric goes into
`references/`, and one of the two bodies ends up with no link to it. The preflight says so:

```text
ticket-triage: reference 'severity-rubric.md' is never linked from the body
```

Which is [27.4.3](../../../day-27-authoring-first-skills/parts/04-the-authoring-loop/4.3-sharpening-without-a-model.md)
catching the exact mistake a split produces, and the reason to run it immediately after one rather than
at the end of the day.

**The refactor justified on tokens alone.** *"Halves the token cost."* True, and it is the weakest of the
three reasons. The strong reason is that the standing rules stop being conditional on an activation, and
that reason survives a move to a paid tier where nobody cares about 9,000 tokens a day.

## In production

**Refactor by container, one move per commit.** Five sections, five commits, each one saying which
property moved it. A single commit titled *"split support-desk"* is unreviewable and unbisectable, and
when the answers change next week nobody can say which of the five moves did it.

**Do the deletion in the same commit as the addition.** Every one of these moves is a cut and a paste,
and the cut is the half that gets forgotten. A commit that adds a rule to the instruction and does not
remove it from the body should not pass review, and the diff makes that trivially visible if the two
happen together.

**Expect the routing numbers not to reward you, and say so in advance.** A refactor that improves every
number is a refactor nobody argues with; this one moves one request's margin from three down to one and
leaves the gate's headline unchanged. An author who has not predicted that will be asked to justify it
under pressure. Predicting it turns the same fact into evidence that you understood the change.

**What changes at scale** is that the overloaded skill stops being one file and becomes a habit. Forty
skills, four authors, and three of them are `support-desk`-shaped because that is what the first one
looked like. The refactor then is not five moves, it is a convention plus a migration, and the convention
is the cheap half: one property per container, stated in `skills/README.md`, applied at review to every
new skill rather than to the shelf once a year.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"this splits `support-desk` into two skills, two lines
of instruction and a reference. The `## Rules` section is still in both bodies — delete it there, it is
in the instruction now. And the reply skill has no link to the rubric, which is correct, so remove the
rubric from its `references/` too or the preflight will report an orphan."*

**The interview question:** *"tell me about a refactor your metrics did not reward."* The answer that
shows you review on more than one axis: *"we had one skill holding a triage procedure, a reply-writing
procedure, a set of standing rules, a rubric and a copy of the knowledge base. Splitting it into the four
containers those things belong in left our routing check reporting exactly the same headline — same worst
margin, same zero ties, same green exit — and moved one request's margin from three down to one. That is
not the check failing; a margin is the distance to the second-best skill, and a single skill has no
second-best, so the check had nothing to measure. What the split actually bought was half the token cost,
because a reply question no longer drags a triage procedure into the context, and standing rules that
apply on every run instead of only the runs that happened to load the skill. I put the routing dip in the
pull request description myself, with the reason."*

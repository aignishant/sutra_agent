# 4.1 — Three axes, one change

## One-line answer

Every change to a shelf moves three numbers at once — what it **costs**, how well it **routes**, and
whether each thing is in the **right container** — and the move that improves any one of them in
isolation is usually the move that damages the other two.

## The story

The bedroom is small and there are three things you want from the furniture.

The door has to open all the way, or you catch your knuckles on the wardrobe every morning. The desk
has to get the light from the window, or you work with the lamp on at two in the afternoon. And the bed
must not sit in front of the cupboard, or you shuffle sideways every time you want a shirt.

Any one of those is easy. Push the wardrobe to the far wall and the door swings free — and now it is in
front of the window. Swing the desk round to the light and it blocks the cupboard. Every single move
that fixes one thing perfectly breaks one of the others, and you find this out by doing it, on a
Saturday, with the mattress standing on its end in the hallway.

The arrangement everybody eventually lands on is not the best for the door, or for the light, or for the
cupboard. It is the one that is not bad at any of them, and nobody could have reasoned their way to it
without moving the furniture at least twice.

## The idea in plain language

Sections 1, 2 and 3 each built one instrument and each answered one question. Put them side by side and
they are three axes on the same object:

| Axis | The question | Read from | Sutra today |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **price** | what does this shelf cost to have? | `price_the_shelf.py` ([1.2](../01-the-price-list/1.2-pricing-your-own-shelf.md)) | index 214, bodies 654 and 375, one reference at 142 |
| **routing** | does the right thing win, and by how much? | `routing_gate.py` ([2.3](../02-descriptions-as-routing/2.3-measuring-routing-without-a-model.md)) | six requests matched, worst margin 1, no ties |
| **placement** | is each thing in the container its property demands? | a person reading ([3.1](../03-four-containers/3.1-four-containers-one-property.md)) | six items, four containers |

Two of the three have a command. The third does not, and that asymmetry is the whole subject of
[4.3](4.3-what-the-gate-cannot-check.md).

The reason to hold all three at once is that **a change never moves only one.** Here are the five moves
that actually get proposed, each with what it does to the other two axes:

| The move | Price | Routing | Placement |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| split one skill into two | index up by one card | specificity up, margins may fall | usually right — two jobs, two homes |
| merge two skills into one | index down, body up per activation | **the check goes blind** — a margin needs a second-best | usually wrong — one home, two jobs |
| lengthen a description to win a request | index up, on every listing | that margin up, every other margin down | unchanged |
| move a section into `references/` | body down, reference up, one round trip | unchanged | wrong if it is needed every run |
| move a rule into the instruction | paid on **every** request | index down slightly | right only if it is always true |

Read the second row twice, because it is the trap this section exists for.

**Merging does not fail the routing check. It removes the check's ability to say anything.** A margin is
the distance to the **second-best** skill
([2.1](../02-descriptions-as-routing/2.1-a-description-is-a-routing-row.md)), so merging two skills
deletes one of the two things a margin was measuring the distance between. The gate does not go red. It
reports on a shelf that has stopped having the property it was built to watch.
[4.2](4.2-refactoring-the-overloaded-skill.md) prints both tables: a shelf holding four containers in one
file and Sutra's real shelf give the **same worst margin, the same zero ties and the same green exit
code.** Take the merge all the way to one skill and the gate stops running at all — the traceback is in
*When it breaks* below.

That is what makes three axes necessary rather than merely thorough. A single-axis gate does not just
miss the problem. It hands the change that created it a green tick.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because Day 31 wires checks into `./m check`, and whatever is wired in becomes the definition of a good
shelf.

Two of this day's three instruments are easy to wire: they exit with a code. If those two go in and
nothing else does, then in six months *"the gate is green"* will mean *"cheap and unambiguous"* — and
one overloaded skill is both. The gate would have to be argued with rather than trusted, and gates that
have to be argued with are turned off.

There is a nearer reason too. Day 29 sources third-party skills, and a sourced pack arrives as somebody
else's answer to all three questions at once. It was priced for their tier, described against their
shelf, and packaged as a skill because a skill is what the pack format holds — including the parts of it
that are standing rules or data. Auditing that with one instrument is how a pack gets installed on the
strength of its routing and quietly brings four misfilings with it.

## The mechanism

Both machine-readable axes, run back to back on the shelf as it stands. There is no new script here: the
point is that the two you already have are read **together**, and the third is read by a person.

```bash
cd days/day-28-progressive-disclosure-design/lab
uv run python price_the_shelf.py
uv run python routing_gate.py; echo "exit: $?"
uv run python containers.py
cd -
```

**Line by line:**

- `cd` into the lab first, because `routing_gate.py` and `containers.py` import `route.py` as a sibling
  module and Python resolves that from the script's own directory. Running them from the root by path
  works too; running them from anywhere else does not.
- The three commands are in **price, routing, placement** order deliberately. Price is a fact, routing is
  a measurement, placement is a judgement, and reading the judgement last means reading it with both
  numbers already in your head.
- `echo "exit: $?"` on the middle one only, because it is the only one of the three that is a gate. The
  other two are reports, and a report that exits non-zero teaches people to ignore exit codes.
- `containers.py` prints six items in four containers ([3.1](../03-four-containers/3.1-four-containers-one-property.md)).
  It cannot say whether they are the right four, which is why it is a report and not a gate — a script
  that printed a placement verdict would be inventing a judgement it has no basis for.
- **Zero generations** for the routing and container reports; `price_the_shelf.py` needs
  `GOOGLE_API_KEY` for `count_tokens` and still spends **zero** of the twenty
  ([1.2](../01-the-price-list/1.2-pricing-your-own-shelf.md)).

The three readings, on 2026-09-04, as one line each:

```text
price      index 214 tokens; bodies 654 and 375; one reference at 142
routing    6 of 7 requests matched, worst margin 1, 0 ties, exit 0
placement  2 tools, 2 skills, 1 instruction, 1 reference - four containers, six items
```

That triple is Sutra's baseline, and the reason to write it down as three lines rather than three numbers
is that they are not comparable. There is no combined score, and any attempt to build one has to invent
an exchange rate between a token and a wrong answer
([3.3](../03-four-containers/3.3-what-a-misfiling-costs.md)).

What a change looks like, then, is three lines against three lines — and the honest ones disagree. Take
the two moves from earlier in this day:

```text
1.4  move the worked example to references/
     price     body 654 -> 473, reference 231, net about 31 tokens over five turns
     routing   unchanged - the description did not move
     placement WRONG - Q3 says it will not grow, so it does not move
     verdict   reverted

2.4  add four plausible general-purpose skills
     price     index 214 -> six cards, up by roughly four hundred
     routing   every margin down one, one request flipped, 1 tie
     placement each decoy is a category, not a job
     verdict   rejected upstream
```

**Line by line:**

- [1.4](../01-the-price-list/1.4-moving-the-example.md)'s change is a **price-axis change that was
  decided on the placement axis.** The tokens were almost neutral and the container rule was not, so the
  container rule decided it. A team reading only the first line would have shipped it.
- [2.4](../02-descriptions-as-routing/2.4-the-crowded-shelf.md)'s change is the opposite: all three axes
  agreed, which is why it is the easy case and why it makes such a clean failure lab. Most real changes
  are not like this.
- The `2.4` price line says *roughly four hundred* rather than a measured figure, because the decoys are
  built in code and were never priced with the tokeniser — the experiment was about margins. Saying
  *roughly* is the honest form; putting a precise number there would be inventing one.
- Neither block has a total. **There is no total**, and the format is written this way so that nobody
  tries to compute one.

## When it breaks

**The single-axis gate that crashes on the design it prefers.** Take the merge all the way — one skill on
the shelf — and run the routing gate from
[2.3](../02-descriptions-as-routing/2.3-measuring-routing-without-a-model.md):

```text
Traceback (most recent call last):
  File "days/day-28-progressive-disclosure-design/lab/routing_gate.py", line 15, in <module>
    margins = {r: rank(skills, r)[0][0] - rank(skills, r)[1][0] for r in positive}
                                          ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~^^^
IndexError: list index out of range
```

Reproduced on 2026-09-04. The gate indexes the second-best skill and there is no second-best skill. Note
what the fix is **not**: `report()` in `route.py` already handles this with
`scores[1] if len(scores) > 1 else (0, "-")`, and copying that guard into the gate makes it print a
margin equal to the score and exit green. The crash is the more honest behaviour of the two, because a
one-skill shelf has no routing to measure and a green gate would say it had perfect routing.

**A pull request judged on the axis its author measured.** The description says *"reduces the index by
40 per cent"* and it is true. Nobody asks what happened to the margins, because the number that was
offered was a good one and reviewers respond to the number in front of them.

**Three axes read at three different times.** Price on Monday, routing after the change, placement in the
retrospective. Each reading is correct and the disagreement between them is never seen, because they
never appear on the same page. This is the failure the three-line block above exists to prevent, and it
is a formatting problem as much as an engineering one.

**An invented combined score.** Somebody weights the three and produces a single number so the gate can
be a threshold. It works, briefly, and then a change that trades a wrong answer for eight hundred tokens
passes — because the weighting said it should, and nobody remembers who chose the weights or what units
they were in.

## In production

**Put the three lines in the pull request template.** Not a form to fill in with numbers, but three
labelled lines with the command that produces each and a blank for the judgement. The author who has to
write *"placement: unchanged"* has at least been asked the question, and about one time in five the
answer will turn out to be no.

**Wire the two machine axes into CI and leave the third to review, explicitly.** A gate that silently
covers two of three teaches people that two of three is all there is. A gate whose output ends with
*"placement is not checked here — see the review checklist"* teaches the opposite, costs one line, and is
the difference between a check and a false assurance.

**Record the triple per release, not per change.** A change moves the numbers a little; a quarter moves
them a lot, and only the quarterly series shows the direction. Three lines a release, in a file, is
enough to answer *"when did the index double?"* — and that question always arrives after the fact.

**What changes at scale** is that the axes acquire different owners and start being optimised
separately. Somebody owns cost, somebody owns answer quality, and nobody owns placement because it is not
on a dashboard. The predictable outcome is a shelf that is cheap, routes acceptably and has four things
in the wrong containers — every one of them defended by a real number, and the failure showing up as
invented records and rules that hold on some runs
([3.3](../03-four-containers/3.3-what-a-misfiling-costs.md)). The structural fix is to give the
placement axis an owner before it has a problem, which is much easier than giving it one afterwards.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"this merges `ticket-triage` and `kb-answer-style` and
the routing gate is still green. It would be — a margin is measured against a second-best and you have
deleted the second-best. Post the price line and the placement line as well: I expect the body to roughly
double and to be carried on every conversation that touches either job, and I expect the merged skill to
be holding two procedures with two different owners."*

**The interview question:** *"how do you review a change to a set of agent skills?"* The answer that shows
you have been bitten: *"three readings, on the same page, every time. What it costs — index tokens and
body tokens per activation. How it routes — the margin between the best and second-best skill on a fixed
list of real requests. And whether each thing is in the right container: an action, a procedure, a
standing rule or a reference. The first two are scripts, the third is a person, and the trap is that they
disagree. The clearest example is merging skills: it does not make the routing check fail, it makes it
blind, because a margin is the distance to the second-best skill and merging deletes the second-best. We
measured it — a shelf with four containers crammed into one file gave us the same worst margin, the same
zero ties and the same green exit as the properly separated one. A gate that only measures routing hands
the worst shelf you can build a green tick."*

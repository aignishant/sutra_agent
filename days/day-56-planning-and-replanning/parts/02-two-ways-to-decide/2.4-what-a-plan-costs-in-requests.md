# 2.4 — What a plan costs in requests

## One-line answer

Planning is **one request whatever the request contains** and reacting is one per step, which on the
free tier is **20 investigations a day against 5** — and the advantage disappears entirely at three
plan editions once any step needs judgement of its own.

## The story

You need a particular size of drill bit and there is a hardware shop about twenty minutes away.

You could just go. It is not far, you have the afternoon, and the shop is usually open.

Or you could ring first. It costs you a minute and it is slightly annoying, because you have to find
the number and then wait while someone goes to look. And what it buys is that you do not spend forty
minutes of driving to arrive at a shutter with a handwritten note on it.

The arithmetic is not subtle. One minute against forty. You ring.

But now change one thing: the shop is at the end of your road. Now the phone call costs a minute and
saves you four, and half the time the person who answers is not sure and you end up walking down
anyway. The same phone call, the same shop, and now it is not worth making.

The call is worth making in proportion to what the wasted trip would have cost.

## The idea in plain language

The unit that runs out on this project is **the request** — one call to a model provider. Principle
15 and Addendum 02 put it plainly: quota is the currency, and it is denominated in requests per
minute and requests per day, not in dollars. Day 24 built the accounting for it.

So the cost of a strategy is a count of model calls, and the two strategies count differently:

- **Reacting** asks the model *what next?* before every step. Its cost is **one request per step**,
  and it grows with the work.
- **Planning** asks the model once. Its cost is **one request**, and it does not grow with the work
  at all — a two-part request and a twelve-part request both cost one plan.

That is the whole up-front argument, and on its own it is too flattering to planning. Two things
complicate it and both belong in this part rather than in a footnote:

1. **A replan is another request.** Section
   [5](../05-the-second-edition/5.3-the-brake-and-the-sentence-it-prints.md) produces second and
   third editions of a plan, and each edition is another call.
2. **Not every step is free.** In this lab every step is a function — looking up a ticket costs
   nothing. In a real triage flow *"draft the reply"* is a step and it is a model call. A plan with
   two judgement steps costs the plan plus two.

Put those together and planning's advantage is not a constant. It is a lead that shrinks with every
edition, and the brake in part
[5.3](../05-the-second-edition/5.3-the-brake-and-the-sentence-it-prints.md) is what keeps the lead.

## Why Sutra needs it

The Phase 7 gate that Day 52 just closed was *"seen anything like this before?" answered at $0*.
Phase 8's gate is a working triage graph, and the constraint has not gone away: the whole system
runs on free-tier quota, and a strategy that costs four requests per ticket instead of one is a
system that can handle five tickets a day.

Day 70 builds the Quota-Router, which routes work to whichever provider still has headroom. It can
only route what it can count. This part is where the triage flow's per-ticket request cost becomes a
number the router will later be given.

## The mechanism

```bash
cd days/day-56-planning-and-replanning/lab
uv run python price.py
```

**Line by line:**

- `price.py` re-runs both strategies over the three shapes from part
  [2.3](2.3-the-two-strategies-miss-different-things.md) and reports `trace.requests` for each,
  which is the counter both choosers increment — planning sets it to `1`, reacting adds one per turn.
- `RPD = 20` is the free-tier generate limit per model per day for this project, read off a live 429
  on Day 2 and recorded in `docs/PACKAGES.md`. It is quoted here, not re-measured: nothing in this
  day spends a request.
- The last two columns are `RPD // requests` — how many investigations of that shape fit in a day.

Measured on 2026-09-05:

```text
shape        parts   planning   reactive   runs/day planning   runs/day reactive
one part     2       1          4          20                  5
three parts  4       1          4          20                  5
four parts   6       1          4          20                  5

Free tier is 20 generate requests per day per model, so the last two columns are how
many of that request you could serve in a day before the quota is gone.
```

**Twenty against five.** On a free tier of twenty requests a day, planning lets the desk investigate
twenty tickets and reacting lets it investigate five. That is the difference between a system
somebody can use and a demo.

Two things in this table deserve a second look.

The **planning column is flat at 1** across all three shapes, and that is the strategy's actual
economic claim: the cost does not depend on how much work the request contains. Four parts costs the
same as one.

The **reactive column is flat at 4**, and that is an artefact worth being honest about. It is flat
because the loop's turn budget is four, not because four is what these requests need — part
[2.1](2.1-deciding-with-your-eyes-on-the-last-thing.md) showed it stopping by itself at four steps
on the four-part request. A reactive loop on a request needing eight steps costs eight requests, and
on this free tier that is two and a half investigations a day.

Now the complication, which is the half of this part that changes decisions:

```bash
uv run python price.py --replan
```

**Line by line:**

- `--replan` holds the three-part request fixed and varies two things: how many plan editions were
  needed, and how many of the plan's steps need a model of their own.
- `judgement steps` of `0` is this lab — every step is a function. `2` is realistic for the triage
  flow, where drafting a reply and reviewing it are both model calls.
- The `reactive` column is the same run's cost, for comparison.

Measured on 2026-09-05:

```text
The three-part request, as the number of editions grows.

editions   plan calls   judgement steps   total   reactive
1          1            0                 1       4  cheaper
1          1            2                 3       4  cheaper
2          2            0                 2       4  cheaper
2          2            2                 4       4  same
3          3            0                 3       4  cheaper
3          3            2                 5       4  dearer
4          4            0                 4       4  same
4          4            2                 6       4  dearer

`judgement steps` is the honest column. In this lab every step is a function, so
the executor costs nothing; in a real system a step like *draft the reply* is
another request. Planning wins while the editions stay few - the brake keeps them so.
```

Read down the `dearer` rows. **At three editions with two judgement steps, planning costs more than
reacting** — five requests against four — and at four editions it is six against four.

That is the argument for the brake, arrived at by arithmetic rather than by taste. Section
[5](../05-the-second-edition/5.3-the-brake-and-the-sentence-it-prints.md) caps the replan count at
one, and the reason is right here: the second edition is still comfortably cheaper than reacting, and
the third is where the strategy stops paying for itself. `MAX_REPLANS = 1` is not a round number
somebody liked. It is the last edition on the cheap side of this table.

## When it breaks

The count is not the bill. Requests are the quota unit, and a request is not a fixed size.

A planning request carries the whole request, the tool vocabulary and any retrieved context, in one
prompt. Four reactive requests each carry a conversation that is *growing* — the request plus every
observation so far — so by turn four the fourth prompt is the largest thing either strategy has sent.
Counting calls says 1 against 4; counting tokens says something else, and on a provider that limits
tokens per minute as well as requests per minute the two limits bite at different times.

This day does not measure tokens, and it should say so rather than imply the request count settles
it. Day 24 built the token accounting and Day 51 built the caching arithmetic that makes a large
repeated prefix cheap; the honest position here is that **request count is the right first-order
number on this project's free tier, and it is not the whole bill.**

There is also a way the table lies by omission. `runs/day` divides a daily quota by a per-run cost,
which assumes the runs are the only thing spending it. They are not: the desk also retrieves, and
Day 51 found that response caching was what actually brought the desk's request count down. A number
that ignores everything else in the system is a ceiling, not a budget.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead.** They do not carry a strategy choice in their head; they put
the counter in the code. Every run records the requests it spent, tagged by strategy and by request
shape, and the choice between planning and reacting for a given stage is then a query rather than an
argument. Day 22's structured logging is where that lands, and Day 70's router is what consumes it.

**What degrades at scale.** Requests per minute, not per day, is what breaks first under load. Ten
tickets arriving together with a reactive stage each is forty calls in a burst, and a free tier that
allows a handful per minute will start returning 429 immediately. Planning's one-call profile is not
just cheaper in total — it is *burstier in a way the limiter tolerates*, because one call per ticket
spreads better than four.

**The failure that only appears with real traffic.** The replan rate is the number nobody
instruments, and it is the one that moves. A system measured in testing at 1.05 editions per run and
shipped on that basis can drift to 1.8 in production — because real requests reference tickets that
have been merged, articles that have been renamed, and customers who have two open cases. At 1.8
editions with judgement steps, the table above says planning has quietly become the expensive
option, and nothing will announce it.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"You have `MAX_REPLANS = 1` with no comment saying
where the 1 came from. Put the table in the comment. Otherwise the first person who hits an
escalation raises it to 3 to make the escalation go away, and they will have no way of knowing they
just made planning cost more than the thing it replaced."*

**The interview question:** *"Is plan-and-execute cheaper?"* An honest answer: *"On my measurement,
one request against four, which on a twenty-request daily free tier is twenty investigations a day
against five. But that is only true while the plan works first time. Every replan is another
planning call, and any step that needs its own model call is another one on top — I ran the table,
and at three editions with two judgement steps planning costs five requests against reacting's four.
So the honest answer is that planning is cheaper conditional on a low replan rate, which makes the
replan cap a cost control rather than a safety feature. That is also why I would instrument the
replan rate in production: it is the number that silently turns the cheaper strategy into the dearer
one, and nobody watches it."*

# 1.2 — What a step has to carry

## One-line answer

A step needs exactly three fields — an **action** from a closed set, an **argument** the executor can
act on, and a **why** a human can judge — and three of the six candidate steps in the lab fail on one
of them, each for a different reason.

## The story

You come out of the doctor's room with a slip of paper. It says:

*Amoxicillin 500 mg · one capsule three times a day · five days · for the chest infection*

You take it to the chemist. He reads it, takes a box off the shelf, writes on the box, and hands it
over. He asks you nothing.

Now imagine the slip said *"some antibiotics, take a few until you feel better."*

The chemist cannot fill it. Not because it is rude, or unclear about intent — you both know roughly
what is meant — but because there is no box on his shelf called "some antibiotics", and no number of
capsules that is "a few". He has to send you back.

And there is a third version, which is the interesting one. The slip says *Amoxicillin 500 mg · one
capsule three times a day · five days* and nothing else. The chemist can fill this perfectly. He has
the drug, the dose, the duration. What he cannot do is notice that you were in last month for the
same thing and it did not work — because nothing on the slip says what it is *for*.

Three fields. Two of them are for the person filling the prescription, and one of them is for the
person checking it.

## The idea in plain language

Every step in a plan carries the same three fields, and they exist for three different readers.

- **`action`** — *what kind of thing to do*, chosen from a **closed set**. A closed set means the
  list of allowed actions is fixed and known in advance: in this lab it is
  `lookup_ticket`, `search_kb`, `compare`, `reply`, and nothing else is a legal value. This is the
  field the **executor** dispatches on.
- **`argument`** — *the one thing the action acts on*. A ticket id, an article id, a recipient. This
  is the field the executor passes to the tool.
- **`why`** — *one line saying what this step contributes*. Nothing in the code reads it. It is for
  the **human** who looks at the plan before it runs, and for the person reading the log afterwards
  trying to work out what the agent thought it was doing.

The closed set is the field that does the most work, and it is worth being precise about why.

An open `action` field — any string the model likes — means the executor has to decide at run time
whether it knows how to do this, and the answer arrives too late to be useful: the plan is already
written, possibly already approved, and possibly already three steps in. A closed set moves that
question to **plan time**. A plan containing `investigate` can be rejected in full, before anything
happens, by a function that does not need to be clever.

This is the same argument Day 4 made about tool schemas and Day 12 made about structured output,
pointed at a third thing. A closed vocabulary is not a restriction on what the agent can do; it is
what makes "can it do this?" a question with an answer.

The `why` field is the one people delete first, and it is the one this project keeps. Day 22
established that a log line nobody can act on is not a log line. A step whose reason is missing is a
step nobody can approve, nobody can review after an incident, and nobody can decide is redundant.

## Why Sutra needs it

Part [6.2](../06-in-production/6.2-the-plan-somebody-reads-first.md) builds the card a human is
shown before the plan runs, and Day 63 turns that card into an approval gate. Look at what the card
is made of: the action tells the reviewer what kind of thing will happen, the argument tells them
what it will happen *to*, and the `why` is the only thing on the card that tells them whether it
*should*. Delete `why` and you have built an approval screen that shows a person a list of function
calls and asks them to be responsible for it.

The closed set matters to Day 63 too, in a way that is easy to miss. An approval gate can only be
built for a known set of actions, because "which of these needs a human?" is a table, and a table
needs rows. An open action field means there is no such table.

## The mechanism

`shape.py --validate` runs six candidate steps past the three rules. Three are well formed. Three
are the three ways a step goes wrong, one per field.

```python
def validate(step: Step) -> list[str]:
    """Every rule this step breaks, as sentences. An empty list means the step is executable."""
    problems = []
    if step.action not in ACTIONS:
        problems.append(f"action {step.action!r} is not one of {list(ACTIONS)}")
    if not step.argument.strip():
        problems.append("argument is empty, so the executor has nothing to act on")
    if not step.why.strip():
        problems.append("why is empty, so a human reviewing the plan cannot judge it")
    return problems
```

**Line by line:**

- The function returns a **list of problems**, not a boolean. A step can be wrong in more than one
  way, and a validator that stops at the first one makes the person fixing it do three round trips.
- `step.action not in ACTIONS` is the closed-set check, and `ACTIONS` is a module-level tuple in
  `_world.py` rather than a literal here, so there is exactly one place in the lab that says what
  the vocabulary is.
- `.strip()` on both text fields, so that a step whose `argument` is `" "` fails. Whitespace is the
  classic way an empty field survives a check that only tested for `""`.
- The problem strings say **what it costs**, not what is missing: *"the executor has nothing to act
  on"*, *"a human reviewing the plan cannot judge it"*. That is Day 21's rule about error messages
  applied to a validator — the reader of this message is deciding whether to care.
- An empty list means executable. Returning problems rather than raising is deliberate: the caller
  is validating a whole plan and wants every problem in it, not the first.


**Line by line:**

- `--validate` selects the second of `shape.py`'s four modes; the others belong to parts
  [1.1](1.1-a-plan-is-a-list-not-a-paragraph.md) and
  [1.3](1.3-the-plan-you-can-read-before-it-runs.md).
- The candidate list is a constant at the top of the file. Open it and add a seventh step of your
  own — that is this part's exercise.

Measured on 2026-09-05:

```text
step                               verdict
  lookup_ticket(4521)              executable
  search_kb(KB-104)                executable
  compare(4521,4610)               executable
  investigate(4521)                action 'investigate' is not one of ['lookup_ticket', 'search_kb', 'compare', 'reply']
  lookup_ticket()                  argument is empty, so the executor has nothing to act on
  lookup_ticket(4610)              why is empty, so a human reviewing the plan cannot judge it

  3 of 6 are executable.
```

Look at the last row for a moment, because it is the only one that would pass in most systems.

`lookup_ticket(4610)` has a legal action and a real ticket id. It will run. It will succeed. There
is nothing wrong with it as an instruction to a computer. What it does not have is a reason, and the
consequence of that is invisible until the day somebody has to answer *why did the agent read this
customer's ticket?* — at which point the honest answer is that nobody knows, including the agent.

The fourth row is the one that would fail at run time in a system without a closed set, and it is
worth noticing *when* it fails here. It fails before anything has happened. In an open-vocabulary
system, `investigate` gets discovered by the executor at step four of six, after two tickets have
been read and possibly after a human has approved the plan.

## When it breaks

The way this rule actually breaks is not a step that fails validation. It is a step that passes it
and is still meaningless.

```text
step                               verdict
  compare(4521)                    executable
```

That is a legal action, a non-empty argument, and a filled-in `why`. It passes all three rules. It
is also nonsense: `compare` takes two ticket ids and it has been given one. The validator in this
part checks that the *fields* are present; it does not check that the argument is well formed **for
that action**, because that check has to be written per action and this lab's version is deliberately
the small one.

Run it and the failure surfaces where it always does — later, and further away:

```text
  1/1 compare(4521)         ok   compared 4521
```

`run_step` splits on commas, gets one id, finds it, and reports success. A comparison of one thing
against nothing has just gone into the evidence pile as a completed step. That is part
[4.3](../04-when-a-plan-dies/4.3-the-step-that-worked-and-told-you-nothing.md)'s failure, and the
reason it belongs to that part rather than this one is that no amount of field validation catches
it: the fields are fine.

The production answer is in the next section, and it is not "add more rules to `validate`".

## In production

**What a professional writes instead.** They do not validate the fields after the fact — they make
the invalid step **unconstructible**. `action` becomes an enumerated type; `argument` becomes a
per-action model, so `compare` takes `left: TicketId` and `right: TicketId` as two fields rather than
one comma-joined string, and `reply` takes a recipient and a body. In this project that is Day 12's
structured output doing the work: the model is constrained to emit one of four shapes, and
`compare(4521)` cannot be represented at all. The validator does not go away — it still guards the
edges where a plan is loaded from disk (Day 60) or arrives from another process — but it stops being
the primary defence.

**What degrades at scale.** The closed set grows. Four actions is a table; forty is a menu the
planner cannot choose from reliably, and Day 55 measured the version of this problem that applies to
sub-agents. The move that keeps working is grouping: the planner picks from a small set of
*capabilities* and a second, cheaper step resolves each to a concrete tool.

**The failure that only appears with real traffic.** The `why` field gets filled with the action
restated. You will see `lookup_ticket(4521) # look up ticket 4521` in a real system within a month
of shipping, and it passes every check in this part while carrying no information at all. There is
no validator for this. What works is making `why` load-bearing somewhere a person looks — put it on
the approval card as the *only* explanation, as part
[6.2](../06-in-production/6.2-the-plan-somebody-reads-first.md) does, and restated actions become
visibly useless to the person who has to approve them.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"`argument: str` is doing four different jobs here.
`compare` is parsing it as a list and `reply` is treating it as a recipient. Give each action its own
argument shape — otherwise the first time somebody writes a ticket id with a comma in it, this
becomes a parsing bug in production and you will find it from the customer's side."*

**The interview question:** *"What goes in a plan step?"* An honest answer: *"An action from a closed
set, the argument it needs, and one line of why. The closed set is the part that matters: it moves
'do I know how to do this?' from run time to plan time, so a plan containing an action I cannot
perform is rejected whole, before anything has happened and before a human has approved it. The
`why` is the field that gets cut and shouldn't — it is the only thing on an approval screen that
tells a reviewer whether a step should happen rather than what it does. And the honest limit is that
field validation only checks the fields. I had a step, `compare` with one ticket id, that passed
every rule and ran successfully and compared nothing, and the fix for that is not more validation —
it is a per-action argument type so the step cannot be built."*

# 6.4 — 🅿️ Plan repair, hierarchy and search

## One-line answer

Three techniques this day deliberately does **not** build — plan repair, hierarchical decomposition
and search over plans — each with the number that would tell you it is time, because the honest reason
Sutra has none of them is that on a four-step plan over six tickets they would all cost more than
they return.

## The story

Two weddings.

The first is a hundred people in a hall. There is one sheet of paper: who is bringing what, when the
food arrives, who has the key. One person holds it and everybody rings that person.

The second is a thousand people over three days. There is a plan for the whole event, and beneath it
there is a separate plan for the caterer, a separate one for the cars, a separate one for the
flowers. The person running the whole thing does not know which van is coming at what time; she knows
that the caterer has a plan and that it is somebody's job.

The second arrangement would be ridiculous at the first wedding. You do not appoint a car
coordinator for a hundred people; you write it on the sheet.

And the first arrangement is impossible at the second wedding — not because it is inelegant, but
because one sheet of paper cannot hold three days.

Somewhere between a hundred guests and a thousand, the sheet stops working. Nobody can tell you the
exact number. What everybody can tell you is what it looks like when you have gone past it: the sheet
gets a second page, then a third, and then somebody starts keeping their own list because they cannot
find their bit on yours.

## The idea in plain language

🅿️ **Parked** means: you learn what it is, you can talk about it in an interview, and you deliberately
do not build it. Three techniques sit here.

**Plan repair** is what part
[5.1](../05-the-second-edition/5.1-let-the-seam-out-or-recut-the-cloth.md) did in its crudest form —
patching rather than rewriting. The full version is a body of work with real theory behind it: repair
the plan **minimally**, preserving as much of the original as the failure allows, using the
dependency structure to compute exactly which steps are invalidated. The reason it is interesting is
counter-intuitive and worth knowing: in the general case, repairing a plan is **not** cheaper than
planning again from scratch — the theoretical result is that plan repair is no easier in complexity
terms than planning. What repair buys is **stability**: a repaired plan resembles the one that was
already approved, already logged, already partly executed. That is a human and operational argument,
not a computational one.

**Hierarchical planning** is the second wedding. Rather than one flat list of primitive steps, the
planner produces a small number of high-level tasks — *investigate the duplicate*, *draft the reply* —
and each is decomposed into a plan of its own, recursively, until the steps are primitive. It is
known as HTN planning, hierarchical task network planning, and its practical payoff is that the
top-level plan stays readable at any scale, which is exactly the constraint part
[6.2](6.2-the-plan-somebody-reads-first.md) hit with the approval card.

**Search over plans** is the third. Everything in this day produced **one** plan and then repaired it.
The alternative is to generate several candidate plans, score each one — and part
[1.3](../01-what-a-plan-is/1.3-the-plan-you-can-read-before-it-runs.md) established that scoring is
free — and execute the best. In the language-model world this is what tree-of-thought style methods
do at the level of reasoning steps; at the level of plans it is cheaper, because scoring a plan needs
no model call at all.

## Why Sutra needs it

It needs to know when to reach for them, and today it needs none of them, which is a claim this part
has to support with numbers rather than assert.

Sutra's archive is six tickets and four articles. Its plans are two to five steps. Part
[2.4](../02-two-ways-to-decide/2.4-what-a-plan-costs-in-requests.md) measured that a second planning
request is affordable and a third is not, and part
[5.3](../05-the-second-edition/5.3-the-brake-and-the-sentence-it-prints.md) set the brake at one
edition on that basis.

At that size, each technique is a loss:

| Technique | What it costs here | What it would return |
| --- | --- | --- |
| plan repair | dependency edges the plan does not have, and a repair algorithm | stability on a plan of four steps that nobody approved |
| hierarchy | two planning requests instead of one, per level | readability of a list that fits on one screen |
| search over plans | *k* planning requests instead of one | a better choice among plans that are nearly identical |

Every row's cost is real today and every row's return is hypothetical today. That is what parked
means.

## The mechanism

There is no code in this part, by design: a parked technique that ships code is not parked. What
there is instead is the **trigger number** for each — the measurement that says the trade has flipped
— because a parked decision with no revisit condition is a decision that never gets revisited.

**Plan repair: when the approval is worth more than the plan.** The trigger is not a size, it is the
arrival of Day 63's approval gate. Once a plan has been approved by a person, a rewrite costs a
second approval and a repair may not — part
[6.2](6.2-the-plan-somebody-reads-first.md) argued that a patch which only removes steps is a weaker
change than a rewrite that adds an irreversible one. **Revisit when: approval gates are live and the
re-approval rate exceeds the replan rate.** Both numbers are already being tracked by then.

**Hierarchy: when the card stops being readable.** Part
[6.2](6.2-the-plan-somebody-reads-first.md) is the constraint that bites first — an approval card is
useful in proportion to what the approver takes in, and a twenty-step card is approved by reflex.
**Revisit when: the median plan exceeds about ten steps**, which on the evidence of this day means
requests with more than four or five distinct parts. Today's four-part request produced a four-step
plan, so this is some distance away.

**Search over plans: when the planner is unreliable and scoring is not.** The condition for search to
pay is that candidate plans differ *meaningfully* in score. Today they do not — the deterministic
planner produces one plan, and even a real model on a six-ticket archive has few distinct plans
available. **Revisit when: the coverage score of the first plan is below the best of three
candidates often enough to matter**, which is a measurement you can run offline for the cost of *k*
planning requests on a sample, not in production for every run.

There is one more thing worth parking explicitly, because it is the technique a reader is most likely
to reach for and it is genuinely wrong at this scale: **letting the planner decide its own limits**.
A planner that decides how many editions to attempt, or when a plan is good enough, is the component
being constrained setting its own constraint — the argument from part
[5.3](../05-the-second-edition/5.3-the-brake-and-the-sentence-it-prints.md) and part
[6.3](6.3-the-step-you-cannot-take-back.md). That one is not parked pending a number. It is parked
permanently.

## When it breaks

Parking breaks in two directions and both are ordinary.

**Parked becomes forgotten.** A technique with no trigger number is not parked, it is dropped, and
the difference is invisible for a year. That is why every row above carries a revisit condition
rather than "we can add this later"; the build brief asks for two of them as an ADR precisely so
there is a document with a date on it.

**Parked becomes cargo-culted.** The opposite failure: hierarchical planning is added because a
system that is going to be serious ought to have it, on a plan of four steps. What that produces is
two planning requests per run instead of one — part
[2.4](../02-two-ways-to-decide/2.4-what-a-plan-costs-in-requests.md)'s table says that is the
difference between twenty investigations a day and ten — in exchange for structure nobody needed.

The test that separates them is the same one Day 50 used on rerankers and vector databases: **name
the number that would change your mind, and check whether you have it yet.**

## In production

**What a professional writes instead.** They keep the flat plan and reach for hierarchy exactly when
the top-level plan stops fitting on a screen — and the first thing they do is not add a planner
level, it is check whether the request should have been split into several runs. Most twenty-step
plans are two requests that arrived in one sentence, and splitting them is cheaper than decomposing
them.

**What degrades at scale.** All three techniques become mandatory somewhere and none of them
announces the moment. What survives is the metrics: median plan length, editions per run, re-approval
rate. A team that tracks those three has the trigger conditions above computed for free and can
answer *is it time yet?* from a dashboard rather than from an argument.

**The failure that only appears with real traffic.** Hierarchy hides failures. A top-level plan that
says *investigate the duplicate* is easy to read and completely opaque about what actually happened
underneath, so part [6.1](6.1-every-step-ran-and-nothing-was-answered.md)'s unanswered run becomes
much harder to see: the sub-plan reported success to the parent, and the parent reported success
upward. Every level of hierarchy is a level at which "it worked" can be said without evidence, which
is why the answer condition has to be attached to the **goal**, at the top, and not to the tasks.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"You have three parked techniques and no trigger
conditions. Write the numbers down as an ADR now, while you still remember why you did not build
them, or in eight months somebody will add hierarchical planning to a four-step plan because it is
what serious systems have."*

**The interview question:** *"Why doesn't your planner do X?"* — where X is search, or hierarchy, or
proper plan repair. An honest answer: *"Because at my scale each one costs more than it returns, and
I can say what would change that. My plans are two to five steps over an archive of six tickets, and
I measured that a second planning request is affordable and a third makes planning more expensive
than the reactive loop it replaced. Hierarchy costs a planning request per level and buys readability
of a list that already fits on a screen — I would revisit it when the median plan passes about ten
steps, because that is where my approval card stops being readable. Search over plans costs *k*
planning requests and needs the candidates to actually differ; I would test that offline on a sample
before paying for it in production. Plan repair is the interesting one, because the theory says
repairing a plan is not computationally cheaper than replanning — what it buys is stability, a plan
that still resembles the one a human approved, so its trigger is approval gates going live rather
than any size. The thing I would not park is the temptation to let the planner set its own limits.
That is the component being constrained writing its own constraint, and it does not get better with
scale."*

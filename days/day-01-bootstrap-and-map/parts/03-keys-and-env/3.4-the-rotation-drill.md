# 3.4 — The rotation drill: rehearsing the incident while it is free

## One-line answer

You are going to **revoke a working key on purpose**, watch everything that depends on it fail, and
put a new one in — because the first time you do that must not be the day it matters.

---

## The story

A small team discovers a leaked credential on a Thursday afternoon. Somebody had pasted a config
snippet into a public issue tracker eight days earlier, and it contained a live key.

They know exactly what to do. Everyone in the room can recite it: revoke, rotate, check the logs for
misuse. It is written in their runbook.

Then they start, and the recitation turns out not to be the same thing as the procedure.

Nobody is quite sure which account owns the key — two people have admin access and it was created
before either of them joined. Once they find it, nobody knows what will break when it is revoked;
the key is referenced in "the deployment config", which turns out to be three places, one of which
is a CI secret nobody has looked at in a year. They revoke it anyway, correctly, and two services go
down that they had not predicted. Bringing those back up takes another forty minutes because the new
key has to reach all three places and one of them requires a redeploy.

Total: about two hours, most of it spent discovering the *shape* of their own system under pressure,
with a live exposure running.

The team that has done this before — deliberately, on a quiet Tuesday, with nothing wrong — takes
about four minutes. Not because they are better engineers. Because they already know which account
owns the key, what breaks when it goes, and how many places the new one has to reach. **They learned
that when it was free.**

---

## The idea in plain language

Day 0 attacked the guard rail: four attempts to commit a secret, two of which succeeded, which
taught you where `.gitignore` stops
([4.3](../../../day-00-toolchain-skeleton-driver/parts/04-repo-memory/4.3-the-first-commit-and-breaking-it.md)). That was *prevention*
testing.

This is the other half: **response** testing. Prevention eventually fails — that is what "eventually"
means — and the question then is whether your response is a procedure or a paragraph.

### Why deleting the file is not the fix

Worth stating once more, in the form it takes here. A key is **compromised the moment it exists
somewhere you did not intend**: a public repository, a screenshot, a log aggregator, a chat message,
a pasted snippet. Removing it from that place changes nothing, because you do not control the copies.

Git makes this concrete — a secret in commit history stays in every clone
([2.3](../../../day-00-toolchain-skeleton-driver/parts/02-repo-skeleton/2.3-git-init-and-what-a-repo-is.md)) — but the principle is broader
than git. **The only action that actually reduces risk is making the credential itself worthless**,
and that is revocation.

So the response, in order, and the order is the whole point:

| # | Step | Why here |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Revoke** | The only step that stops the bleeding. Works even if every later step fails. |
| 2 | **Rotate** | Create the replacement and put it where it is needed. |
| 3 | **Verify** | Confirm the new one works and the old one does not. |
| 4 | **Assess** | Check provider logs for use during the exposure window. |
| 5 | **Prevent** | Fix whatever let it out — and only now. |

**Revocation comes first, before you have worked out what will break.** That is
counter-intuitive and it is correct: an outage you caused deliberately is recoverable in minutes; an
active credential in someone else's hands is not bounded at all. Teams that reverse steps 1 and 5 —
"let me just fix the `.gitignore` first" — are optimising the wrong variable under stress.

### What a drill is actually measuring

Not whether you can click "delete key". It is measuring the things you only find out by doing:

- **Which account owns this key?** (The story's first ten minutes.)
- **How many places does the new one have to reach?** For Sutra today: one. Later: `.env`, a CI
  secret, a container's environment.
- **What does the failure look like?** So you recognise it when it is *not* deliberate.
- **How long does the whole thing take?** A number you can put in a runbook.

> 💡 **The mental model:** a fire drill does not test the fire alarm. It tests whether people know
> where the stairs are — and the only way to find out is to make them walk down while nothing is
> burning.

## Why Sutra needs it

- **This repository goes public on Day 93** (plan §14, Phase 14). Not hypothetically. Every key that
  has ever touched this project's history becomes readable by anyone, including automated scanners
  whose entire job is finding credential-shaped strings.
- **Day 92 is the pre-publication security review** (SEC-16). "Has any credential ever been
  committed, and was it rotated?" is a question that day asks, and having a rehearsed answer beats
  having a policy.
- **Day 69 covers PII and data boundaries** (SEC-12, SEC-13), including the free-tier training
  caveat. Rotation is what you do when the boundary has been crossed.
- **Day 72 is backoff with honesty** (SEC-15, OPS-13). A revoked key produces an auth failure, not a
  429 — and **the two need opposite responses**: retry the rate limit, never retry the auth error.
  Today is where you see the difference in a real response body.
- **Principle 13 — blast radius before capability.** You cannot name what a credential can destroy
  until you have watched what stops working when it disappears.
- And the practical one: **today's keys are free.** A leaked free-tier key costs quota and
  embarrassment. Practise the response on the system where being wrong is survivable, so the muscle
  memory exists on the system where it is not.

## The mechanism

Do this for real, with a real key, now. **Groq is the right one to practise on** — Sutra does not
call it until Day 9, so nothing you have built depends on it, and the drill costs you nothing but
the four minutes it takes.

**Step 0 — record the baseline.** Before revoking, prove the key currently works:

```bash
uv run python -c "
from sutra.config import load_env, require, describe
load_env(); print(describe('GROQ_API_KEY'))
" && curl -s -o /dev/null -w "HTTP %{http_code}\n" \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $GROQ_API_KEY" \
  https://api.groq.com/openai/v1/models
```

**Line by line:**

- `describe(...)` prints presence and length, never the value
  ([3.3](3.3-loading-keys-failing-loudly.md)).
- `curl -s -o /dev/null -w "HTTP %{http_code}\n"` — `-o /dev/null` discards the body since you only
  want the status; `-w` writes a formatted line, and `%{http_code}` is the status curl received.
  **This is the smallest possible "does this credential work" probe** and it is worth remembering.
- Expected: `HTTP 200`. Write down the length `describe` reported — you will compare it afterwards.

**Step 1 — revoke.** Go to `console.groq.com` → **API Keys** → delete the key you are holding.

Do it before reading step 2. That is the drill: in a real incident you revoke before you have
finished working out the consequences.

**Step 2 — watch it fail.** Run the identical probe:

```bash
curl -s -w "\nHTTP %{http_code}\n" \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $GROQ_API_KEY" \
  https://api.groq.com/openai/v1/models | tail -5
```

**Line by line:**

- Same command, no `-o /dev/null` this time — **you want the body**, because the shape of an auth
  failure is the thing you are here to learn.
- `| tail -5` — the last few lines, which is where the error object sits.
- Expected: `HTTP 401` and a JSON body saying the key is invalid. **Look at it properly.** This is
  what a revoked credential looks like from the inside, and it is what you will see the day it is
  not deliberate.
- **401 is not 429.** A 401 must never be retried — the key will not become valid by asking again —
  whereas a 429 must be retried with backoff (Day 72). Two failures, two opposite responses, and
  conflating them is how a rate-limit handler quietly hammers a dead credential.

**Step 3 — rotate.** Create a new key in the same console, then replace it in `.env`:

```bash
sed -i.bak "s|^GROQ_API_KEY=.*|GROQ_API_KEY=PASTE_THE_NEW_KEY_HERE|" .env && rm .env.bak
grep -c "^GROQ_API_KEY=" .env
```

**Line by line:**

- `sed -i.bak` — edit in place, keeping a backup at `.env.bak`. On this platform the suffix is
  required for `-i`, and the backup is genuinely useful: a bad substitution in a file of live keys
  is worth being able to undo.
- `s|^GROQ_API_KEY=.*|...|` — substitute the whole line. `|` is the delimiter instead of `/` because
  keys can contain slashes ([3.2](3.2-env-and-the-environment-as-interface.md)); `^` anchors to the
  line start so a mention in a comment is untouched; `.*` consumes the old value.
- `&& rm .env.bak` — **delete the backup immediately.** It contains the old key, and while that key
  is now worthless, leaving files full of credentials lying around is a habit that transfers badly.
  It is also not covered by a `.env` exact-match ignore rule, though Day 0's `.env.*` pattern does
  catch it — check yours.
- `grep -c "^GROQ_API_KEY="` — must print `1`. If it prints `2`, the substitution appended rather
  than replaced and you now have two lines, of which the loader will take the first
  ([3.3](3.3-loading-keys-failing-loudly.md) uses `setdefault`, so the *first* wins).

**Step 4 — verify both directions.** The new key works **and** the old one does not:

```bash
unset GROQ_API_KEY
set -a; . ./.env; set +a
curl -s -o /dev/null -w "new key: HTTP %{http_code}\n" \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $GROQ_API_KEY" https://api.groq.com/openai/v1/models
```

**Line by line:**

- `unset GROQ_API_KEY` — **essential, and the step people miss.** Your shell still holds the old
  value, and `load_env`'s `setdefault` will not overwrite it
  ([3.3](3.3-loading-keys-failing-loudly.md)). Without this you would test the dead key and conclude
  rotation failed.
- `set -a; . ./.env; set +a` — re-source with export enabled
  ([3.2](3.2-env-and-the-environment-as-interface.md)).
- Expected: `HTTP 200`. Combined with step 2's `401`, you have now proved **both halves**: the new
  credential works, and the old one is genuinely dead rather than merely replaced.

**Step 5 — write down the number.** How long did that take, and how many places did the key have to
reach? Today the answer is one place. Note it — because on Day 86 the answer becomes three, and the
runbook you write then should start from a number you measured rather than one you guessed.

## When it breaks

**The revoked key, in full** — the thing you came here to see:

```text
HTTP 401
{
  "error": {
    "message": "Invalid API Key",
    "type": "invalid_request_error",
    "code": "invalid_api_key"
  }
}
```

**How to read it.** `401` is authentication: *I do not accept this credential*. Not `403`
(authenticated but not permitted), not `429` (accepted, but slow down). **The response to a 401 is
never a retry** — the credential will not improve. It is either a rotation you did not finish, a key
from the wrong account, or whitespace ([3.3](3.3-loading-keys-failing-loudly.md)).

**Rotation that half-worked:**

```text
$ grep -c "^GROQ_API_KEY=" .env
2
```

Two lines. `sed` appended instead of replacing — usually because the pattern did not match the
existing line, so the substitution silently did nothing and something else added the new one. The
loader takes the **first** occurrence, so you may be running on either. **Open the file and look.**
This is a case where reading the file beats any command.

**The shell that keeps the dead key:**

```text
$ grep GROQ .env
GROQ_API_KEY=gsk_NEWKEY123...
$ uv run python -c "from sutra.config import load_env,describe; load_env(); print(describe('GROQ_API_KEY'))"
GROQ_API_KEY: set (56 chars)
$ curl ... -> HTTP 401
```

The file is right, the loader reports a key, and the call fails. `setdefault` refused to overwrite
the exported old value ([3.3](3.3-loading-keys-failing-loudly.md)). **This is the precedence rule
working as designed** — it is what stops a stray `.env` overriding a platform's injected secret in a
container — and it surprises you exactly once. `unset`, or a fresh shell.

**The drill that reveals an unknown dependency.** You revoke, and something you had forgotten stops
working — a script, a scheduled job, a second machine. **This is the drill succeeding**, not
failing. You have just discovered a dependency you did not know you had, at a moment when nothing is
wrong. Write it down; it belongs in the runbook.

**The nightmare version, for completeness.** If a key has been committed *and pushed*:

```text
$ git log -p --all -S "gsk_" -- .env | head -20
```

`-S` searches for commits where the count of a string changed — a *pickaxe* search, and the right
tool for "was this ever in here". If it finds anything, the key is in history, in every clone, in
every fork. **Rotate. Do not start with `git filter-repo`** — history rewriting changes every commit
hash after the leak and breaks every existing clone, and it does nothing about the copies that
already left. It is sometimes worth doing afterwards; it is never the first move.

## In production

**Rehearsed response is the difference between a drill and an incident, and it is measurable.** The
teams that recover in minutes are not the ones with the best runbook — they are the ones who have
executed it. This is the same argument as restoring from a backup you have never restored from: an
untested procedure is a *belief* about a procedure. **The professional habit is to schedule the
rehearsal**, on a quiet day, with a real credential, and to time it.

**Rotation should be routine, not exceptional.** The strongest posture is rotating credentials on a
schedule whether or not anything has leaked, because a key that gets rotated monthly has a bounded
exposure window by construction, and — more importantly — **the rotation path is exercised
constantly, so it cannot silently rot.** Teams that only rotate under duress discover their rotation
path is broken at exactly the wrong moment.

**Short-lived credentials are the direction the industry is moving, for exactly this reason.**
Instead of a permanent key, the platform vouches for the workload and issues a token that expires in
an hour. Rotation becomes automatic and continuous, and a leaked token is worthless before anyone
can use it. **The best incident response is the one that runs by itself.** Free tiers use long-lived
keys, so Sutra uses them — but knowing why a paid production system reaches for something else is
part of understanding what you are compensating for.

**Layer prevention with detection, because prevention is not enough on its own.** Day 0's
`.gitignore` stops the accident; a pre-commit secret scanner catches the key hard-coded in a tracked
file; server-side scanning catches what reaches the remote, and several providers participate in
automatic revocation so a pushed key can be dead within minutes. **Notice that the last layer is
somebody else performing step 1 of your runbook for you** — which is excellent, and not something to
rely on.

**What degrades at scale.** One key in one file is a four-minute rotation. Forty credentials across
six services, each in `.env`, a CI secret, and a container environment, is a coordination problem —
which is why organisations centralise on a secret manager with versioned secrets and a rollout
mechanism. **The signal you have outgrown files is the first time a rotation requires a spreadsheet
to track**, and the answer is a system rather than more discipline.

**The failure that only shows with real traffic.** In a drill you revoke, and you are watching. In
production a credential expires or is revoked by somebody else, and the failure surfaces as a burst
of 401s from a service that was healthy a second ago. **The thing that makes this survivable is that
your code distinguishes 401 from 429**: one should alert immediately and stop retrying, the other
should back off and continue. A retry loop that treats them identically turns a credential problem
into a thundering herd against a dead endpoint.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves.** On a pull request adding a broad `except Exception`
around an API call with a retry: *"A 401 and a 429 need opposite handling — retrying auth failures
just hammers a dead credential and delays the alert. Catch them separately: back off on 429, fail
fast and page on 401."* The unstated point: **an error handler that cannot tell the difference
between "slow down" and "you are not welcome" will do the wrong thing in one of the two cases**, and
the cases are not equally rare.

**The interview question.** *"A credential has leaked. Walk me through it."* The order is the answer,
and most candidates get it wrong by starting with the file. **Revoke first** — it is the only step
that stops the exposure, and it works even if everything after it goes badly. Then rotate, then
verify both directions, then check provider logs for use during the window, then fix the cause. The
sentence that marks experience is the one about deletion: *"removing the file doesn't help, because
I don't control the copies — the only thing that helps is making the credential worthless."*

## Check yourself

You have already done the drill. Confirm the end state:

```bash
unset GROQ_API_KEY; set -a; . ./.env; set +a
curl -s -o /dev/null -w "rotated key: HTTP %{http_code}\n" \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $GROQ_API_KEY" https://api.groq.com/openai/v1/models
grep -c "^GROQ_API_KEY=" .env
ls .env.bak 2>/dev/null && echo "!! delete .env.bak - it holds the old key" || echo "clean: no backup left"
```

`HTTP 200`, exactly `1` matching line, and no `.env.bak`. Then write two numbers into your notes:
**how long the rotation took**, and **how many places the key had to reach**. Those are the first two
lines of a runbook you will finish on Day 92.

**Answer out loud, without scrolling up:**

> Why is revocation the first step rather than the last, when you do not yet know what will break?
> And why must a 401 never be retried when a 429 always should be?

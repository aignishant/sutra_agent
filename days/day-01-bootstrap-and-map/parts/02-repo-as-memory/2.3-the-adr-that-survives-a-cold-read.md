# 2.3 — The ADR that survives a cold read

## One-line answer

An **Architecture Decision Record** captures one decision, the situation that forced it, the options
you rejected, and — the part almost everyone omits — **what would make you change your mind**, which
is the only section that turns a record into something you can act on later.

---

## The story

An engineer joins a team and finds something odd in the codebase: every request to one internal
service goes through a queue, even though the service responds in milliseconds and the caller waits
for the result anyway. A queue that nobody consumes asynchronously is a queue doing nothing except
adding a hop.

They ask around. The people who wrote it have moved on. The people who remain say some version of
*"it's always been like that"* and *"I think there was a reason"*.

So the engineer does the obvious thing and removes it. The tests pass. The change ships. It is
faster.

Nine days later there is an incident. The downstream service has a rate limit that nobody documented
anywhere, and the queue had been shaping traffic to stay under it. Under normal load the direct
calls were fine. Under a spike, they were not.

**The queue was correct.** It was correct for a reason that existed only in the memory of somebody
who had left, and once that memory was gone the queue looked exactly like an accident. So it was
removed — reasonably, competently, and expensively.

Here is the part worth sitting with: **the engineer did nothing wrong.** Faced with unexplained
complexity and no record, removing it is the right default; the alternative is a codebase that
accretes forever because nobody dares touch anything. The failure was not the removal. The failure
was that a decision with a real reason left no trace of the reason.

---

## The idea in plain language

**An Architecture Decision Record — an ADR — is a short document capturing one decision.** One
decision per file, numbered, dated, and never edited after it is accepted.

The format is old, small, and stable, and it exists because of the story above. Code shows you
*what* was decided. Comments show *how* something works. Neither reliably survives to tell you
**why**, and "why" is exactly what you need when deciding whether a thing still applies.

An ADR has six sections, and each earns its place:

| Section | The question it answers | What goes wrong without it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Context** | What was true that forced a decision? | The reader cannot tell if the situation still holds. |
| **Options considered** | What else could we have done? | The decision looks arbitrary — or worse, inevitable. |
| **Decision** | What did we choose, and what actually decided it? | You get a conclusion with no load-bearing reason. |
| **Consequences** | What became easier, harder, and committed? | Nobody knows what depends on this. |
| **What would change our minds** | Under what conditions is this wrong? | **The record cannot expire.** It hardens into folklore. |
| **Cold read** | Does it still make sense a day later, to a stranger? | Written-in-the-moment reasoning that reads as nonsense later. |

The fifth row is the one that separates a useful ADR from a tidy one, and it is the one most
templates leave out.

**Compare these two:**

```text
What would change our minds: if it stops working well.
```

```text
What would change our minds: if p99 latency through the queue exceeds 200ms,
  or if the downstream rate limit is raised above 500 rps, the queue is no
  longer earning its hop and should be removed.
```

The first is a sentence that can never fire — "working well" has no threshold, so nobody will ever
notice it has stopped. The second is a **falsifiable condition with a number**, which means a
dashboard can tell you, and which also — read it again — would have told the engineer in the story
exactly what the queue was *for*.

> 💡 **The mental model:** an ADR is a **message to a stranger who is about to delete your work.**
> They are competent, they are acting reasonably, and they cannot ask you. Write for them.

### Why the cold read exists

The last section is unusual and worth explaining. You re-read your own ADR a day later, wearing a
reviewer's hat, and sign it.

The reason is that reasoning written in the middle of a decision is written by someone with all the
context loaded, and it systematically under-explains. A day later, some of that context is gone —
which makes you a much better approximation of the reader the document is actually for. **The cold
read is the cheapest possible substitute for a second person**, and on a solo project it is the only
review the document will get.

## Why Sutra needs it

- **Principle 10 — interview-ready artifacts.** The plan says *"every phase ends with a written
  decision record (ADR) you could defend to a hiring panel."* Sixteen phases, sixteen or more ADRs,
  and **Day 95 is an interview drill over every one of them**. An ADR you cannot defend is one you
  did not really make.
- **You have already inherited three.** `ADR-0001` records that the master plan was lost and
  reconstructed — the scar that produced "the repo is the memory, not the chat". `ADR-0002` adds the
  deployment track. `ADR-0003` is why this document has thirteen siblings instead of being one long
  page. **Reading decisions you did not make is most of what joining a codebase is.**
- **Day 9 benchmarks four free providers** (ADK-08, ADK-09) and produces a table of quality, latency
  and RPD. That table is an ADR's *evidence* — and today's ADR-0005 is the hypothesis it tests.
- **Day 63 designs approval gates** (SEC-05, ADK-47): which actions need a human, and why. That is a
  decision with real consequences and a real "what would change our minds" — it is exactly the
  question of when autonomy has been earned.
- **Day 92 is the pre-publication security review** (SEC-16). A reviewer reading your repo cold, for
  real. Every ADR is a question they do not have to ask you.
- **`docs/adr/` was created on Day 0** and is empty of *your* decisions. Today it stops being.

## The mechanism

You are going to write **ADR-0005**, recording a decision this day actually makes: what each of the
three free providers is *for*.

This is a genuine architectural decision, not an exercise. Sutra will route work across providers
(the Quota-Router on Day 70), and the routing policy needs a stated intent that Day 9's benchmark
can then test. Writing it today, before the benchmark, is what makes Day 9 a *test* rather than a
description.

First read one you inherited, so you have a model in front of you:

```bash
sed -n '1,40p' docs/adr/ADR-0003-depth-contract.md
```

**Line by line:**

- `sed -n '1,40p'` — print lines 1 to 40 only ([2.1](2.1-what-sutra-actually-is.md)).
- Notice its **Context** section leads with a *table of measurements* — 107 documents, ~471 lines
  average, 107 of 107 carrying a time estimate. Numbers beat adjectives: "the documents were too
  long" is an opinion, and a mean line count is a fact somebody can check.
- Notice its **Options considered** includes an option D that was rejected as harmful. **Recording
  the options you rejected is what stops the decision looking inevitable**, and it saves the next
  person from re-proposing them.

Now create yours. The template is at `docs/adr/ADR-TEMPLATE.md` if one exists; otherwise this is the
shape:

```bash
cat > docs/adr/ADR-0005-provider-roles.md <<'EOF'
# ADR-0005 — Each free provider gets one named role

- **Date:** 2026-08-23
- **Day:** 1
- **Phase:** 1
- **Status:** proposed
- **Related:** Addendum 02 §2, §3 · tested by Day 9 (ADK-08, ADK-09)

## Context

Sutra runs on $0 (Principle 15). Three free API keys are available — Gemini AI Studio, Groq,
OpenRouter — plus local Ollama. Verified on the providers' own pages, 2026-08-23:

| Provider | Observed free limits | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gemini | not published in the docs; per-project, read from the AI Studio dashboard | Flash-class stable ids documented; Pro effectively behind billing |
| Groq | 30 RPM; RPD and TPM vary per model; **per organization** | 429 carries `retry-after` + `x-ratelimit-remaining-*` |
| OpenRouter | 20 RPM; 50 RPD free floor, 1000 RPD once ≥$10 credit ever purchased | the `:free` suffix is what makes a model free |
| Ollama | none — local hardware is the limit | no key, offline, smaller models |

Without a stated role per provider, calls land wherever the code happens to point, and quota
exhaustion becomes unpredictable rather than budgeted.

## Options considered

| Option | Pros | Cons |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A. One provider for everything (Gemini) | simplest; native to ADK, no adapter | one 429 stops the whole desk; wastes Groq's separate quota pool |
| B. Round-robin across all three | spreads load evenly | ignores that the providers differ in latency and context size; a reasoning task lands on a fast small model by luck |
| C. **One named role each, routed deliberately** | each provider used for what it is best at; quota pools stay independent | requires a routing layer (Day 70) and a stated policy to route against |

## Decision

**Option C.** Each provider gets one role, pinned in code, never implicit:

- **Gemini Flash-class — the primary brain.** Native to ADK, largest context, the default for
  anything needing judgement.
- **Groq — the speed lane.** Latency-sensitive, high-volume, small-output work: the classifier.
- **OpenRouter `:free` — the breadth lane.** Second opinions and model families the other two do
  not have. Lowest daily quota, so used sparingly.
- **Ollama — the offline baseline.** The provider-outage branch, and the privacy lane.

Every agent pins its model explicitly (ADK-73). No implicit defaults.

## Consequences

Easier: a 429 on one provider does not stop the desk; the Day 70 Quota-Router has a policy to
route against; each lane's budget is separately countable.

Harder: four providers means four sets of limits to track, and LiteLLM as an adapter for three
of them (Day 9).

Committed to: recording the current free model list and date before pinning anything
(Addendum 02 §3), and handling 429 with `retry-after` on every call path (Principle 10).

## What would make us change our minds

- If Day 9's benchmark shows Groq's latency advantage is under ~200ms on classification-sized
  prompts, the speed lane is not worth the adapter and the classifier moves to Gemini.
- If OpenRouter's free floor drops below ~20 RPD, the breadth lane stops being usable for
  anything but one-off comparisons.
- If any pinned model loses its free tier, the plan is amended first (Principle 14).

## Cold read

*(Re-read this a day later, with your reviewer hat on. Sign here.)*
Reviewed on YYYY-MM-DD — still stands / amended because ______
EOF
```

**Line by line:**

- `cat > ... <<'EOF'` — the quoted heredoc from Day 0
  ([2.2](../../../day-00-toolchain-skeleton-driver/parts/02-repo-skeleton/2.2-gitignore-before-secrets-exist.md)); `'EOF'` keeps `$` and
  backticks literal.
- `ADR-0005-provider-roles.md` — zero-padded number, then a **slug describing the decision**, not
  the area. `ADR-0005-providers.md` would tell a future reader nothing about what was decided.
- `**Status:** proposed` — it becomes `accepted` after the cold read. An ADR that is born accepted
  has skipped its only review.
- `**Related:** ... tested by Day 9` — a forward link. **This is what makes the ADR falsifiable
  rather than decorative**: it names the day that will produce evidence.
- The Context table carries **observed limits with a date**, not remembered ones. Note the Gemini
  row says the numbers are *not published* — recording what you could not find is as valuable as
  recording what you found, because the next reader will otherwise spend the same twenty minutes
  looking.
- **Options considered** lists two rejected alternatives with real cons. Option A is the one you
  would drift into by default, which is exactly why it must be written down and refused explicitly.
- The Decision names roles, not preferences, and ends with `No implicit defaults` — which is ADK-73,
  and which exists because ADK 2.2 changed the default model to a preview model with tighter free
  quota. A default you did not choose is a decision somebody else made for you.
- **What would make us change our minds** carries three conditions and two of them have numbers.
  The `~200ms` threshold is a guess, and that is fine — a guessed threshold that Day 9 will replace
  with a measurement is infinitely more useful than "if it seems slow".
- The **Cold read** line is left unsigned deliberately. You sign it tomorrow.

Verify it lands where the tooling expects:

```bash
ls docs/adr/ && grep -c "^## " docs/adr/ADR-0005-provider-roles.md
```

**Line by line:**

- `ls docs/adr/` — four records now: three inherited, one yours.
- `grep -c "^## "` — count second-level headings. **You want 6.** If you get fewer, a section was
  dropped — and the one people drop is almost always *What would make us change our minds*, which is
  the one the story at the top of this page was about.

## When it breaks

**The ADR nobody can act on.** No error text — just a record that fails at the moment it is needed:

```text
## What would make us change our minds

If this approach stops meeting our needs.
```

Read it as the engineer in the story would: it tells them nothing about whether the situation still
holds. **A condition without a threshold cannot fire**, so this ADR will sit unexamined until an
incident examines it for you. The fix is a number and a source: *"if X exceeds N, measured by Y"*.

**The ADR written after the fact.** A record created three weeks later, when the decision has
already been implemented and defended in a meeting, reliably reads as justification rather than
reasoning. The tell is that **Options considered** contains only options that are obviously worse.
Real decisions have at least one rejected option that was genuinely tempting — and if yours does
not, either you did not consider alternatives or you are not recording them honestly.

**The ADR edited in place.** Somebody updates ADR-0005 six months later when the provider roles
change.

Nothing breaks, and the document is now accurate — and the history is gone, exactly as in
[2.2](2.2-the-docs-tree-and-precedence.md)'s plan-editing failure. **The correct move is a new
record**: ADR-0011 supersedes ADR-0005, and ADR-0005's status line becomes
`superseded by ADR-0011`. One line changed, everything preserved. **The chain of superseded ADRs is
the most valuable thing in `docs/adr/`**, because it shows how thinking evolved, which is the thing
a hiring panel actually probes.

**The ADR for something that is not a decision.** *"ADR-0007: we use Python."* If there was no
alternative seriously considered and no consequence worth stating, it is documentation, not a
decision record. The test is the **Options considered** table: if you cannot fill it with two
genuine candidates, this belongs in the README.

**The decision made with no ADR at all** — the story's failure, and the one with no symptom until it
costs you. The practical trigger to remember: **if you would be annoyed to discover this decision
had been reversed without discussion, it needed a record.**

---

## In production

**ADRs are one of the few documentation practices that survive contact with a busy team**, and the
reason is that they are cheap and immutable. A wiki page rots because everyone can edit it and
nobody owns it. An ADR is written once, numbered, and never touched — so there is no maintenance
burden, and staleness is *expected* rather than a failure. A three-year-old ADR marked superseded is
working correctly.

**They live in the repository, not the wiki.** In git, an ADR is versioned alongside the code it
governs, reviewed in the same pull request that implements it, and available offline to whoever
clones. **A decision record that lives in a different system from the code will eventually describe
a different codebase.**

**The real audience is a stranger who is about to delete your work.** Write for the engineer nine
days from an incident, not for your future self who will remember the context. Everything in the
format follows from that: the numbers in Context (so they can check whether the situation still
holds), the rejected options (so they do not re-propose them), and above all the change-our-minds
conditions (so they can check whether the decision has expired without having to reconstruct your
reasoning).

**What degrades at scale.** With four ADRs you read them all. With four hundred, discovery becomes
the problem, and the responses are an index, tags by subsystem, and — most usefully — **linking the
ADR from the code it governs**. A comment reading `# queue shapes traffic to stay under the
downstream rate limit — see ADR-0031` next to the queue would have ended the story on line one, and
it costs one line.

**The failure that only appears with real time.** ADRs do not fail in week one; everybody remembers
everything in week one. They fail at month eighteen, when the people who wrote them have moved and
the conditions they assumed have quietly changed. **This is why the change-our-minds section is not
optional** — it is the only part of the document that has an expiry date built in, and without it an
ADR degrades from a decision into a tradition.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves.** On a pull request introducing a non-obvious
architectural choice with no record: *"This is a real decision and it's going to look arbitrary in
six months. Add an ADR — I mainly want the 'what would change our minds' section, so that whoever
finds this next knows whether it still applies."* Note what the reviewer asks for: not
documentation, and not justification. **The expiry condition**, because that is the section that
does work later.

**The interview question.** *"Tell me about a technical decision you made and how you'd know if it
was wrong."* The second half is the real question and most candidates have no answer to it — they
can defend the choice but not falsify it. A strong answer names the trade honestly, names the option
that was genuinely tempting and why it was rejected, and then gives a **specific, measurable
condition** under which the decision should be revisited. That last part is what distinguishes
someone who makes decisions from someone who has opinions.

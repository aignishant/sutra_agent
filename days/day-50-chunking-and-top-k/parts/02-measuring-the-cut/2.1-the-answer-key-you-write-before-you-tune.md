# 2.1 — The answer key you write before you tune

## One-line answer

You cannot tune a retriever without an answer key, and the key needs **three** columns — the question,
the document that answers it, and the words that *are* the answer — because the difference between the
second and the third column is where every chunking bug lives.

## The story

You have a spare key cut for the flat, because your sister is arriving on Thursday and you will be at
work. You drop it through the letterbox of the neighbour who has agreed to let her in.

Thursday. Your sister rings from the doorstep. The key goes into the lock. It turns halfway and stops.

You had a key. You did not have a key that opened the door, and the difference between those two things
takes one turn of your wrist to discover. On Tuesday that turn would have cost nothing. On Thursday it
costs somebody standing outside in the rain while you are an hour away.

The test is trivial. What matters is that it happens *before* you need the thing to work, and against
the actual lock rather than against your memory of it.

## The idea in plain language

A **gold set** is a small list of questions where somebody has written down, by hand and in advance,
what the right answer is. It is the answer key. Everything else in this day — every table, every
recommendation, the chunk size, the k, the floor — is a comparison against it, and without it you have
no way to say that one setting is better than another, only that it is different.

The word "gold" is the usual term for it and it means exactly what it says: this is the standard, a
person wrote it, and when the retriever disagrees with it the retriever is wrong.

Most tutorials give the gold set two columns: the question, and the document that should come back.
That is enough to compute **hit@k** — *"is the right document in the top k?"* — and hit@k is the metric
almost everybody reports.

Sutra's gold set has three columns, because
[1.3](../01-the-unit-you-index/1.3-cut-too-small-to-mean-anything.md) demonstrated that two are not
enough. The third column holds the **words that are the answer**: a short phrase that must be present
in the retrieved text for the retrieval to have been useful. That gives a second metric,
**answered@k** — *"did the text I actually sent contain the answer?"* — and the whole of section 1
consisted of settings where those two numbers disagreed.

Two more properties the questions themselves need, both easy to get wrong:

- **A gold question must be a paraphrase.** If the question repeats the document's distinctive words,
  you are measuring whether string matching works, and string matching always works. The question has
  to be phrased the way a person who has not read the document would phrase it.
- **A gold set must contain questions with no answer.** If every question in the key is answerable, no
  measurement in your entire test suite can ever tell you that the retriever invents things, and
  inventing things is what retrievers do ([4.1](../04-the-floor/4.1-cosine-never-says-it-does-not-know.md)).
  Sutra's key carries four of them, in a separate list, and their correct result is nothing.

## Why Sutra needs it

Every number in this day comes off this file. If the key is wrong, the chunk size is wrong, the k is
wrong and the floor is wrong, and all of them are wrong with the full confidence of a printed table.

It also has a use beyond today. Principle 11 says evals are tests, and a gold set is the smallest
honest eval a retrieval system can have. Once it exists, re-indexing is no longer an act of faith: you
change the splitter, you re-run the sweep, and you find out. Day 49's index went in with a chunk size
nobody had measured because there was nothing to measure it against — that is not a criticism of Day
49, it is what the first version of anything looks like.

Phase 12 industrialises this. Today builds the version that fits in one file and runs on no model at
all.

## The mechanism

The key is data, not code, and it lives beside the archive it grades:

```python
# days/day-50-chunking-and-top-k/lab/_archive.py
GOLD: list[tuple[str, str, str]] = [
    (
        "customer bounced back to the sign-in page during single sign-on",
        "ticket:4610",
        "SameSite and Secure",
    ),
    (
        "rollback did not change the failure rate",
        "kb:KB-900",
        "rollback was attempted and did not change",
    ),
    # ... eight more
]

NOTHING: list[str] = [
    "how do I claim travel expenses for a client visit",
    "the office printer jams on thick paper",
    "what is the notice period for resigning",
    "which meeting room has the projector",
]
```

**Line by line:**

- `GOLD` is a list of three-tuples and not a dictionary, because two of its ten questions have the same
  target document — `kb:KB-900` is asked about twice, from two different paragraphs — and a dictionary
  keyed on the document would silently drop one of them. A container that cannot hold your data is a
  bug you find later.
- The first column is the **question**, written the way a support agent types it. *"customer bounced
  back to the sign-in page"* — the ticket it targets never uses the word "bounced".
- The second column is the **ref of the document** that answers it, in Day 49's `kind:id` form. Note
  that it is the *document*, never a chunk ref: the key must survive re-chunking, and `ticket:4610#2`
  means a different thing at every chunk size
  ([1.1](../01-the-unit-you-index/1.1-the-unit-you-store-is-the-unit-you-find.md)).
- The third column is the **answer phrase**: the shortest run of text that, if present in what you
  retrieved, means a model could have answered. `"SameSite and Secure"` is three words out of a
  nine-hundred-character ticket, and the entire point of the ticket.
- `NOTHING` is a separate list with no second or third column, because there is nothing to put in them.
  Keeping it separate rather than using a sentinel value in `GOLD` means the two are counted by
  different code and can never be accidentally averaged together.

The two metrics are four lines each:

```python
# days/day-50-chunking-and-top-k/lab/_index.py
def hit_at_k(index: Index, gold: list[tuple[str, str, str]], k: int) -> int:
    """How many gold queries find their target document inside the top `k`."""
    return sum(
        any(parent(ref) == want for _, ref in search(index, query, k)) for query, want, _ in gold
    )


def answered_at_k(index: Index, gold: list[tuple[str, str, str]], k: int) -> int:
    """How many gold queries get back text that actually contains the answer."""
    return sum(
        any(answer in index.texts[ref] for _, ref in search(index, query, k))
        for query, _, answer in gold
    )
```

**Line by line:**

- `hit_at_k` compares `parent(ref)` against the wanted document, so a chunk counts for the document it
  came from. Without that call, chunking would score zero on every question by definition, because the
  key says `ticket:4610` and the index returns `ticket:4610#2`.
- `answered_at_k` does **not** call `parent`. It reaches into `index.texts[ref]` — the actual text of
  the row that was returned — and asks whether the answer phrase is inside it. That is the whole
  difference between the two functions and the whole reason both exist.
- `any(...)` in both: the answer only has to be in *one* of the k rows. It does not need to be first,
  and this measurement deliberately does not care about rank. Rank is
  [3.3](../03-the-k-is-a-budget/3.3-twenty-rows-and-nothing-new-in-nineteen.md)'s subject.
- Both return a plain `int` rather than a fraction. Ten questions is ten questions; printing `0.8`
  invites the reader to forget that the denominator is small enough to matter.

Look at the key itself before trusting anything it says:

```bash
cd days/day-50-chunking-and-top-k/lab
uv run python gold.py
uv run python gold.py --leak
```

**Line by line:**

- `gold.py` prints each question with the number of words it shares with its target document. That
  count is the honesty check on the key: a question sharing every distinctive word with its answer is
  not testing retrieval.
- `--leak` additionally prints the third column, the answer phrases. It is behind a flag because
  reading the answers before you write a question is how a gold set stops being independent.

Measured on 2026-09-05:

```text
10 answerable questions, 4 unanswerable ones.

  shared words   target        question
            10   ticket:4610   customer bounced back to the sign-in page during single sign-on
             6   ticket:4633   the reset link is already used up before the person clicks it
             4   ticket:4701   spreadsheet import breaks because the header keeps moving
             4   kb:KB-104     why would a browser throw away a cookie after a redirect
             7   kb:KB-900     rollback did not change the failure rate
             9   kb:KB-900     the first customer message described it wrongly as a slowdown
             6   ticket:4522   download of a big project only contains the header row
             7   ticket:4612   automation cannot tell how long to wait after being throttled
             4   ticket:4604   a visitor account could see something private
             5   ticket:4536   reminders arrive in the middle of the night

  questions with no answer in this archive
    how do I claim travel expenses for a client visit
    the office printer jams on thick paper
    what is the notice period for resigning
    which meeting room has the projector
```

The `shared words` column runs from four to ten, and that is a fair spread — but be careful about what
it means. Most of those shared words are `the`, `a`, `and`, `to`. The question targeting `ticket:4701`
shares four words with a ticket about export columns, and the four are not `spreadsheet`, `breaks`,
`header` or `moving`. A question with four shared words is a genuinely hard question for an index built
on word overlap, and this key contains several.

## When it breaks

The first failure of a gold set is that it is **too small to say what you want it to say**. Ten
questions means one question is ten percentage points. When
[1.4](../01-the-unit-you-index/1.4-the-margin-you-pay-for-twice.md) reports answered@3 moving from nine
to eight, that is one question changing its mind, and one question is exactly the resolution of this
instrument. A table like that supports *"the shape of this curve is X"* and does not support *"setting
A beats setting B by ten per cent"*. Every number in this day should be read at that resolution.

The second failure is **writing the key from the documents**. It is the natural way to do it — open a
ticket, read it, write a question about it — and it produces questions that quote the ticket, which
scores every retriever at a hundred per cent. The defence is the `shared words` column above, and the
discipline of writing the question first, from the question you actually got at the desk.

The third failure is a key that has drifted from the archive. It fails loudly, at least:

```text
Traceback (most recent call last):
  File "<stdin>", line 4, in <module>
  File ".../days/day-50-chunking-and-top-k/lab/gold.py", line 21, in main
    overlap = len(set(tokenize(query)) & set(tokenize(DOCS[want])))
                                                      ~~~~^^^^^^
KeyError: 'ticket:4611'
```

That is a `want` ref that no longer exists in `DOCS`, and it is what happens when somebody renames or
deletes an archived document and the key is not a test that runs in CI. A gold set that is not executed
by `./m check` decays into fiction within about two changes.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead.** They do not write the gold set from imagination; they mine it
from logs. The best source of gold questions is the actual queries users typed, and the best source of
gold answers is the cases where a human afterwards said *"this one was right"* — a thumbs-up, a closed
ticket citing an article, an agent copying a KB number into a reply. Fifty questions harvested that way
beat five hundred invented ones, and they keep arriving for free. The key also gets versioned and
reviewed like code, because changing the key changes every historical number and a silently edited key
is how a system appears to improve.

**What degrades at scale.** The archive moves and the key does not. Documents are archived, merged,
rewritten; the answer phrase that was in a runbook last quarter has been reworded this quarter, and
answered@k drops for a reason that has nothing to do with retrieval. The production defence is to make
the third column a **regular expression or a set of alternatives** rather than a literal string, and to
fail the build when a `want` ref no longer resolves — the `KeyError` above, promoted from an accident
to a check.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic.** The key is written by the people who built
the system, so it contains the questions they thought of. Real users ask shorter, vaguer and worse
questions than any engineer writes down — *"login broken"* — and no amount of tuning against a
well-formed key predicts behaviour on those.
[4.2](../04-the-floor/4.2-there-is-no-floor-that-separates-them.md) meets this head on, because the
rule that works beautifully on ten-word paraphrases is the rule that refuses to answer *"logged out"*.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Ten questions, hand-written by the person tuning the
knobs, is a smoke test and not an evaluation, and every conclusion in this document is stated at a
precision the sample size does not support. Ship the constants, but put the sweep in CI, write the
sample size next to every percentage, and open a ticket to harvest real queries so this is fifty rows by
the end of the quarter."*

**The interview question:** *"How do you know your retrieval is any good?"* An honest answer: *"I keep a
gold set — questions with the answer written down in advance — and I made it three columns instead of
two. The usual key has the question and the document that should come back, which gives you hit@k. That
metric could not see the bug I actually had: after chunking, the right document was in the top three
for ten questions out of ten while the text I was sending only contained the answer for six of them.
The third column is the phrase that has to be present in the retrieved text, and it is the column that
moved. I would also say what my key cannot do — it has ten questions, so one question is ten points, and
I only trust it for the shape of a curve, not for small differences."*

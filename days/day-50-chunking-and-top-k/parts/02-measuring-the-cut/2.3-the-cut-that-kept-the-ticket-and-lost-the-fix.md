# 2.3 — 💥 The cut that kept the ticket and lost the fix

## One-line answer

At a chunk size of four hundred characters the top result for *"customer bounced back to the sign-in
page during single sign-on"* is the correct ticket, scoring `0.381`, and it does **not** contain the
resolution — the cut fell between the sentence naming the cause and the sentence describing the effect,
and every metric that counts documents reports this retrieval as a success.

## The story

Your washing machine stops mid-cycle, so you take it back to the shop under guarantee.

The man behind the counter fills in a card. On the front he writes what you told him: *stops after ten
minutes, drum full of water, light flashing.* He tears off your half and keeps the rest.

Three weeks later it happens again. You bring the same machine back and a different man is on. He goes
to the drawer and finds the card. He reads it out to you, word for word, and it is completely correct:
stops, water, flashing light.

You ask what they did about it last time.

He turns the card over. The back is blank. Not because nobody wrote anything — the engineer wrote it, in
the workshop, on the back — but because the office photocopies the front of every card for the file and
files the photocopy. What is in the drawer is a true and accurate record of your complaint and nothing
at all about the repair.

He is holding the right card. He cannot answer the question.

## The idea in plain language

This is the failure the third column of the gold set exists to catch, and it happens because of a fact
about documents that nobody writes down: **a document usually states the problem in one place and the
resolution in another, with several sentences in between.**

A support ticket does it. A post-mortem does it. A doctor's note does it, a legal clause does it, a bug
report does it. Symptom, then investigation, then cause, then fix, in that order, spread over a page.

A blind character cut does not know any of this. It counts to four hundred and cuts. If four hundred
happens to fall in the investigation section, the symptom ends up in one row and the fix in another,
and now the archive contains two rows where it used to contain one document:

- **The symptom row** matches the question beautifully, because the question is a description of the
  symptom. It wins.
- **The fix row** matches the question badly, because the customer did not say the word "SameSite" —
  they said they got logged out. It loses.

Retrieval returns the winner. The winner is the half of the ticket that repeats the question back at
you.

That is the sting: **the better your question describes the problem, the more certain you are to
retrieve the half of the document that describes the problem.** The rows are ranked by resemblance to
the question, and the half you need is the half that resembles the question least.

## Why Sutra needs it

The desk exists to answer *"has anything like this happened before, and what did we do?"* The first half
of that question is a similarity search and the second half is not; the second half is a request for the
part of the past case that does **not** look like the present one.

Day 46 ended on a sentence that has been waiting for this day:
[5.1](../../../day-46-sessions-vs-memory/parts/05-failure-lab/5.1-the-past-that-matched-on-nothing.md)
watched an unranked store return four of four matches and send a customer an article about invoices.
Day 49 fixed the ranking. This part is the failure that survives the fix — a perfectly ranked result,
from the right document, that cannot answer the question — and it is a failure created by a decision
made at index time, before the ranking ever ran.

## The mechanism

🎬 **The scene.** You have read
[2.2](2.2-one-table-seven-chunk-sizes.md), you like the token column, and four hundred characters looks
better than nine hundred: `hit@3` is `10/10` on both and four hundred costs a hundred and fifteen fewer
tokens per question. You set the constant to four hundred and ship it.

😬 **The naive fix.** When somebody eventually reports that the desk gives half-answers, the instinct is
to raise k. Get more rows back and the missing half will be in there somewhere.

💥 **Why it fails.** Look at what is actually returned.

```python
# days/day-50-chunking-and-top-k/lab/split.py
QUERY, WANT, ANSWER = GOLD[0]


def main(argv: list[str]) -> int:
    size = int(argv[argv.index("--size") + 1]) if "--size" in argv else 400
    index = build_index(DOCS, size)
    for rank, (score, ref) in enumerate(search(index, QUERY, 3), start=1):
        right_doc = "yes" if parent(ref) == WANT else "no "
        carries = "yes" if ANSWER in index.texts[ref] else "NO "
        print(
            f"  {rank}. {score:.3f}  {ref:<16} right document: {right_doc}   answer in it: {carries}"
        )
        print(f"       {index.texts[ref][:100]!r}")
    return 0
```

**Line by line:**

- `QUERY, WANT, ANSWER = GOLD[0]` unpacks the first gold row into its three columns
  ([2.1](2.1-the-answer-key-you-write-before-you-tune.md)). Taking it from the key rather than typing
  the question here means this script grades itself against the same standard as every table in the day.
- `right_doc` is the `hit@k` test, applied to one row: does this result come from the document the key
  names?
- `carries` is the `answered@k` test, applied to the same row: is the answer phrase inside the text of
  this result?
- The two are printed **side by side on the same line**, which is the entire design of the script. The
  bug is invisible in either column alone and obvious when they disagree.
- `index.texts[ref][:100]!r` prints the beginning of the row as a Python repr, so leading spaces and
  mid-word starts are visible rather than swallowed by the terminal. A row that begins with a space is
  a row that began mid-sentence.

- The first arm runs at four hundred characters — the setting that looked cheaper.
- The second arm runs at nine hundred, the setting the sweep chose, so the two can be read against each

  3. 0.288  ticket:4521#0    right document: no    answer in it: NO

Rank one is the guarantee card with a blank back. It is the right ticket, it scores highest by a clear
margin, and the text is *"bounced to the sign-in page. Applied the fix from KB-104"* — the symptom
restated and a pointer to an article, with the actual cause sitting in `ticket:4610#1`, the row that did
not come back.

Rank two is the interesting one. It **does** contain the answer — the incident review's root-cause
paragraph says the cookie was issued without SameSite and Secure — and it comes from the wrong document
by the gold set's reckoning. So a model reading these three rows could, with luck, piece the answer
together from a document about a different incident. `answered@3` counts that as a success, which is
correct: the answer was in the context. But it arrived by accident, from a document the key did not
name, and the ticket the agent is actually working stayed half-told.

Now nine hundred:

```text
chunk size 900, 61 rows

  1. 0.380  ticket:4610#0    right document: yes   answer in it: yes
       'Ticket 4610. Signed out mid-form. The customer fills the long onboarding form and is returned to the'
  2. 0.304  ticket:4521#0    right document: no    answer in it: NO

  3. 0.253  kb:KB-900#1      right document: no    answer in it: yes
       's applied by hand. Impact. Roughly four hundred accounts were affected for two hours and the desk re'
```

Same query, almost the same top score — `0.380` against `0.381` — and rank one now says **yes** in both
columns. The symptom and the resolution are in one row because the cut that separated them did not
happen.

💡 **The insight.** Raising k is not the fix, and the reason is worth stating precisely. Raising k
widens the *net*, and the missing row is not outside the net because it scored just below the
cut-off — it is outside because it scores badly **by design**, being the half of the document that does
not resemble the question. At four hundred characters, `ticket:4610#1` — the row holding
*"Resolution: the session cookie is issued without the SameSite and Secure attributes"* — is not
somewhere around rank four or five waiting to be let in. It is a row whose words barely overlap the
words a customer uses. You would have to raise k until you were sending most of the archive, and by then
[3.3](../03-the-k-is-a-budget/3.3-twenty-rows-and-nothing-new-in-nineteen.md) is charging you for it.

**The fix for a cut is at the cut.** Three real options, in increasing order of effort:

1. **Cut bigger**, so symptom and resolution stay together. This is what
   [2.2](2.2-one-table-seven-chunk-sizes.md)'s sweep landed on, and on this archive it is enough.
2. **Cut on structure**, so the boundary lands at a paragraph break rather than at character four
   hundred. A ticket's `Resolution:` line is a heading in all but name.
3. **Widen after retrieval** — retrieve the small row, then send its neighbours too, using the parent
   ref the chunk ref already carries. This is the production answer and it is the one
   [1.4](../01-the-unit-you-index/1.4-the-margin-you-pay-for-twice.md) named.

## When it breaks

This failure has no error text. That is the whole reason it needs a part of its own, and it is worth
seeing what it looks like in the two places you might have hoped to catch it.

In the sweep, four hundred characters looks like this:

```text
         400     69     12716   10/10         9/10            746             164
```

`hit@3` is perfect. `answered@3` is nine out of ten. Nine out of ten is a good number, and if you are
reading a table of seven rows looking for the cheapest acceptable setting, nine out of ten with
forty-two per cent fewer tokens than nine hundred is a tempting row. Nothing in that line tells you
that the one question it fails is the desk's most common question, or that it fails it by returning a
confident, correct-looking, half-empty answer.

In the transcript it looks like a success:

```text
  1. 0.381  ticket:4610#2    right document: yes   answer in it: NO 
```

A monitoring dashboard tracking retrieval quality by document hit rate reports this as a hit. A
retrieval log recording *"query, top ref, score"* records `ticket:4610#2` at `0.381`, which is a strong
score. There is no line anywhere in the system that says *"the row I returned is the wrong half"*,
because nothing in the system knows what the halves are.

The only instrument that sees it is the third column of the gold set, and the only reason the third
column exists is that somebody sat down and wrote out what the answer to each question actually is.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead.** They separate *matching* from *sending*. The row that wins is
the row that best resembles the question, and that is the right way to choose; but what gets pasted into
the prompt is the winning row **expanded** — its neighbours, or the section it sits in, or the whole
parent document when the parent is short. The store keeps the parent ref and the offsets for exactly
this. It costs tokens, so it is bounded: expand up to a budget, and prefer expanding the top result over
including a fourth.

**What degrades at scale.** As the corpus grows, chunks compete harder, and the symptom half of a
document is a better competitor than the resolution half for every question phrased as a complaint. The
effect strengthens with corpus size: more documents means more symptom-shaped rows crowding the top of
every ranking, and the resolution rows sink. A system that was fine at five hundred documents gives
half-answers at fifty thousand for reasons that have nothing to do with the retriever.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic.** The model does not tell you. Handed the
symptom half of the right ticket, a competent model writes a fluent reply that restates the problem,
mentions KB-104 because the row named it, and stops. It reads like an answer. Nobody files a bug
against *"the desk was a bit vague"*, so the failure is reported as satisfaction and shows up as agents
quietly not using the desk.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"The top hit here is the right ticket and the wrong
half of it, and every metric on the dashboard calls that a success. Before we tune the size any further,
store the parent ref alongside each chunk and expand the winning chunk to its neighbours at retrieval
time. Also: the second result answered this question from a completely different incident, which is
luck, and I do not want a metric that scores luck as correctness without saying so."*

**The interview question:** *"Tell me about a retrieval bug that was hard to find."* An honest answer:
*"Chunk boundaries splitting a symptom from its resolution. My tickets state the problem at the top and
the fix at the bottom, and at a four-hundred-character chunk size the cut landed in between. The
question a support agent types is a description of the symptom, so the symptom row wins every time — it
scored 0.381 and it was the correct ticket, and it did not contain the fix. Everything on my dashboard
called it a hit. What found it was a second metric that checks whether the retrieved text actually
contains the answer phrase, not just whether the right document was returned. The instinct is to raise
k, and that does not work: the resolution row is not just below the cut-off, it is genuinely dissimilar
to the question, because it is the half that does not restate the problem. The fixes are all at index
time — cut bigger, cut on structure, or keep the parent ref and expand the winner to its neighbours
before you send it."*

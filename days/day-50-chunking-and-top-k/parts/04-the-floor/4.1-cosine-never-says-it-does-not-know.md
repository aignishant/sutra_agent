# 4.1 — 💥 Cosine never says it does not know

## One-line answer

Asked *"the office printer jams on thick paper"* — a question about which the archive contains nothing
whatsoever — retrieval returns three confident rows, the best of them scoring **0.388**, which is higher
than the score of the row that carries the correct answer for nine of the ten real questions.

## The story

You go into a shop for a particular kind of screw. You have the old one in your pocket and you put it on
the counter.

The assistant picks it up, turns it over, goes to the drawers behind him, and comes back with a small
paper bag. He does not say *"we don't have that one"*. He never says that. He puts the bag on the counter
and says this is the closest thing, and it looks close — same colour, roughly the same length, thread
that looks about right.

At home you find out. It goes in three turns and then binds, and now there is a half-driven screw in a
hole that will not take the correct one either.

He was not lying. He genuinely brought you the nearest thing in the shop. The trouble is that the nearest
thing in the shop is a category that always has a member, whether or not the shop stocks what you asked
for, and nothing in the transaction distinguished *"this is the one"* from *"this is the closest of the
wrong ones"*.

## The idea in plain language

Cosine similarity ranks. It does not judge.

Give it a question and an index, and it will compute a number for every row, sort them, and hand back the
top k. That is all it can do. There is no branch anywhere in the arithmetic that says *"none of these are
good enough"*, because the arithmetic has no notion of good enough — it has a notion of *higher than the
others*, and something is always higher than the others.

So a retriever built out of ranking alone has one behaviour, and it is worth stating as baldly as
possible:

**It returns k rows for every question, always, whatever you ask it.**

Ask it about your archive and it returns the right rows. Ask it about the weather, about your landlord,
about a product you do not sell, and it returns three rows with scores, formatted identically, indexed
identically, indistinguishable in type and shape from a correct answer.

This is a different failure from the two in section 1. Dilution and fragmentation are about the *right*
document scoring badly. This is about a **wrong** document scoring well, and there being no place in the
pipeline where anything is allowed to say no.

Day 46 met the same wall from the other side. Its
[5.1](../../../day-46-sessions-vs-memory/parts/05-failure-lab/5.1-the-past-that-matched-on-nothing.md)
watched a memory store match four of four filed tickets on one shared word and hand back an article about
printing invoices for a login fault, and its diagnosis was that the store had no ranking. Day 49 supplied
the ranking. The store now returns the *best* wrong answer instead of an arbitrary one, and — this is the
uncomfortable part — that is worse for a human reader, because the best wrong answer looks more like a
right answer than an arbitrary one does.

## Why Sutra needs it

Principle 10 is *fail honestly*: errors surface, escalate and are logged, and a result is never fabricated
to cover an error. A retriever that answers every question is a small, continuous violation of that
principle, running underneath a system that is otherwise careful.

It matters most for the desk specifically. A support agent asks *"has anything like this happened
before?"* and the honest answer is often **no** — a genuinely new fault, a new integration, a customer
doing something nobody has done. That answer is useful. *"Nobody has written this up"* tells the agent to
stop searching and start investigating. A confident, irrelevant past ticket tells them to go and read it,
and they will, and it will take them somewhere that has nothing to do with their problem.

[4.2](4.2-there-is-no-floor-that-separates-them.md) tries to fix this with a similarity floor and finds
that a floor alone cannot. [4.3](4.3-nothing-is-an-answer.md) is what the desk says when the fix works.

## The mechanism

🎬 **The scene.** Retrieval is working. The sweep is green, the k is chosen, the answers are good, and
somebody at the desk types a question about the office printer into the same box.

😬 **The naive fix.** There is nothing to fix. Nothing failed. That is the problem.

💥 **Why it fails.** Look at what comes back.

```python
# days/day-50-chunking-and-top-k/lab/garbage.py
def main(argv: list[str]) -> int:
    index = build_index(DOCS, CHUNK_SIZE)
    show_terms = "--terms" in argv
    for query in NOTHING:
        print(f"  {query!r}")
        if show_terms:
            known = {t for t in set(tokenize(query)) if t in index.idf}
            print(f"     words the index has ever seen: {sorted(known)}")
            print(f"     of those, informative       : {sorted(informative_terms(index, query))}")
        for score, ref in search(index, query, TOP_K):
            matched = sorted(set(tokenize(query)) & set(tokenize(index.texts[ref])))
            tail = f"   matched on {matched}" if show_terms else ""
            print(f"     {score:.3f}  {ref:<14} {index.texts[ref][:52]!r}{tail}")
    return 0
```

**Line by line:**

- The loop runs over `NOTHING`, the fourth list in the gold set
  ([2.1](../02-measuring-the-cut/2.1-the-answer-key-you-write-before-you-tune.md)) — four questions whose
  correct result is nothing at all. A test suite with no unanswerable questions in it can never detect
  this failure, which is why they were written down at the same time as the answerable ones.
- `search(index, query, TOP_K)` is called with **no floor**. This is Day 49's retriever exactly as it
  stands, so the transcript below is the current behaviour of the system and not a straw man.
- `known` is the set of the question's words that appear anywhere in the index. It is the diagnostic
  that turns *"the score was high"* into *"the score was high because of these two words"*.
- `informative_terms` filters that set down to words rare enough to carry meaning — the definition is in
  `_index.py` and [4.2](4.2-there-is-no-floor-that-separates-them.md) uses it to build the repair.
- `matched` per row shows which words this particular row shared with the question, printed beside the
  score, so the reader can see that a strong-looking number came from nothing.
- `--terms` gates the diagnostics because the plain arm is what a caller sees in production: scores and
  refs, and no explanation.

**Line by line:**

- The first arm is what the system returns. Read it as a caller would, with no idea what is behind the
  numbers.
- `--terms` adds the explanation. The gap between the two arms is the point of the part.

Read the second block twice.

`'the office printer jams on thick paper'` — the words the index has ever seen are `['on', 'the']`.
**Every meaningful word in that question is unknown to the archive.** Not weakly matched. Absent.
`office`, `printer`, `jams`, `thick`, `paper` do not exist in the vocabulary at all.

And the result is `0.388`.

Put that number in context. The row that actually carries the answer scores `0.380` for the sign-in
question, `0.303` for one of the incident-review questions, `0.236` for the cookie question and `0.180`
for the export-columns question — those figures come from
[4.2](4.2-there-is-no-floor-that-separates-them.md)'s run. **A question with no informative words in it
at all outscored nine of the ten real answers.**

The reason is visible in the `matched on` column: `['on', 'the']`. Cosine divides by the length of both
vectors, so a question consisting of two surviving stopwords is a very short vector pointing almost
entirely along `the`, and a row that uses `the` and `on` a lot points a little in that direction too.
Two short vectors with a small angle between them score highly. The arithmetic is completely correct and
the result is completely meaningless.

💡 **The insight.** The failure is not in the ranking; it is in the **absence of a refusal**. A retriever
has exactly one thing it can say, and it says it every time. Adding a way to say nothing is not a
refinement of retrieval — it is a second decision, made after the ranking, out of information the
ranking threw away.

Notice what the diagnostic line gives you for free: `of those, informative: []`. That question could have
been rejected before a single score was computed, on the grounds that there is nothing in it to search
for. That is the thread [4.2](4.2-there-is-no-floor-that-separates-them.md) pulls.

## When it breaks

The dangerous property of this failure is that **it does not break**. There is no error, no warning, no
exception, no empty list. Every part of the system reports success.

Here is what it looks like without the diagnostics — which is what production looks like:

```text
index: 61 rows, chunk 900, k=3, no floor

  'how do I claim travel expenses for a client visit'
     0.246  ticket:4612#0  'Ticket 4612. API returns a rate limit with no retry '
     0.134  ticket:4525#0  'Ticket 4525. Export takes an hour and then stops. A '
     0.122  ticket:4570#0  'Ticket 4570. Search returns nothing for project name'

  'the office printer jams on thick paper'
     0.388  ticket:4610#0  'Ticket 4610. Signed out mid-form. The customer fills'
     0.335  kb:KB-900#0    'KB-900 Incident review, March. Summary. This review '
     0.247  ticket:4501#0  'Ticket 4501. Invoice shows the wrong company name. T'
```

Three rows, three real refs, three plausible scores, descending. A retrieval log records a top score of
`0.388`, which is a strong score by this index's standards. A monitoring dashboard tracking *"queries
that returned results"* records a hundred per cent. Nothing on any screen anywhere says that the top
result is a sign-in ticket returned to a question about a printer.

And it gets worse when a model reads it. Handed a ticket about being signed out mid-form and asked about
a printer, a competent model does one of two things: it says the context is not relevant — which is the
good case and is a coin flip — or it finds a bridge. *"This may be related to a session issue"* is a
sentence a model will write, and a support agent will read it, and it is a sentence about nothing.

The failure is worst on precisely the questions where retrieval should have been most valuable: a
genuinely new fault. That is the case where the archive really has nothing, where the honest answer is
*no*, and where instead the agent is handed the three nearest old tickets and sent off to read them.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead.** Every serious retrieval system has a **refusal path**, and it is
built out of at least two of these three signals rather than one:

1. **An absolute score floor** — reject anything below a threshold
   ([4.2](4.2-there-is-no-floor-that-separates-them.md) measures what that alone can and cannot do).
2. **A vocabulary or coverage check** — if the question's meaningful words are not in the index at all,
   there is nothing to search for and the ranking is arithmetic on stopwords.
3. **A score-gap check** — if the top score and the twentieth score are nearly equal, the ranking has not
   discriminated between anything and the top row is not meaningfully "best".

They also **strip stopwords at index time**, which removes the specific mechanism above. That is a real
fix and it is not a complete one: it turns *"matched on the and on"* into *"matched on nothing"*, and a
question that matches nothing must still be handled, which is the refusal path again.

**What degrades at scale.** It gets worse, and it gets worse in a way that feels like improvement. A
larger archive means the nearest wrong document is *nearer* — with sixty thousand rows there is always
something that shares three or four words with any question, so the score of a meaningless match rises.
The confident-nonsense floor comes up as the corpus grows, and so does the plausibility of the text
returned.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic.** Users ask questions outside the domain
constantly, and they do not phrase them as obviously out of scope. *"Can I get a copy of my invoice for
tax"* to a technical support archive, *"who do I talk to about my contract"*, *"is the office open on
Friday"*. Each one gets three tickets and a confident tone. Nobody files a bug, because the reply reads
like an answer, and the failure is measured in trust rather than in errors.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"`search` cannot return an empty list. That is not a
tuning issue, it is a missing branch: there is no code path in this system where retrieval is allowed to
say no. Before you tune the floor, log the top score and the number of informative query terms on every
retrieval, and show me the distribution for a week — I want to know how often we are answering questions
this archive has nothing to do with."*

**The interview question:** *"What happens when your retriever gets a question your corpus cannot
answer?"* An honest answer: *"By default, it answers it anyway, and confidently. Cosine similarity ranks
— it has no notion of 'good enough', only 'higher than the others', and something is always higher. I
measured it: I asked my archive about an office printer, a subject it contains nothing about, and the top
result scored 0.388, which beat the correct answer for nine of my ten real questions. The reason was
that the only two words of the question the index had ever seen were 'on' and 'the', so the whole thing
was cosine over stopwords. The important part is that nothing broke — three real refs, three plausible
scores, a green dashboard. So a retriever needs a refusal path as a separate decision after the ranking,
built from a score floor plus something the ranking discarded, like how many meaningful query terms are
in the index at all."*

# 3.3 — 💥 Twenty rows, and nothing new in nineteen

## One-line answer

Raising k from two to twenty answers the same ten questions out of ten, costs eight and a half times as
much over a conversation, and pushes the answer-bearing row away from the front of the block — for one
question it lands at **rank 7, a quarter of the way into the context**, which is the region the model
reads least reliably.

## The story

You knock on a neighbour's door to ask for the electrician's phone number.

She is generous, and generosity is the problem. She goes inside and comes back with her address diary —
the fat one with the elastic band, filled in over fifteen years, three different pens, entries crossed
out and rewritten in the margins — and puts it in your hands and says it is definitely in there.

It is definitely in there. You stand on the step turning pages. The names at the front you can read
because you started at the front. The last few pages you can read because you have just been looking at
them. Somewhere in the middle there is an electrician, and also a different one from before they
moved, and a number with no name against it, and by page forty you have stopped reading properly and
started skimming.

You did not want the diary. You wanted eleven digits. She gave you everything, and everything is what
you have to search now instead of her.

## The idea in plain language

There are two separate arguments against a large k, and they are usually collapsed into one, which is
why neither lands.

**The first is cost**, and [3.1](3.1-k-is-a-budget-not-a-preference.md) measured it: k multiplies by the
number of turns, so a big k is expensive in a place you were not looking. That argument is about money
and room.

**The second is that a large k makes the answer harder to use**, and it is the one this part is about.
It has nothing to do with cost. Even if tokens were free, twenty rows would be worse than two, because
of where the useful row ends up.

The mechanism is **position**. A prompt is a sequence, and a model does not attend to every part of a
long sequence equally. Day 19's
[3.3](../../../day-19-context-engineering-selection/parts/03-selection/3.3-position-is-not-presence.md)
made this a rule for Sutra — *a fact in the window is not a fact the model will use* — and the paper it
rests on measured the shape: best at the beginning, best at the end, worst in the middle.

Now put those two facts together, because the combination is the failure:

- At **k = 2**, the answer-bearing row is one of two rows. It occupies roughly half the retrieved block.
  It is at the front and it is also near the back. Both good positions.
- At **k = 20**, the same row is one of twenty. If it ranks first it is still at the front and nothing is
  lost. If it ranks seventh — and rank seven is a perfectly normal rank for a real question — it now sits
  a quarter of the way into a block of twenty, with six irrelevant rows in front of it and thirteen
  behind.

Raising k did not move the answer down the ranking. The answer was always at rank seven. What raising k
did was **build a middle for it to be in**. At k equals three there is no middle; there are three rows and
the reader sees all of them.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because raising k is the universal first response to a retrieval complaint, and it is the one change a
support desk under pressure will actually make. Somebody says *"it missed the obvious ticket"*, k goes
from three to ten, the obvious ticket comes back, everybody moves on
([3.2](3.2-where-the-curve-goes-flat.md)'s production section calls this out as the constant that never
gets lowered).

Sutra needs the reader to be able to say why that is a bad trade with numbers rather than with a feeling.
Day 46 ended on *the cap fixes cost but buys no correctness, because you cannot take the top three of an
unranked list*. Day 49 supplied the ranking, and this is the sentence that completes the pair: **a ranked
list with a bad k is still a bad answer.** Ranking makes k meaningful. It does not make k safe.

## The paper behind it

> **Lost in the Middle: How Language Models Use Long Contexts**
> `arXiv:2307.03172` · 2023
> <https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172>

It measured what happens when the relevant document is moved to different positions inside a long
context, and found accuracy highest when it sits at the beginning or the end and significantly degraded
when it sits in the middle — including in models built specifically for long contexts.

Taught in full on Day 19:
[`01-lost-in-the-middle.md`](../../../day-19-context-engineering-selection/papers/01-lost-in-the-middle.md).
Read it there. What this part adds is the arithmetic on the other side of the same problem: retrieval is
the thing that decides how long the context is and where in it the answer lands.

## The mechanism

🎬 **The scene.** The desk misses a question. The retrieval log shows the right ticket at rank four.
`TOP_K` is three. The fix is obvious and takes one character.

😬 **The naive fix.** `TOP_K = 20`. Generous, safe, and it definitely catches rank four.

💥 **Why it fails.** Measure where the answer ends up.

```python
# days/day-50-chunking-and-top-k/lab/middle.py
CHUNK_SIZE = 280


def main(argv: list[str]) -> int:
    k = int(argv[argv.index("--k") + 1]) if "--k" in argv else 20
    index = build_index(DOCS, CHUNK_SIZE)
    for query, want, answer in GOLD:
        found = search(index, query, k)
        block = sum(len(index.texts[ref]) for _, ref in found)
        offset = 0
        rank = 0
        for position, (_, ref) in enumerate(found, start=1):
            if answer in index.texts[ref]:
                rank = position
                break
            offset += len(index.texts[ref])
        useful = sum(1 for _, ref in found if parent(ref) == want)
        where = f"{offset / block:.0%}" if rank else "not there"
```

**Line by line:**

- `CHUNK_SIZE = 280` rather than the shipped nine hundred, because this part needs questions whose
  answer is **not** at rank one, and at nine hundred characters almost every gold question ranks its
  answer first. Demonstrating a middle requires something to be in the middle.
- `block` is the total characters of everything retrieved. It is the length of the thing the model has
  to read, which is what "the middle" is measured against.
- `offset` accumulates the length of every row **before** the answer-bearing one, so it is the character
  position at which the answer starts inside the assembled block. That is the number the paper's finding
  is about — not the rank, the *position in the text*.
- The loop `break`s at the first answer-bearing row and `offset` is only added to before that point,
  which is why the `offset += ...` line sits after the `if`. Adding it first would count the answer row
  itself and put the answer permanently past its own start.
- `offset / block` as a percentage turns two archive-specific character counts into something
  comparable across questions.
- `useful` is carried over from [3.2](3.2-where-the-curve-goes-flat.md) so that the reader can see, on
  the same line, how many of the twenty rows were even from the right document.

```bash

```

**Line by line:**

- `--k 2` is the control arm: the k [3.2](3.2-where-the-curve-goes-flat.md) chose.
- No flag means k equals twenty, the generous fix.
- Everything else is held: same index, same chunk size, same key, same archive.

Measured on 2026-09-05, at k equals two:

```text
index: 75 rows, chunk 280, k=2

  ticket:4633       -                  -   not there      115             1
  ticket:4701       -                  -   not there       86             1
  kb:KB-104         1                  0          0%      119             1
  kb:KB-900         1                  0          0%      123             2
  kb:KB-900         1                  0          0%      115             1

  ticket:4604       1                  0          0%       55             1
  ticket:4536       2                121         48%       55             1
```

Eight of ten answers are at rank one, at character zero — the front of the block, the best position
there is. Two are missing entirely, which is the honest cost of a small k at this chunk size, and it is
what k equals twenty is being reached for.

Now k equals twenty:

```text
index: 75 rows, chunk 280, k=20

  target        rank   answer starts at   of block   tokens   useful rows
  ticket:4610       1                  0          0%     1037             3
  ticket:4633       3                524         12%      953             2
  ticket:4701       7               1028         24%      936             3
  kb:KB-104         1                  0          0%      820             1
  kb:KB-900         1                  0          0%      787             6
  kb:KB-900         1                  0          0%      898             5
  ticket:4522       1                  0          0%      774             1
  ticket:4612       1                  0          0%      722             1
  ticket:4604       1                  0          0%      731             1
  ticket:4536       2                121          3%      904             1
```

The two missing answers are now present. That is the win, and it is real: `ticket:4633` at rank three
and `ticket:4701` at rank seven.

Now read what it cost.

**`ticket:4701`'s answer begins 1,028 characters into a 4,260-character block** — a quarter of the way
in, behind six rows nobody needed, with thirteen more behind it. That is precisely the region the paper
measured as worst. The answer is present. Presence is not the same thing as use, which is the sentence
Day 19's [3.3](../../../day-19-context-engineering-selection/parts/03-selection/3.3-position-is-not-presence.md)
exists to install.

**Every other question got worse for nothing.** `ticket:4604`'s answer was at rank one, character zero,
in a fifty-five-token block. It is still at rank one and character zero — in a **seven-hundred-and-
thirty-one-token** block. The answer did not move; nineteen rows of unrelated text were stacked behind
it. Thirteen times the reading, same answer, and the model now has to ignore more than it reads.

**And `ticket:4536` shows the trap in miniature.** At k equals two its answer starts at 48% of the block
— which sounds terrible until you notice the block is fifty-five tokens, so "the middle" is a middle you
could hold in one glance. At k equals twenty the same answer is at 3% of a nine-hundred-and-four-token
block. The percentage improved and the situation got worse, because a percentage of a large number is a
long way to read. Percentages of different-sized things are not comparable, and this row is the reminder.

💡 **The insight.** k equals twenty did not find anything the retriever could not find at rank seven all
along. It changed **what you send**, and sending more of the archive makes the model do the retrieval you
were supposed to do for it — over a worse index, with no scores, in the one region of the context where
it reads least reliably.

**A ranked list with a bad k is still a bad answer.** The right response to *"the answer was at rank
seven"* is to ask why it was at rank seven — a chunk boundary
([2.3](../02-measuring-the-cut/2.3-the-cut-that-kept-the-ticket-and-lost-the-fix.md)), a chunk size that
is too small ([1.3](../01-the-unit-you-index/1.3-cut-too-small-to-mean-anything.md)), a question that is
not a similarity search at all
([5.1](../05-the-wrong-tool/5.1-when-the-answer-is-a-rule.md)) — and to fix that. At nine hundred
characters, on this same key, every answer that exists is at rank one or two
([3.2](3.2-where-the-curve-goes-flat.md)). The cure for rank seven was the chunk size, not the k.

## When it breaks

This measurement has a real limit and it must be stated plainly, because it is the difference between an
argument and a claim.

**This part does not measure the model getting the answer wrong.** It measures cost, and rank, and
character position. The step from *"the answer is a quarter of the way into a long block"* to *"the model
will use it less reliably"* is the paper's, measured on its benchmarks, and Day 19 reproduced the
positional effect in
[`lab/papers/lost-in-the-middle/positions.py`](../../../day-19-context-engineering-selection/papers/01-lost-in-the-middle.md).
This day spends zero model calls, so it does not re-run that and does not pretend to. What it contributes
is the other half: **retrieval is the component that decides how long the context is and where the answer
sits in it**, and that half is measurable with arithmetic.

The second limit: `answered@20` is `10/10` and `answered@2` is `8/10` at this chunk size. On the metric,
twenty is better. Anyone reading only the quality column would raise k, and they would be following the
evidence. The argument against them is the cost column, the noise column and the paper — three things,
none of which is the quality metric, which is exactly why this is a trap that catches careful people.

And the break with no error text is the ordinary case:

```text
  ticket:4604       1                  0          0%      731             1
```

Rank one. Character zero. Perfect on every quality measure, and it is sending seven hundred and
thirty-one tokens to deliver an answer that fitted in fifty-five. Nothing anywhere reports this. It is a
row that says *"working"* in every column that anybody watches.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead.** Three things, and the first two are ordering rather than sizing:

- **Put the best row last, or first, and never in the middle of a pile.** If you must send many rows,
  order them so the highest-scoring one is adjacent to the question — many production prompts place
  retrieved context immediately before the user's question for exactly this reason.
- **De-duplicate before sending.** Adjacent chunks from the same document, and near-identical rows from
  overlap ([1.4](../01-the-unit-you-index/1.4-the-margin-you-pay-for-twice.md)), waste slots and pad the
  middle. Collapsing them is usually the cheapest quality win available.
- **Use a reranker when rank seven is common.** A reranker scores each retrieved row against the question
  properly instead of by vector distance, so twenty candidates can be narrowed to two good ones before
  anything reaches the prompt. That is the honest role of the parked infrastructure in
  [6.2](../06-in-production/6.2-rerankers-hybrid-search-and-a-vector-database.md): it exists to let you
  retrieve widely and still send narrowly.

**What degrades at scale.** Long contexts arrive from more directions than retrieval. The system
instruction grows, skills are loaded, tool declarations accumulate, and the conversation itself gets
longer. Retrieval's block is one contributor, and it is the one that grows silently because k is a
constant nobody revisits. The position of your retrieved answer within the whole prompt gets worse over a
year without k changing at all.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic.** It looks like intermittent stupidity. The desk
answers eight questions well and gives a vague answer to the ninth, and the ninth is the one where the
useful row was at rank seven of twenty. It is not reproducible from the logs, because the logs say the
answer was retrieved. It is a positional effect, and positional effects present as flakiness.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"k was raised to twenty in an incident and the incident
is closed. Put it back and fix the ranking: the question that needed twenty had its answer at rank seven
because of a chunk boundary, and at the chunk size we ship it is at rank one. If we genuinely need to
retrieve wide, retrieve wide and rerank down to two before it reaches the prompt — do not paste twenty
rows into a window and hope."*

**The interview question:** *"Somebody raises top-k from three to twenty to fix a missed answer. What do
you say?"* An honest answer: *"That it will work and it is still the wrong fix. On my archive, going from
two to twenty answered the same questions and cost eight and a half times as much over a six-turn
conversation, because retrieved rows are written into the transcript and re-sent. And it moves the answer
into the middle: for one question the answer-bearing row was at rank seven, starting a quarter of the way
into a four-thousand-character block, which is the region Lost in the Middle measured as the least
reliably used. Even the questions that were fine got worse — one of them had its answer at rank one,
character zero, and I turned a fifty-five-token block into a seven-hundred-and-thirty-one-token one to
deliver the identical answer. The real question is why the answer was at rank seven, and in my case it was
a chunk boundary; at the chunk size I ship, it is at rank one. If you genuinely need to retrieve wide, put
a reranker between retrieval and the prompt and send two."*

# 1.2 — The Pod is the unit, not the container

## One-line answer

A cluster does not schedule containers, it schedules Pods — a group of containers that share one
network namespace, one lifecycle and one machine — and every interesting decision today follows from
the fact that containers in one Pod reach each other on `localhost` while containers in two Pods do
not.

## The story

The driver and the conductor.

They are two people doing two jobs. One steers, one takes fares and rings the bell, and neither
could do the other's work. But they are not two independent workers who happen to be near each
other: they are assigned to the same bus for the same shift. When the bus goes to the depot, both go
to the depot. When it is off the road, neither of them is driving a different bus that afternoon.
They talk by turning their heads, because they are in the same vehicle — no radio, no phone number,
nothing anybody else can listen to.

Nobody schedules a driver. The depot schedules a bus, and the bus comes with its crew.

## The idea in plain language

The smallest thing Kubernetes will place on a machine is a **Pod**. A Pod holds one or more
containers, and what makes it one thing rather than a folder of things is four shared facts:

| Shared | What it means in practice |
| --- | --- |
| **network namespace** | one IP address for the whole Pod; containers reach each other on `127.0.0.1` and must not both claim the same port |
| **lifecycle** | scheduled together, restarted together, deleted together |
| **node** | always on one machine; a Pod is never half here and half there |
| **volumes** | any volume declared on the Pod can be mounted into more than one container |

The first row is the one that decides this day. Two containers in the same Pod are, from the
network's point of view, two processes on one box. There is no service, no DNS name, no port
mapping and nothing for anybody else to connect to. That is the driver turning his head.

Two containers in *different* Pods are two boxes. To talk, one needs an address the other can
resolve, and in Kubernetes an address for a Pod means a **Service** — a stable name and IP that
selects Pods by label and forwards to them. That is a radio, and a radio is a thing other people can
tune into.

**A useful rule of thumb:** put things in one Pod when you would have put them on one machine, and
in separate Pods when you would have bought a second machine. Two processes that must be on the same
host, scale together and die together belong in one Pod. Two services with independent lifecycles do
not.

The container that runs alongside the main one, providing something to it rather than to the outside
world, has a name: a **sidecar**. Logging shippers, proxies and — for Sutra — an MCP server are the
usual examples.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because `sutra-mcp` has been a separate process since Day 33, and until today nothing forced a
decision about where it lives. Locally it was another terminal. In Day 86's compose file it was
another service on a shared network. Neither of those made the *boundary* explicit.

Kubernetes does. The MCP server either goes in the Pod with the agent or it gets a Service, and
those two documents describe two different systems with two different blast radii. Part
[2.1](../02-the-sidecar-decision/2.1-one-pod-or-two.md) makes the choice; part
[2.2](../02-the-sidecar-decision/2.2-the-address-is-the-boundary.md) is why it is a security
decision rather than a convenience one.

## The mechanism

The lab's manifest holds two containers and one Pod. Here is what the loader sees when it reads
them:

```python
def containers(deployment: dict) -> list[Container]:
    """App containers and init containers together, each labelled with what it really is."""
    spec = pod_spec(deployment)
    out = [Container(c["name"], c, "app") for c in spec.get("containers", [])]
    for c in spec.get("initContainers", []):
        kind = "sidecar" if c.get("restartPolicy") == "Always" else "init"
        out.append(Container(c["name"], c, kind))
    return out
```

**Line by line:**

- Two lists are read, not one. A Pod spec has `containers` and `initContainers`, and a container in
  either list is in the Pod — same network namespace, same node. Reading only `containers` is the
  most common way to miss half a Pod.
- `restartPolicy: Always` on an entry in `initContainers` is what distinguishes a *sidecar* from an
  ordinary init container that runs once and exits. Kubernetes has no separate `sidecars:` list;
  this field is the whole of the declaration, which is why the loader records it as `kind` rather
  than throwing it away.
- `pod_spec(deployment)` reaches through `spec.template.spec` — the Pod spec is nested two levels
  inside a Deployment, because a Deployment is a description of Pods rather than a Pod.
- Returning one flat list with a `kind` on each entry lets every check in part
  [3.1](../03-what-the-manifest-must-say/3.1-checking-what-the-cluster-would-read.md) ask *"which
  containers are in this Pod"* without repeating the two-list dance.

**Line by line:**

- `address.py` groups containers by Deployment, which is grouping by Pod, because one Deployment

**Line by line:**

- The same script; the only difference is which document it reads.

```text
manifest: k8s/deployment-split.yaml
  Deployments: ['sutra-api', 'sutra-mcp']

  Pod from sutra-api  holds [('sutra-api', 'app')]
  Pod from sutra-mcp  holds [('sutra-mcp', 'app')]
```

**Two Pods, and a Service appears.** Nobody added the Service for fun: the moment the two containers
are in different Pods, the agent needs an address for the MCP server, and a Service is the only way
a Pod gets one. The topology did not just move a container — it created a new named thing in the
cluster.

## When it breaks

The Pod's shared network namespace is a resource with one obvious way to collide:

**Two containers, one port.** Both containers see the same `127.0.0.1`, so if the agent listens on
8080 and the MCP server also listens on 8080, the second one to start gets a bind error and the Pod
never becomes healthy. The error is the ordinary operating-system one, and this lab can produce the
same class of failure honestly, because `serve.py` handles it:

```python
    try:
        listener.bind(("127.0.0.1", PORT))
    except OSError:
        return 2  # something else holds the port; race.py reports this rather than hanging
```

**Line by line:**

- `bind` is where a port collision surfaces, not `listen` and not `accept`. A container that fails
  here has started, done work, and then died — which is precisely the state part
  [4.1](../04-the-order-nobody-promised/4.1-the-clinic-was-not-open-yet.md) is about.
- Returning `2` rather than raising keeps the failure distinguishable from a crash, so the parent
  can say *"the port was taken"* instead of showing a traceback about a machine that is fine.

The second failure has no error text at all: **assuming isolation that is not there.** Containers in
one Pod are isolated by filesystem and process namespace but *not* by network. A container in the
Pod can reach anything else in the Pod on loopback, on any port, with no credential. Part
[2.2](../02-the-sidecar-decision/2.2-the-address-is-the-boundary.md) is where that becomes a
security argument in both directions, and it is the reason Principle 13 has something to say about a
choice that looks like plumbing.

## In production

**What a professional does.** Treats "same Pod" as a strong claim and asks for a reason. The
standard test is three questions: do these two things have to be on the same machine, do they scale
one-for-one, and do they have to be upgraded together? Three yeses is a sidecar. Two out of three is
usually two Deployments and a Service.

**What degrades at scale.** Pods with many containers get hard to reason about, because everything
in the table above is shared. Memory limits interact, a crash-looping sidecar takes the whole Pod's
readiness with it, and the port space becomes something a team has to coordinate. The usual
discipline is one app container plus as few sidecars as the platform forces on you.

**The failure that only shows with real traffic.** Independent scaling needs. A sidecar scales
exactly with its Pod, which is fine until the thing in the sidecar is doing work proportional to
something other than the agent's request rate — an archive server that other agents also want, for
example. Then you are running N copies of a thing that needed two, paying for all of them, and part
[2.3](../02-the-sidecar-decision/2.3-what-the-sidecar-costs.md) is the sentence that has to be
written down today so that somebody notices later.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"You have two containers in this Pod. Write one
sentence in the manifest saying why they cannot be in two, and one saying when that stops being
true."*

**The interview question:** *"Why does Kubernetes have Pods instead of just scheduling
containers?"* The answer that shows experience names the shared network namespace first, because it
is the thing you cannot get any other way — you can co-schedule containers with affinity rules, but
you cannot give them one loopback interface without putting them in one Pod.

# 3.2 — Which calls are safe to retry

## One-line answer

A refusal and a timeout are not two flavours of the same failure: a **429 means the request was never
performed**, so retrying it is free, while a timeout means **you do not know**, and the same retry loop
that credits a card once after a refusal credits it **twice** after a lost acknowledgement.

## The story

You are at the station and your travel card is nearly empty, so you go to the top-up machine by the
gates.

The first machine has a message on the screen before you touch it: *top-up not available at this
machine*. That is annoying and it is completely harmless. Nothing of yours has gone anywhere. Your
card is in your hand with the same balance it had, your money is in your pocket, and the machine has
not done a single thing on your behalf. You walk twenty steps to the machine on the other side of the
concourse and start again, and you would happily do that four times over, because each attempt costs
you nothing but the walk.

The second machine takes your note. It pulls it in, the screen says *processing*, and then it goes
blank. No receipt. No beep. The card slot does not light up. You stand there for a while and then you
tap the card on the reader by the gate to see the balance, and the reader shows the old number — which
might mean the top-up never happened, or might mean it happened and has not reached the gates yet.

Now the walk to the third machine is a completely different decision. Feed in another note and there
are two possibilities: you fix the problem, or you have just paid twice for one top-up and you will
spend a week and two phone calls getting one of them back.

Same station, same card, same "try again" instinct. What changed is not how hard the machine failed.
It is whether the machine had already done something before it stopped talking to you.

## The idea in plain language

Everything the day has built so far treats a refusal as a thing to wait out. Section 1's ladder,
section 2's `retry-after`, part [3.1](3.1-escalate-never-invent.md)'s honest ending — all of them
assume that retrying is a reasonable thing to want to do. This part is the condition under which that
assumption holds.

One term first. A call is **idempotent** when performing it twice has the same effect on the world as
performing it once. *Read the vendor's status* is idempotent: ask twice, and the world is exactly as it
was. *Add 100 to this card* is not: ask twice, and the card has 200 more on it. The word says nothing
about whether you get the same *answer* both times — a status endpoint may well answer differently a
second later — it is entirely about the **effect left behind**.

Now the two failures, which look alike in a log and are not alike at all.

- **A refusal.** The server answered. It said 429, or 503, and the meaning of that answer is *I have
  not done this*. The request reached the server, the server declined to perform it, and it told you
  so. Your side of the world and the server's side of the world are still in agreement.
- **An unavailability.** The server did not answer. The connection dropped, or the read timed out, or
  a proxy in the middle gave up. **You have no verdict.** The request may never have arrived, may have
  arrived and been rejected, or may have arrived and been fully performed with only the reply lost.

The whole rule follows from that difference:

| What came back | Was the request performed? | Safe to retry? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `429` / `503` with a body | no, definitively | **always** — nothing happened, so nothing can happen twice |
| a timeout, a dropped connection, no reply | unknown | **only if the call is idempotent** |

The second row is the one that costs money. A retry loop cannot tell the difference between "it never
happened" and "it happened and the receipt was lost", because from inside the loop both are the same
silence. So the loop retries, and if the call was not idempotent, the effect lands a second time.

Notice what this does to the day so far. Every ladder, every honoured `retry-after`, every escalation
is about **when** to try again. This part is about **whether**, and it is a different question that has
to be answered first. A perfect ladder attached to a non-idempotent call that timed out is a machine
for doing something twice, politely, with good spacing.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because the desk's tools are not all reads.

The desk asks a vendor for status, which is a read and is idempotent — the whole of `honest.py` is
built on a call it is always safe to repeat, which is exactly why part 3.1 could concentrate on the
*answer* and ignore the *effect*. But the same desk closes tickets, sends replies to customers, and
writes to systems that belong to other teams. Those are not reads. A reply sent twice is a customer
who has been told the same thing twice by a machine, and a ticket closed twice is a duplicate row in
somebody's audit trail.

So a retry policy for Sutra cannot be one setting. It has to be attached to the call, because the
answer to *may I retry this on a timeout* is a property of the call and not of the client, the
provider, or the day's mood. Section 5's plugin will apply a policy across everything the desk does,
and this part is the classification it has to apply it with.

The wider Sutra rule is Principle 13, *blast radius before capability*. A retry loop is a capability —
it converts a transient failure into a success — and its blast radius is exactly the set of effects
that can happen more than once because of it. You do not get to add the loop without first knowing
that set.

## The mechanism

The lab encodes the distinction in the type system, which is the cheapest place to put it. Here is the
refusal:

```python
class Refused(Exception):
    """A refusal that carries what the server said.

    `retry_after` is `None` when the server did not say - which is a different situation from
    `retry_after=0`, and conflating the two is part 2.3's failure.
    """

    def __init__(self, status: int, retry_after: float | None) -> None:
        after = "none" if retry_after is None else f"{retry_after:.0f}s"
        super().__init__(f"{status}: refused, retry-after {after}")
        self.status = status
        self.retry_after = retry_after
```

**Line by line:**

- `status` is stored, not just formatted into the message, because a `429` and a `503` are both
  refusals and a caller may want to treat them differently — one is *you are asking too often*, the
  other is *I am temporarily unable*. Both, crucially, are answers.
- `retry_after` is a `float | None`, and part
  [2.3](../02-the-number-the-server-gave-you/2.3-zero-is-not-absent.md) is about why `None` is not
  `0`. What matters here is simply that both are values the **server sent**. Something at the other
  end read the request and formed an opinion about it.
- The message is built in the constructor, so every place this exception is printed shows the status
  and the stated wait in the same shape. When part 3.1's `Escalated` carries `last`, this is the string
  it carries.

And here is the other one, which is a whole class for the sake of one docstring:

```python
class Unavailable(Exception):
    """A failure that is not a refusal: the request never got a verdict.

    Kept separate from `Refused` because the correct response differs. A refusal means *not now*; a
    connection error means *unknown*, and retrying an unknown is safe only when the call is
    idempotent - part 3.2.
    """
```

**Line by line:**

- It has no `__init__`, no fields and no formatting. That is the point: there is nothing to carry.
  `Refused` has a status and a stated wait because the server spoke; `Unavailable` has neither because
  it did not.
- Read the docstring's reasoning as the design argument it is: *kept separate from `Refused` because
  the correct response differs*. Two exception classes exist here not because two different things
  went wrong in the network, but because the **caller has to do two different things**, and a single
  `except TransportError:` would let one branch handle both.
- *A refusal means not now; a connection error means unknown.* That is the sentence to memorise. "Not
  now" is a fact about the future. "Unknown" is a gap in the present, and gaps do not close by waiting.
- *Retrying an unknown is safe only when the call is idempotent* — the conditional is the whole part,
  written where the exception is defined so that whoever catches it reads it.

Putting a name on the distinction is not decoration. In real client libraries this is exactly how it
appears: a refusal arrives as an HTTP response object with a status code, and an unavailability arrives
as a transport exception with no response object at all. If your code funnels both into one `except`,
the information is destroyed at the point of catching and no policy downstream can recover it.

### Measuring it

Build the two cases with a counter standing in for an effect. The counter is a list, and appending to
it is the thing that must not happen twice.

```bash
cd days/day-72-backoff-with-honesty/lab
uv run python -c "
from _provider import Clock, Provider, Refused, Unavailable, ladder_wait

credits = []


def top_up(provider, *, ack_lost):
    provider.call()
    credits.append('+100')
    if ack_lost:
        raise Unavailable('connection reset before the receipt came back')
    return 'credited'


def with_retries(provider, *, lose_ack_on=()):
    for attempt in range(1, 5):
        try:
            top_up(provider, ack_lost=attempt in lose_ack_on)
            return attempt
        except (Refused, Unavailable):
            provider.clock.sleep(ladder_wait(attempt))
    return 0


refused_first = Provider(opens_at=3.0, clock=Clock(0.0))
tries_a = with_retries(refused_first)
credits_a = len(credits)

credits.clear()
answering = Provider(opens_at=0.0, clock=Clock(0.0))
tries_b = with_retries(answering, lose_ack_on=(1,))
credits_b = len(credits)

print()
print('  what the retry loop met            attempts   card credited')
for label, tries, applied in (
    ('Refused - 429, never performed', tries_a, credits_a),
    ('Unavailable - verdict unknown', tries_b, credits_b),
):
    print(f'  {label:<35}{tries:>9}{applied:>15}x')
print()
print('  same loop, same ladder, one call charged the customer twice')
"
```

**Line by line:**

- `credits` is the side effect, and it is a list rather than an integer so that the *number of times
  the effect happened* is visible rather than inferred. This is the card being topped up, the ticket
  being closed, the reply being sent.
- Inside `top_up`, `provider.call()` comes **first** and `credits.append` comes second. That ordering
  is the entire model of a refusal: the server gets to say no before anything is done, so a `Refused`
  raised on that first line means the second line never ran.
- `if ack_lost: raise Unavailable(...)` is placed **after** the append, and that placement is the
  model of a lost acknowledgement. The work is done. The world has changed. The only thing that failed
  is the caller finding out.
- `with_retries` catches `(Refused, Unavailable)` in **one** `except` clause on purpose. This is the
  code being criticised: a loop that treats the two identically, which is what almost every retry
  helper does by default.
- `lose_ack_on=(1,)` loses the acknowledgement on the first attempt only, so the second attempt
  succeeds and returns. Losing it every time would only prove that a loop that always fails runs four
  times; losing it once is the realistic case — a blip, followed by a clean success, and a caller who
  sees nothing but the success.
- `refused_first = Provider(opens_at=3.0, ...)` refuses until the clock reaches `t=3`, which the
  ladder's `1 + 2` reaches on the third attempt. `answering = Provider(opens_at=0.0, ...)` is open from
  the start, so nothing in case B is refused at all — the only failure there is the missing reply.
- `provider.clock.sleep(...)` advances the lab's hand-moved clock instead of really waiting, so the
  ladder's timing is exact and the two rows are comparable.
- The final `print` is the finding, not a decoration: the loop, the ladder and the budget are
  **identical** in both rows. Only the kind of failure differs.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
  what the retry loop met            attempts   card credited
  Refused - 429, never performed             3              1x
  Unavailable - verdict unknown              2              2x

  same loop, same ladder, one call charged the customer twice
```

Read the rows against each other. The first row took **more** attempts — three, because it had to wait
out two refusals — and left the world with exactly one credit on the card. The second row took
**fewer** attempts, looked healthier by every metric a dashboard collects, and charged the customer
twice.

That inversion is why this failure survives review. The bad case is the *quiet* one: two attempts,
quick success, no refusals, no escalation, nothing in the error rate. The only trace it leaves is in
the customer's balance.

### Where the answer is supposed to come from

The uncertainty in the second row cannot be removed by better retry logic, and this is where the day
leans on work already done.

Day 60 part
[3.3](../../../day-60-durable-execution/parts/03-doing-it-twice/3.3-the-key-names-the-intention.md)
built the **idempotency key**: a value that names the *intention* rather than the attempt, sent with the
request, so the receiver can recognise a repeat and decline to perform it twice. Its rule was that the
key is stable across attempts, because a key minted per attempt is no better than no key at all.

Day 65 part
[3.4](../../../day-65-kill-it-mid-run/parts/03-four-kills/3.4-killed-inside-the-effect-window.md) then
gave the gap a name — the **effect window**, between an effect happening and the system knowing it
happened — and showed the three possible orderings under a kill: do-then-record, which repeats the
effect; record-then-do, which loses it; and `keyed`, one write that is both, which has no window at all.
It also noted why serious APIs accept an idempotency key on the request in the first place: so that a
caller *can* retry after a lost reply without having to know whether the first attempt landed.

Put together with this part, the production rule is short. **A timeout is only safe to retry when
either the call is naturally idempotent, or you made it idempotent with a key.** There is no third
option, and "we retried and it seemed fine" is not a fourth.

## When it breaks

The failure in the second row does not arrive as a neat `Unavailable`. Here is what it actually looks
like: a server that accepts the connection and then never replies, with the client's own read timeout
firing.

```bash
cd days/day-72-backoff-with-honesty/lab
uv run python -c "
import socket, threading, urllib.request

server = socket.socket()
server.bind(('127.0.0.1', 0))
server.listen(1)
port = server.getsockname()[1]

def accept_and_say_nothing():
    conn, _ = server.accept()
    threading.Event().wait(5)

threading.Thread(target=accept_and_say_nothing, daemon=True).start()
urllib.request.urlopen(f'http://127.0.0.1:{port}/top-up', data=b'card=4471', timeout=2)
"
```

**Line by line:**

- `server.bind(('127.0.0.1', 0))` binds to port `0`, which asks the operating system for any free
  port, so the command never collides with something already running on your machine.
- `accept_and_say_nothing` accepts the connection and then does nothing with it. That is the shape of
  the failure being reproduced: the request **arrived**. A server that refused the connection outright
  would be the safe case, because nothing would have been delivered.
- `data=b'card=4471'` makes it a POST rather than a GET — a write, the kind of call this part is about.
- `timeout=2` is the client's read timeout, and it is what turns a hang into an exception. Without it
  the client waits for the server for ever and there is no error to catch at all, which is a worse
  version of the same problem.

Measured on 2026-09-06, with fourteen frames of `urllib`'s own handler chain elided at the marked
point and nothing else changed:

```traceback
Traceback (most recent call last):
  File "<string>", line 14, in <module>
  File "C:\Users\nisha_gnzw\AppData\Local\Programs\Python\Python312\Lib\urllib\request.py", line 215, in urlopen
    return opener.open(url, data, timeout)
                          [ ... urllib's handler chain ... ]
  File "C:\Users\nisha_gnzw\AppData\Local\Programs\Python\Python312\Lib\http\client.py", line 292, in _read_status
    line = str(self.fp.readline(_MAXLINE + 1), "iso-8859-1")
  File "C:\Users\nisha_gnzw\AppData\Local\Programs\Python\Python312\Lib\socket.py", line 720, in readinto
    return self._sock.recv_into(b)
TimeoutError: timed out
```

Two things in that traceback decide whether you may retry, and neither of them is the exception name.

The frame `_read_status` is the client waiting to read the **first line of the response**. To be
waiting there, it has already opened the connection and written the request body — `card=4471` is on
the wire and, as far as anything on your machine can tell, in the server's hands. The exception is
`TimeoutError: timed out`, and it says nothing whatsoever about what the server did with it.

Contrast the error you get when the same code points at a port with nothing behind it:
`ConnectionRefusedError`. That one is *safe*, because the connection was never established and the
body was never sent — it is a refusal in the sense of this part, delivered by the operating system
instead of by an application. **`ConnectionRefusedError` is closer in meaning to a 429 than it is to
`TimeoutError`**, which is exactly the sort of thing a retry policy written by exception name gets
wrong.

The smallest fix is not in the exception handling at all. It is to send an idempotency key with the
request, so the question the timeout raises becomes one the server can answer.

## In production

**What changes at ten thousand of these.** Timeouts are not rare at scale; they are a steady
background rate. If one call in a thousand times out and a hundred thousand writes go out a day, that
is a hundred uncertain writes daily, every one of them a coin toss between a lost effect and a
duplicated one. At that volume the question stops being *did this one land* and becomes *what is our
duplicate rate*, which is a number you either measure or discover from customers.

**What degrades, and where it shows up.** Duplicates do not surface in the service that made them.
They surface downstream: two replies on one ticket, two rows in a partner's audit export, two charges
on a statement, an analytics count that drifts a percent above reality. By the time anyone traces it
back, the retry loop that caused it has been running correctly for months and is nobody's suspect,
because its own metrics are perfect — remember that the duplicating row in the measurement above used
*fewer* attempts than the safe one.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic.** Load causes timeouts, and timeouts cause
retries, and retries cause load. A provider that slows down under pressure starts timing out clients
that then send the same write again, which adds pressure. This is the herd of part
[4.1](../04-everyone-at-once/4.1-the-herd.md) with duplicated effects attached, and it is why "retry
on timeout" as a blanket default is a load amplifier as well as a correctness bug.

**What a professional does instead of choosing one policy.** They classify the call, once, where it is
defined, and let the policy follow the classification:

| Call | Idempotent? | Policy on a timeout |
| --- | --- | --- |
| read a status, fetch a document, list tickets | yes, naturally | retry freely, on the ladder |
| write with a stable key the server honours | yes, made so | retry freely; the server collapses repeats |
| write with no key — send a reply, close a ticket, charge a card | no | **do not retry**: escalate, and let a human or a reconciliation job decide |

The third row is where this part meets part [3.1](3.1-escalate-never-invent.md). *Do not retry* does
not mean *pretend it worked*, and it does not mean *pretend it failed*. It means raise, carrying the
fact that the outcome is unknown — an escalation that says *we do not know whether this reply was sent*
is genuinely useful, and it is a different message from *this reply was not sent*.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"The retry decorator is on every client method,
including `close_ticket` and `send_reply`, and it catches transport errors. That means any read timeout
on those closes the ticket twice — the request is already on the wire when the read times out, so we
have no idea whether it landed. Two changes: split the exceptions so a refusal with a status code and a
transport failure with no response take different paths, and put an idempotency key on every write, the
way Day 60 did, derived from the run id and the ticket rather than minted per attempt. Until the key is
there, writes escalate on a timeout rather than retrying, and the escalation says 'outcome unknown' in
those words, because that is a different thing for the on-call to do."*

**The interview question:** *"When is it safe to retry a failed request?"* An honest answer: *"When
the request either was definitely not performed, or is idempotent. A 429 or a 503 is an answer from
the server saying it did not do the thing, so retrying costs nothing but a request. A timeout or a
dropped connection is not an answer at all — the body may already be on the wire and the effect may
already have happened, and all you lost is the reply. I measured that with the same retry loop over
two failures: the refusal path took three attempts and credited the card once, and the lost-reply path
took two attempts and credited it twice, which is the uncomfortable part, because the broken run looked
healthier. The fix isn't in the retry code, it's an idempotency key on the write derived from the
intention, so the server can collapse the repeat. Without a key I don't retry non-idempotent writes on
a timeout at all: I escalate and say the outcome is unknown, because 'unknown' and 'failed' are
different things to hand to whoever is on call."*

# 1.3 — The last rung

## One-line answer

A ladder needs two limits that nothing computes for you — **how many attempts** and **how long a
single wait may get** — because uncapped doubling reaches over eight minutes by the tenth rung, and
an uncapped attempt count never escalates at all.

## The story

You open the taxi app outside the station at half eleven at night and press the button. The screen
says *finding you a driver*, with the little circle going round.

At a minute you are still watching it. At two minutes you start looking up the road. At four minutes
you are wondering whether the app has quietly given up and not told you, or whether it is still
genuinely trying, and there is nothing on the screen that distinguishes those.

What you want it to say, at some point, is **no drivers available**. Not because that is good news —
it is bad news — but because it is news. You can walk, or ring somebody, or go back inside. The
spinner is the one outcome you cannot act on.

## The idea in plain language

Part [1.2](1.2-the-ladder-measured.md) measured what a ladder reaches within a budget of six
attempts. This part is about where that six came from, and about the other limit hiding inside
`ladder_wait`.

**Two separate caps, doing two separate jobs.**

The **attempt budget** decides when the loop stops trying. Without it there is no line after the loop
at all, because the loop never ends — and section 3 is entirely about what that line does. A retry
loop with no budget cannot escalate, which means a failure never reaches a person, which means the
spinner goes round forever.

The **delay cap** decides how long a single wait may become. Doubling is not a gentle curve; it is a
cliff you walk off without noticing, because the early rungs look so reasonable:

| Rung | Uncapped wait | Cumulative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 1s | 1s |
| 2 | 2s | 3s |
| 3 | 4s | 7s |
| 4 | 8s | 15s |
| 5 | 16s | 31s |
| 8 | 128s | 255s |
| 10 | 512s | 1023s |

Nobody sits down and decides *"the tenth retry should wait eight and a half minutes"*. It is decided
by the fifth line of a loop somebody wrote in a hurry, and it is why the lab's `ladder_wait` stops
doubling at the end of the tuple and repeats the last rung instead.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because `gate.py`'s third check is exactly this, and it is worth knowing why a gate spends a check on
a function signature. It asserts that `with_backoff` takes `attempts` as an explicit parameter — not
that the number is right, which no check could know, but that **somebody had to choose one**. A
default buried three frames down is a decision nobody made.

And because the alternative failure is the one that does not look like a failure. A run that dies
loudly gets fixed. A run that is still politely retrying at nine the next morning, having slept
through the night in eight-minute increments, looks from the outside exactly like a run that is
working — which is Day 65's whole argument about processes that fail without saying so.

## The mechanism

The cap is one `min` and the comment beside it carries the reasoning:

```python
LADDER = (1.0, 2.0, 4.0, 8.0)


def ladder_wait(attempt: int) -> float:
    """The delay before attempt `attempt`, counting from 1.

    Beyond the ladder's length the last rung repeats rather than doubling forever, because a ladder
    that keeps doubling reaches delays nobody meant to schedule - part 1.3.
    """
    return LADDER[min(attempt, len(LADDER)) - 1]
```

**Line by line:**

- `LADDER` is a **tuple of the actual waits**, not a base and an exponent. That is a deliberate
  choice: a reader can see every value the system will ever wait, and changing the policy means
  editing a list rather than reasoning about a formula. Day 68's argument for a permission table
  being data rather than branches is the same argument.
- `min(attempt, len(LADDER))` is the cap. Past the fourth attempt the index stops advancing, so the
  wait stays at 8s however many attempts follow.
- `- 1` converts a 1-based attempt number to a 0-based index. Counting attempts from 1 rather than 0
  is worth the arithmetic, because every message this day prints — *"gave up after 4 attempts"* — is
  read by a person who counts from one.
- Note what this function does **not** do: it does not know how many attempts there will be. The cap
  on delay and the cap on attempts are separate concerns in separate places, and conflating them is
  how you end up with a policy nobody can state.

Here is what the uncapped version would have done, run rather than asserted:

```bash
cd days/day-72-backoff-with-honesty/lab
uv run python -c "
from _provider import LADDER, ladder_wait

print(f'  {\"rung\":<6}{\"capped\":>10}{\"uncapped\":>12}{\"cumulative\":>14}')
total = 0.0
for attempt in range(1, 11):
    uncapped = 2.0 ** (attempt - 1)
    total += uncapped
    print(f'  {attempt:<6}{ladder_wait(attempt):>9.0f}s{uncapped:>11.0f}s{total:>13.0f}s')
"
```

**Line by line:**

- `ladder_wait(attempt)` is the lab's real function, so the `capped` column is what the code actually
  does rather than a description of it.
- `2.0 ** (attempt - 1)` is the uncapped doubling the same tuple would produce if it never ran out —
  the thing a `delay *= 2` loop computes.
- The `cumulative` column is the one that makes the point: it is how long a caller would have been
  waiting by the time that rung is reached.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
  rung      capped    uncapped    cumulative
  1             1s          1s            1s
  2             2s          2s            3s
  3             4s          4s            7s
  4             8s          8s           15s
  5             8s         16s           31s
  6             8s         32s           63s
  7             8s         64s          127s
  8             8s        128s          255s
  9             8s        256s          511s
  10            8s        512s         1023s
```

The two columns agree for four rows and then separate completely. By the tenth attempt the uncapped
version is waiting **512 seconds** — over eight minutes for one retry — and has been going for
**1023 seconds**, which is seventeen minutes of a caller waiting on a spinner.

The capped version has spent the same ten attempts inside about a minute and a half of waiting. That
is the trade the cap makes: it gives up **reach** past the fourth rung, and in exchange every attempt
after that costs a bounded, predictable amount.

## When it breaks

Capping the delay makes the attempt budget do more work, and that is worth being explicit about
rather than treating the cap as free.

With uncapped doubling, ten attempts reach seventeen minutes. With the cap at 8s, ten attempts reach
about ninety seconds. **If you want to survive a two-minute outage, the capped ladder needs more
attempts than the uncapped one** — so the two caps are not independent knobs, and tightening one
silently weakens the policy unless you loosen the other.

There is a second failure that this file cannot show you, because it is about the wrong shape rather
than the wrong number. Repeating the last rung means the retries become **evenly spaced** once the cap
is reached — 8, 8, 8, 8 — which is flat retry, the strategy part 1.2 measured as worse. So a long
outage puts you back where you started, and the honest description of this ladder is *"exponential for
four attempts, then flat"*. That is a reasonable policy and it is not what "exponential backoff"
suggests to a reader, which is why the tuple is written out in full where somebody will see it.

The usual alternative is to cap the delay at a larger value — sixty seconds is a common choice — and
accept that the first minute of an outage is handled by doubling and everything after it by a
one-minute poll. Whichever you pick, section 3's line is still where the loop ends.

## In production

**What a cap has to be chosen against.** Not a feeling about politeness — the actual distribution of
how long the thing you are calling stays unavailable. Day 70 part 1.4 established that a provider has
at least two windows that fail on completely different timescales: a per-minute limit that clears in
under a minute, and a daily quota that does not clear until midnight. A ladder capped at 8s is well
matched to the first and is simply the wrong tool for the second, where no amount of retrying is the
answer and the only correct behaviour is to stop and escalate.

**What degrades at scale.** Timeouts stack. The caller has its own deadline — a request from a
customer, a queue's visibility timeout, a container's shutdown grace period — and a retry policy that
can run for ninety seconds inside a thirty-second deadline is a policy that will be killed part-way,
producing exactly the half-finished state Day 65's drill was about. The retry budget has to be derived
from the deadline above it, and in most systems it is instead chosen independently and discovered to
conflict during an incident.

**What a professional writes down next to the numbers.** The reasoning, in one line each: *six
attempts because our p99 429 clears in twenty seconds; capped at eight because past that the customer
has gone.* Both numbers will be wrong within a year, and a number with a reason attached can be
re-derived when the reason changes, while a number without one gets doubled by whoever is on call
during the next incident.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"The cap is right and the two limits need to be
visible together. Right now `LADDER` is in one file and the attempt budget is a parameter somewhere
else, so nobody can see that ten attempts under this cap reaches about ninety seconds — which is less
than the queue's visibility timeout, so we'll get killed mid-retry and re-delivered. Put both in one
place with the derivation in a comment, and add the assertion that the total possible wait is under
the deadline."*

**The interview question:** *"You've added exponential backoff. What else does the retry policy need?"*
An honest answer: *"Two caps and a deadline it's derived from. A cap on the delay, because doubling
gets away from you fast — by the tenth attempt an uncapped ladder is waiting over eight minutes for a
single retry, and nobody chose that. And a cap on attempts, because a loop that never exhausts never
escalates, so the failure never reaches a person and from outside it's indistinguishable from working.
Then the honest bit: capping the delay makes the ladder flat after the cap, so 'exponential backoff'
stops describing what it does after the fourth rung, and the total possible wait has to fit inside
whatever deadline is above it — otherwise you get killed mid-retry and redelivered, which is a worse
failure than giving up."*

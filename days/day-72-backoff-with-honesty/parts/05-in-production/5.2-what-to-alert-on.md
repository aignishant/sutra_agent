# 5.2 — What to alert on

## One-line answer

A retry that works reports **no error at all**, so the error rate is the one number guaranteed to stay
flat while the system degrades — the signals are the **escalation rate** and the **wasted-request
count**, and this day's own successful run wasted **4 requests** to produce one answer.

## The story

A night watchman looks after four warehouses. Every morning he leaves a note for the manager.

The note says what he could not deal with: a broken window on the second, a van in the yard that
should not have been there on the fifth. Most mornings the note says *nothing to report*, and the
manager reads that and moves on.

What the note has never once mentioned is that the side gate on warehouse three does not latch. He
found it swinging on his first round eight months ago, pulled it to, and has pulled it to on every
round since — twice a night, four hundred and eighty times. It has never become a thing that happened,
because he has always dealt with it.

He is doing his job. The note is accurate. And the manager has no idea there is a gate to fix.

## The idea in plain language

Retries are the watchman. Their entire purpose is to absorb failures so that nothing downstream sees
one, and they are very good at it — which means **the better your retry policy is, the less your
error rate tells you**.

That produces a specific and quite common blindness:

| What you measure | What it does during a degradation | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| error rate | flat | retries absorbed it |
| success rate | flat | the requests did succeed, eventually |
| p50 latency | flat | most requests never retried |
| p99 latency | rises | the retried ones show up in the tail, mixed with everything else slow |

Only the last row moves, and it moves for a dozen reasons that are not this one, so it prompts an
investigation rather than an answer.

The two numbers that do tell you, and that nothing produces unless you deliberately record them:

**Escalations.** How many calls exhausted their budget and gave up. Part
[3.1](../03-after-the-last-retry/3.1-escalate-never-invent.md) established that this must be an
exception rather than a default — and an exception that is caught and logged as a generic failure has
been rounded back into the error rate, losing the thing that made it informative. An escalation is not
"an error"; it is "we tried N times over T seconds and could not".

**Wasted requests.** How many requests reached a provider and could never have succeeded. This is the
number that moves *first*, because it rises while the retries are still working, and it is the one
number in this list that is invisible in every success/failure framing — the requests succeeded at
being sent and failed at being useful.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because Addendum 02 denominates the budget in requests per day, so a wasted request is not a rounding
error — it is quota spent on nothing. Day 70's router already counts spend per lane; what it cannot
see is how much of that spend was a retry against a window that was never going to open.

And because the gate's fifth check exists for this reason. It asserts that `Escalated` carries
`attempts` and `waited`, and that is not tidiness: an escalation without those fields cannot be
distinguished from any other exception once it reaches a log, which puts it straight back into the
error rate it needed to be kept out of.

## The mechanism

The wasted count is four lines and the definition is the whole of it:

```python
    def wasted(self) -> int:
        """Requests that reached the provider and could never have succeeded."""
        return sum(1 for t in self.attempts if t < self.opens_at)
```

**Line by line:**

- `self.attempts` is appended to in `call()` **before** the refusal check, so a refused request counts
  as having reached the provider. That is Day 70 part 5.1's rule — the provider counts attempts, not
  successes — and it is what makes this number mean anything.
- `t < self.opens_at` is the definition: a request that arrived before the window opened. In the lab
  the provider knows `opens_at` exactly. In production you do not, which is the honest limitation
  handled below.
- It returns a count rather than a rate, because the denominator you want differs by question — per
  call, per minute, per provider — and a helper that picks one has decided for you.

Six red, because `sutra/backoff.py` is the build brief. Two of them are this part's:

- **`with_backoff takes an explicit attempt budget`** — a loop with no budget never escalates, so the
  escalation rate is permanently zero and the watchman never writes the note.
- **`Escalated carries what was tried`** — an escalation carrying `attempts` and `waited` is a distinct,
  countable event; one carrying nothing is an exception that will be logged as a generic failure and
  disappear back into the error rate.

## When it breaks

**`wasted()` is easy in the lab and hard in production**, and the honest version of this part has to
say why. Here the provider's `opens_at` is known, so "could never have succeeded" is decidable. In
production you do not know when the window would have opened, and the closest available proxy is
**refused attempts** — requests that came back 429. That is not the same quantity: a request refused
at the instant before the window opened was wasted, and one refused because the window genuinely had
minutes left was equally wasted, and neither is distinguishable from the outside. Counting refusals is
a good approximation and calling it "wasted" is a small lie worth being aware of.

**And the escalation rate has an interpretation problem at low volume.** Escalations are rare by
design, so the rate is noisy, and alerting on a small absolute count produces pages for a provider
having a normal bad minute. The usual answer is to alert on escalations *as a share of calls* over a
window long enough to be stable, and to alert separately on any single escalation for a call path where
one matters — a payment, a write, anything Day 65's approval gate stands in front of.

**The third failure is the one this whole day has been circling.** If part 3.1's `--invent` branch
exists anywhere in your code, none of this works: the escalation never happens, the exception is never
raised, and the metric that was supposed to be your signal is structurally zero. Monitoring cannot
recover information that the code decided not to produce.

## In production

**What a runbook for a sustained 429 needs to contain.** Not "the API is rate limiting us" — that is
the observation, not the runbook. It needs: which provider and which window (Day 70 part 1.4:
per-minute and per-day fail on different timescales and only one of them clears by waiting); whether
the escalation rate or only the wasted count has moved, because the first means customers are being
told no and the second means they are not yet; whether anything changed on our side, since a deploy
that restarts every worker produces both a herd (part 4.1) and a fresh counter (Day 70 part 5.2); and
what to shed if it continues. A runbook that ends at "wait for it to clear" has not said what to do
with the queue that is building.

**What degrades at scale.** Retry metrics are per-call and the interesting quantity is per-fleet. One
service's escalation rate says nothing about whether the provider is refusing everybody or just you,
and the two have completely different responses. Where a fleet shares a quota — which Day 70 part 5.3
established is the normal case — the wasted count has to be aggregated across workers before it means
anything, and per-worker dashboards will each show a small number that is fine.

**What a professional adds that this lab does not have.** A record of *why* each wait happened, which
parts [2.2](../02-the-number-the-server-gave-you/2.2-when-it-is-absent.md) and
[4.2](../04-everyone-at-once/4.2-one-multiplication.md) both arrived at independently:
`waited=3.4s base=4s source=ladder`. With that field, a shift from `source=header` to `source=ladder`
across a provider is visible as a change in behaviour rather than as an unexplained rise in latency —
and it moves before anything else does, because a provider that stops sending the header has not yet
started refusing you more.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Two things before this ships. Emit escalations as
their own event with the attempt count and total wait — not through the generic error path, because
the whole point is that it's distinguishable from an error. And count refused attempts per provider
per minute; that's our proxy for wasted quota and it's the number that moves first. Don't alert on the
error rate for this service at all: if the retries are working it won't move, and if it does move
we're already past the point I wanted to hear about it."*

**The interview question:** *"Your service retries on failure. What do you monitor?"* An honest answer:
*"Not the error rate — that's the one number a working retry policy guarantees will stay flat, because
absorbing errors is what it's for. I'd watch two things. Escalations: calls that exhausted the budget
and gave up, emitted as their own event with the attempt count and total wait, so they don't get
rounded back into generic errors. And wasted requests — attempts that reached the provider and could
never have succeeded — because that number rises while the retries are still succeeding, so it's the
early signal. I measured two runs that both succeeded and both looked identical on a success metric;
one wasted four requests and the other wasted one. On a quota-metered provider that difference is the
whole story and no default dashboard shows it. The caveat is that 'wasted' is exactly computable in a
test and only approximable in production, where the honest proxy is refused attempts."*

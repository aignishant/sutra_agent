# 4.2 — One multiplication

## One-line answer

Multiplying each wait by a random factor takes the fleet from **6 distinct instants to 123** and the
biggest retry burst from **40 requests to 7** — and leaves the first burst at **40 in both runs**,
because jitter delays a retry and nothing delays a first attempt.

## The story

The lift in the office block is fine all day and impossible at nine.

Not because it is too small. It carries the same people up and down for eight hours without anybody
thinking about it. But everyone's contract says nine, so everyone arrives between five to and five
past, and the lobby is thirty people watching a display count down from eleven.

The building next door has the same lift and no queue, because two of its floors start at half eight
and one starts at half nine. Nobody bought a bigger lift. Somebody moved three start times.

## The idea in plain language

Part [4.1](4.1-the-herd.md) measured the herd: forty clients, six instants, forty requests in each.
The cause was that every client computes the same ladder from the same start, so the fix has to break
one of those two — and the start is not yours to move, because the provider chose it when it refused
everybody at once.

So you break the **sameness of the ladder**. Each client multiplies each of its waits by a random
factor, so client A's rungs land at 0.7, 2.4, 3.6 and client B's at 1.3, 1.8, 5.1, and the fleet
smears across the interval instead of stacking on its edges.

Two things are worth separating, because they are commonly run together and only one of them is what
this part measures.

**Jitter does not reduce load.** The `total requests the provider saw` row is **240 in both runs**.
Every client still makes six attempts. What changes is only *when* they arrive — and that turns out to
be the whole problem, because a rate limiter is a statement about requests per unit time rather than
requests in total.

**And jitter cannot fix the first attempt.** The first request goes out at `t=0` for every client,
because there is no preceding wait to multiply. Whatever synchronised the fleet — a refusal, a deploy,
a window rollover — still lands all of them together, and the measurement below says so plainly rather
than quietly reporting the improved number.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because the build brief asks for jitter in `sutra/backoff.py`, and asks you to pick a range. Picking
one without having seen what the multiplication does is picking by feel, and the two obvious choices
behave differently in a way that shows up in this measurement.

It also closes the loop Day 70 part 4.2 opened. That part said jitter was the standard answer to a
cold lane coming back and did not measure it. **6 → 123 instants and 40 → 7 in the worst retry burst**
is what "the standard answer" is worth on a forty-client fleet, and the residual 40 at `t=0` is what it
is not worth.

## The mechanism

The fix is one line inside the loop from part 4.1:

```python
            wait = ladder_wait(attempt)
            if jitter:
                wait *= 0.5 + rng.random()
            now += wait
```

**Line by line:**

- `ladder_wait(attempt)` is unchanged. Jitter is applied **on top of** the ladder rather than instead
  of it, so the doubling still does its job of reaching further per attempt — part
  [1.2](../01-the-ladder/1.2-the-ladder-measured.md)'s finding survives.
- `0.5 + rng.random()` produces a factor in `[0.5, 1.5)`, so a 4-second rung becomes something between
  2 and 6 seconds. The expected wait is unchanged, which matters: this spreads the fleet without
  systematically making anybody wait longer or shorter on average.
- `rng` is seeded, so the measurement is reproducible. In production it must not be — and it must not
  be seeded **per client from something they share**, which is a real bug: seeding from a hostname or a
  start time can leave two workers generating identical sequences and staying synchronised through the
  jitter.
- The multiplication happens per attempt, not once per client. Multiplying once would shift each
  client's whole ladder by a constant and preserve the spacing, which spreads the fleet at the first
  rung and re-synchronises it at the later ones.

Run it:

```bash
cd days/day-72-backoff-with-honesty/lab
uv run python jitter.py --jitter
```

**Line by line:**

- `--jitter` enables the multiplication. Same forty clients, same ladder, same six attempts each.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
40 clients, window opens at t=20s, jitter on

  distinct instants requests landed at   123
  biggest simultaneous burst             40 requests at t=0.0s
  biggest burst after the first attempt  7 requests at t=0.8s
  total requests the provider saw        240

  the same total load, spread across many more instants.
  note the first row: t=0 is still all of them, because jitter delays a
  retry and nothing delays the first attempt. That burst needs a different fix.
```

Against part 4.1's run:

| | No jitter | With jitter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| distinct instants | 6 | **123** |
| biggest retry burst | 40 | **7** |
| biggest burst overall | 40 at `t=0` | 40 at `t=0` |
| total requests | 240 | 240 |

**The retry bursts are gone** — 40 down to 7, which is the difference between a spike a rate limiter
rejects and a trickle it absorbs. And the total is identical, which is the proof that nothing was
achieved by doing less work.

**The first burst is untouched**, and the output says so rather than letting the headline number
imply otherwise. Forty clients still arrive at `t=0`, because there is no wait before a first attempt
and therefore nothing to multiply. If the thing that synchronised your fleet is a deploy or a window
rollover, jitter has not addressed it at all — it has addressed everything that happens afterwards.

The fixes for a synchronised *first* attempt are different in kind and belong to different parts of a
system: a staggered rollout so workers do not all start together; a random initial delay before the
first request, which is the same trick applied one step earlier; admission control or a queue in front
of the provider, so the fleet's concurrency is bounded by something other than politeness. Each is a
larger change than one multiplication, which is presumably why the multiplication gets done and they
often do not.

## When it breaks

**The range matters and `[0.5, 1.5)` is not the usual recommendation.** The lab's factor keeps the
expected wait equal to the ladder's, which is easy to reason about and spreads modestly. The widely
recommended alternative — often called *full jitter* — picks uniformly from `[0, ladder_wait(attempt)]`,
so a 4-second rung becomes anything from 0 to 4. That spreads much harder and halves the expected wait,
which is a real trade: better spreading, less reach per attempt, and part
[1.2](../01-the-ladder/1.2-the-ladder-measured.md)'s horizon shrinks accordingly. Neither is wrong;
picking one without noticing you were picking is.

**Randomness has to be independent, and it is easy to make it not be.** Two workers that seed from the
same value produce the same sequence and remain a herd of two through every rung — with the added
insult that the trace looks jittered. Seeding from the OS entropy source rather than from anything
about the deployment is the fix, and it is the sort of thing that gets "optimised" into a bug by
somebody making tests deterministic.

**And jitter makes traces harder to read.** After this change, a support question like *"why did this
call take eleven seconds"* has no clean answer beyond "it drew a high factor three times". That is a
genuine cost paid by whoever debugs the system, and it is worth logging the drawn waits for exactly
that reason.

## In production

**Jitter goes in with the retry, not after it.** The follow-up ticket to add jitter is written during
the incident it would have prevented, because the herd is invisible in per-client metrics — part 4.1's
finding — so nothing prompts the work until a provider complains. It costs one line at the time the
retry is written and a postmortem at any other time.

**What degrades at scale.** The spreading is only as good as the interval it spreads across, and the
first rungs are short. At `t=1` the whole fleet is smearing across half a second either way, which for
forty clients is enough and for four thousand is not. Larger fleets need either a wider factor, a
longer first rung, or a concurrency bound in front — and the arithmetic that tells you which is fleet
size against the width of the earliest interval, not a rule of thumb.

**What a professional records.** The drawn wait, per attempt, alongside the source of the base value
that part [2.2](../02-the-number-the-server-gave-you/2.2-when-it-is-absent.md) argued for. `waited=3.4s
base=4s source=ladder` is a line that answers both *why did we wait this long* and *were we obeying the
server*, and neither is reconstructible after the fact from a duration alone.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Good, and please pick the range deliberately —
`0.5 + random()` keeps the expected wait but spreads less than full jitter, and with the fleet size
we're heading to I'd want the wider one. Two things to guard: make sure the RNG isn't seeded from
anything the workers share, because two workers with the same seed stay a herd, and log the drawn wait
next to the base. Also note in the ticket that this doesn't touch the t=0 burst on deploy — that's the
rollout's problem and it's still open."*

**The interview question:** *"How do you stop a thundering herd?"* An honest answer: *"Jitter — multiply
each backoff by a random factor so clients stop computing identical schedules. I measured it on forty
clients: the bare ladder put every request into six instants with forty in each, and jitter took that
to a hundred and twenty-three instants with a worst retry burst of seven. The total request count is
identical, which is the point — you haven't reduced load, you've stopped it arriving in spikes. The
honest caveat is that it doesn't touch the first attempt: there's no preceding wait to multiply, so
whatever synchronised the fleet still lands all of them at once, and fixing that is a staggered rollout
or a concurrency bound rather than a multiplication. And the range is a real choice — full jitter over
the whole interval spreads harder and costs you reach."*

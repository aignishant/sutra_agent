# 4.1 — The herd

## One-line answer

Backoff spreads **one** client out in time and does nothing to spread clients out from **each other**:
forty clients running the identical ladder from the identical start land at **6 distinct instants**,
with all forty arriving together at each one.

## The story

The power comes back at twenty past eight after being off for three hours.

Every fridge in the building starts its compressor. Every air conditioner that was left switched on
comes back on. Two hundred and forty water heaters that were mid-cycle resume. Nobody in the building
did anything — they were all waiting, and the thing they were waiting for happened to all of them at
the same instant.

The building's supply, which carries this load fine on an ordinary evening, trips.

The problem was never the total load. It is the same total load the building draws every night. The
problem is that three hours of waiting collapsed it into one moment.

## The idea in plain language

Sections 1 and 2 looked at one client and one provider. Everything they concluded is correct and
incomplete, because a retry policy is not a property of a client — it is a property of a **fleet**.

Here is the thing that makes it counter-intuitive. Backoff is the mechanism that spreads requests out
in time, so it feels like it should help with crowding. It does, for a single client: the ladder puts
that client's six attempts at `t=0, 1, 3, 7, 15, 23` instead of all in the first second.

But every client computes the **same ladder** from the **same start**. If a provider refuses forty
clients at once — which is exactly what happens when a quota window closes — then all forty compute
`1, 2, 4, 8` and all forty come back at `t=1`, then all forty at `t=3`, then all forty at `t=7`.

The ladder has spread each client's requests out and left the fleet perfectly synchronised. Worse, the
refusal itself is what synchronised them: they were not aligned before the provider refused them
together.

There is a second-order effect that makes it self-sustaining. Forty simultaneous requests are more
likely to be refused than one, so the herd's next attempt is also refused together, so it re-forms
for the next rung. The pattern does not decay on its own.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because Day 70 part 4.2 named exactly this and stopped short of measuring it. That part was about a
cold lane coming back, and it observed that every waiting request tries it at once and that jitter is
the standard answer. What it did not do is put a number on the burst, and a number is what makes the
next part's one-line fix arguable rather than a slogan.

It also matters for a Sutra-shaped reason. The desk is one process today, so "forty clients" sounds
hypothetical — but Day 70 part 6.1 already showed the router replicated across workers, and every one
of those workers runs this ladder. Forty is not a thought experiment; it is a worker pool.

## The mechanism

The simulation is deliberately the simplest thing that can show the effect — no provider, no
refusals, just where each client's requests **land**:

```python
def arrivals(clients: int, *, jitter: bool, seed: int = 7) -> Counter[float]:
    """When each client's requests land, rounded to a tenth of a second."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    landings: Counter[float] = Counter()
    for _ in range(clients):
        now = 0.0
        for attempt in range(1, MAX_ATTEMPTS + 1):
            landings[round(now, 1)] += 1
            if now >= OPENS_AT:
                break
            wait = ladder_wait(attempt)
            if jitter:
                wait *= 0.5 + rng.random()
            now += wait
    return landings
```

**Line by line:**

- `now = 0.0` **inside** the client loop is the assumption that produces the herd, and it is the
  realistic one: every client starts its ladder at the moment it was refused, and they were all
  refused together.
- `landings[round(now, 1)] += 1` counts arrivals per instant rather than per client. The question is
  not *what did each client do* — they all did the same thing — it is *what did the provider see*.
- `ladder_wait(attempt)` is section 1's function, unmodified. Nothing about the ladder is wrong here;
  the ladder is doing exactly what it was designed to do.
- The `if jitter` line is the whole of part [4.2](4.2-one-multiplication.md) and is inert in this run.
- `round(now, 1)` buckets to a tenth of a second. That is a generous definition of "simultaneous" —
  real requests arriving within 100ms of each other are, from a rate limiter's point of view, together.

**Six instants.** Two hundred and forty requests arrived at six moments, forty at a time, and the
gaps between those moments are 1, 2, 4 and 8 seconds — the ladder, visible in the arrival pattern of
the whole fleet.

Look at what the ladder achieved and did not achieve. Each individual client behaved impeccably: it
waited, it doubled, it made six requests over twenty-three seconds. The **fleet** made forty
simultaneous requests four times over. Every property you wanted from backoff is present at the level
you measured it and absent at the level the provider experiences.

And note the `total requests the provider saw` row is **240 in this run and 240 in the next one**. The
next part does not reduce load at all. It only changes when the load arrives, which turns out to be the
entire problem.

## When it breaks

The lab is generous to the herd in three ways, and each one makes the real case worse rather than
better.

**The lab has no feedback.** Here a request lands and is counted. In reality forty simultaneous
requests against a rate-limited provider are more likely to be refused than a single one, so the herd's
members stay synchronised through the next rung too, and the pattern reinforces itself rather than
decaying.

**The lab assumes one refusal event.** Real fleets are refused repeatedly, so a herd forms, breaks up
slightly as some members succeed, and re-forms on the next window closure — meaning the burst is a
recurring feature rather than a single incident.

**And forty is small.** The effect does not change shape with scale; it changes magnitude. The same
run with four hundred clients produces the same six instants with four hundred requests in each, which
is the difference between a provider absorbing a burst and a provider having an incident.

One thing that is genuinely not the herd's fault: the `t=0.0` burst. All forty clients arrive at zero
because they were all refused at zero — that is the *cause* of the herd, not an effect of the ladder,
and part [4.2](4.2-one-multiplication.md) is careful about which of the two its fix addresses.

## In production

**Where this actually bites.** Not on a normal afternoon. It bites at the moment a shared resource
becomes available again — a quota window rolling over at midnight, a provider recovering from an
outage, a deploy restarting every worker at once (Day 70 part 6.1's finding), a cache expiring on a
fixed schedule. Every one of those is an event that happens to the whole fleet simultaneously, which
is precisely the starting gun this measurement assumes.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic.** The herd is invisible in per-client metrics
and obvious in provider-side ones. Each client's dashboard shows six well-spaced requests over
twenty-three seconds and a policy behaving correctly; the provider sees four spikes of forty. Teams
therefore find this from the *outside* — a vendor's rate-limit complaint, a support ticket — rather
than from their own monitoring, because their own monitoring is aggregated per client and averages the
spikes away.

**What a professional checks before adding retries at all.** Whether the thing being retried is shared.
A retry policy against a per-tenant resource is a local decision; a retry policy against a shared quota
is a fleet-wide one, and Day 70 part 5.3 already established that free tiers meter per project rather
than per process. If the resource is shared, the retry policy needs jitter before it ships, not after
the first incident.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"The per-client behaviour is right and I want a
fleet-level view before this goes out. We run this in a worker pool and they all restart together on
deploy, so every worker's ladder starts at the same instant — that's forty simultaneous requests four
times in the first twenty-three seconds. Add jitter in the same change rather than as a follow-up,
because the follow-up is what we'll be writing during the incident."*

**The interview question:** *"You added exponential backoff and the provider still complains about
bursts. Why?"* An honest answer: *"Because backoff spreads one client's requests out in time and does
nothing to spread clients apart. They all compute the same ladder from the same start, and the thing
that started them was usually the provider refusing all of them at once — so the refusal synchronises
the fleet and the ladder preserves the synchronisation. I measured it: forty clients on a 1-2-4-8
ladder landed at six distinct instants, forty requests in each. The per-client metrics look perfect,
which is why people find this from the vendor rather than from their own dashboards. The fix is jitter,
and it's one multiplication — but it fixes the retries, not the initial burst, which needs something
else."*

Then work out on paper why the six instants are at `t=0, 1, 3, 7, 15, 23` and not at `1, 2, 4, 8, 8,
8`. The distinction is the difference between a wait and a cumulative time, and getting it wrong is
how people mis-read this kind of trace.

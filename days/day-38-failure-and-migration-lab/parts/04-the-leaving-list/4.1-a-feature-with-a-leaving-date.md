# 4.1 — A feature with a leaving date

## One-line answer

Since the 2026-07-28 revision, an MCP feature does not simply disappear one day: it enters a
**Deprecated** state with a published migration path and a minimum twelve-month window before it is
even eligible for removal, and every such feature is listed on one page you can read.

## The story

The newspaper stops coming next month.

Not without warning. The boy who brings it hands over a printed slip with the paper one morning: the
delivery round is closing at the end of March, here is the date, and here is the number of the agency
two streets over that will take over if you ring them.

You do not have to do anything today. The paper still comes tomorrow and it comes every day until the
end of March, exactly as before. What you have been given is not an interruption; it is a **date**,
and the name of the thing that replaces it.

Compare that with the shop on the corner that closed. One Tuesday the shutter was down and it stayed
down, and for a fortnight everyone stood in front of it wondering whether it was a holiday. No date,
no note, no successor. The same loss, and it cost every single person on that street a separate
afternoon of finding out.

## The idea in plain language

A **lifecycle policy** is a written rule about how a feature is allowed to disappear. MCP adopted one
in the 2026-07-28 revision, under proposal SEP-2596, and it is short enough to hold in your head.

Every feature of the core specification is in exactly one of three states:

| State | What it means | What you should do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Active** | It is part of the current revision. | Implement it normally. |
| **Deprecated** | It is still fully in the specification and still works, but it is scheduled to go. | New code should not adopt it. Existing code should migrate before the earliest removal. |
| **Removed** | It is gone from the draft and will be absent from the next current revision. | Code targeting that revision must not depend on it. |

Three properties of the Deprecated state do the real work.

**It is fully functional.** Deprecated does not mean broken, disabled, or warned about at runtime. A
deprecated feature behaves exactly as it always did. The only thing that has changed is its future.

**There is a minimum window, and it is at least twelve months.** The policy says the window is
*"measured from the release of the specification revision in which the feature is first marked
Deprecated, not from the date the SEP reaches Final"*, and the feature becomes eligible for removal in
the first revision released as current on or after that window elapses. There is one exception, for a
feature with a live security problem, and even that must leave at least ninety days.

**The migration path is part of the deprecation, not an afterthought.** The policy requires the
deprecating proposal to *"document the migration path, or state explicitly that none is required"*,
and — this is the part that makes it usable — *"if the migration path names a replacement feature,
that feature must be Active in the revision in which the deprecation takes effect"*. You are never
told to leave without being told where to go, and the destination exists before the departure is
announced.

There is also one page listing everything currently in the Deprecated state. The specification calls
it *"the canonical answer to 'what is on its way out, and by when'"*, and its existence is the
difference between the newspaper slip and the shutter.

Two words that are easy to blur, because the difference decides what you have to do:

- **Eligible for removal** is a date. After it, the maintainers *may* remove the feature in some
  future revision. The registry is careful about this: the earliest removal for the three features
  deprecated in this revision is written as *"First revision released on or after 2027-07-28"*, not
  as "2027-07-28". A revision released later than that date is the one that may drop it.
- **Removed** is an event. It has happened or it has not. As of this writing the policy's Removed
  section says *"No features have been removed under this policy yet."*

## Why Sutra needs it

Sutra will read other people's servers for the rest of the curriculum.
[Day 40](../../../day-40-filtering-and-allowlists/LESSON.md) introduces third-party servers behind an
allowlist and [Day 45](../../../day-45-the-mcp-audit/LESSON.md) audits everything Phase 5 and 6
built. Both of those need a way to answer *"is anything here on its way out?"* that is a lookup rather
than an opinion.

There is also a direct effect on how this repository is allowed to change. Principle 14 says that if
reality moves, the plan is amended before any code is written. A lifecycle policy with a published
window is what makes that principle affordable: you are not reacting to a surprise, you are reading a
schedule that was posted a year in advance. The addendum this whole phase implements
(`docs/01_MASTER_PLAN_ADDENDUM_GAPS.md`, Part 2) exists because a revision moved; the registry is
what stops the next move being a surprise.

And it settles a design question for `sutra_mcp`. If a feature is Deprecated, Sutra should not adopt
it — but Sutra must still **recognise** it, because a partner's server built two years ago will
declare it. Recognising without adopting is the whole of MCP-31, and
[4.3](4.3-dating-somebody-elses-server.md) turns it into a script with an exit code.

## The mechanism

> 🎬 **The scene:** you are choosing how a new MCP server should let a client adjust its log verbosity,
> and you find a specification page describing `logging/setLevel`. It is on the official site, it is
> fully documented, and it works.
>
> 😬 **The naive fix:** implement it. It is in the specification, and being in the specification has
> always been the test.
>
> 💥 **Why it fails:** being in the specification is no longer sufficient, because the specification
> now has two kinds of "in". The feature you just adopted is on a published schedule to leave, so you
> have written new code that already has a migration in front of it — and the migration exists today,
> which means you skipped a working replacement to build on something scheduled to go.
>
> 💡 **The insight:** the question is not *"is this in the specification?"* any more. It is *"what
> state is this in?"* — and there is one page that answers it.

The registry is a table, so read it as one. Each row is copied from
`https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2026-07-28/deprecated`, read on 2026-09-04:

```python
# days/day-38-failure-and-migration-lab/lab/lifecycle.py
@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Feature:
    """One row of the registry: the feature, when it was deprecated, what replaces it."""

    name: str
    sep: str
    deprecated_in: date
    earliest_removal: date | None  # None where the registry gives a rule, not a date
    migration: str


REGISTRY = [
    Feature(
        "Roots",
        "SEP-2577",
        date(2026, 7, 28),
        date(2027, 7, 28),
        "pass directories or files via tool parameters, resource URIs, or server configuration",
    ),
    ...
    Feature(
        "HTTP+SSE transport",
        "SEP-2596",
        date(2025, 3, 26),
        None,  # "Three months after SEP-2596 reaches Final"
        "Streamable HTTP",
    ),
]
```

**Line by line:**

- `@dataclass(frozen=True)` because a registry row is a fact, not a variable. Frozen means an attempt
  to edit one raises rather than quietly succeeding, which is the right behaviour for something whose
  only job is to reproduce what a published page says.
- `deprecated_in` is a `date` rather than a string, so the arithmetic below is arithmetic. It is also
  the **revision** identifier — MCP revisions are dates — so one field carries both meanings honestly.
- `earliest_removal: date | None` is the field that made this worth writing as code. Two of the six
  rows do not have a date at all: the registry gives a **rule** instead — *"Follows Sampling"* for
  the `includeContext` values, and *"Three months after SEP-2596 reaches Final"* for the HTTP+SSE
  transport. Forcing those into a date would be inventing a fact, so the type says `None` and the
  comment beside it quotes the rule verbatim.
- Even where there *is* a date, it is a simplification the part has to declare: the registry says
  *"First revision released on or after 2027-07-28"*. The date is the floor, not the event.
- `sep` records which numbered proposal did it, because that is how you find the reasoning. `SEP-2577`
  deprecated three features at once; `SEP-2596` is the policy itself and also reclassified two older
  soft-deprecations under it.
- `migration` is a sentence rather than a symbol because that is what the registry gives you, and
  because two of the three migrations are not a replacement API at all — they are *"go and do this
  somewhere else"*.
- The list is a module-level constant, so [4.3](4.3-dating-somebody-elses-server.md) can import it and
  the migration text lives in exactly one place.

The arithmetic on top of it is deliberately small:

```python
TODAY = date(2026, 9, 4)  # the day the registry was read; change it and re-run

SUTRA_USES = {"Roots": False, "Sampling": False, "Logging": False, "HTTP+SSE transport": False}


def days_left(feature: Feature) -> str:
    """Days from TODAY until the feature is *eligible* for removal, as a string."""
    if feature.earliest_removal is None:
        return "rule, not a date"
    return f"{(feature.earliest_removal - TODAY).days} days"
```

**Line by line:**

- `TODAY` is a constant, not `date.today()`. That is on purpose: the transcript in this document must
  be reproducible, and a script whose output changes every day cannot be checked against a document.
  The comment tells you to change it, which is the honest version of "this is a snapshot".
- `SUTRA_USES` is the only opinion in the file. Everything else is copied; this is decided. It is
  written as data so that the answer to *"do we use this?"* is a line somebody edited deliberately
  rather than a belief somebody holds.
- `days_left` returns a **string**, including the string `"rule, not a date"`. A function that
  returned `None` or a sentinel integer would push the two-kinds-of-answer problem onto every caller;
  returning the honest text keeps the ambiguity in one place, where it is visible in the output.
- The subtraction of two `date` objects gives a `timedelta`, and `.days` is the whole-day count. No
  time zones are involved because a specification revision is a date, not a moment.

Run it:

```bash
cd days/day-38-failure-and-migration-lab/lab
uv run python lifecycle.py
```

**Line by line:**

- No arguments, no network, no configuration. The registry is in the file. **Zero model calls.**

Measured on 2026-09-04:

```text
registry read on 2026-09-04; specification revision 2026-07-28

feature                                SEP        deprecated   eligible in        used by Sutra
Roots                                  SEP-2577   2026-07-28   327 days           no
Sampling                               SEP-2577   2026-07-28   327 days           no
Logging                                SEP-2577   2026-07-28   327 days           no
Dynamic Client Registration            PR #2858   2026-07-28   327 days           -

  (none today - the audit is the point, not the answer)
```

`327 days` is the number that makes the policy real. It is not "soon" and it is not "eventually"; it
is a figure you can put in a plan next to other figures. And `features Sutra must migrate: 0` is the
right answer for the right reason: Sutra has not adopted any of these, because Phase 5 was written
after the deprecation, not before it.

Note the fourth row. Dynamic Client Registration was deprecated by a plain pull request rather than a
numbered proposal, and Day 37's auth work is the day that meets its replacement. It is on this list
too, which is the point of having one list.

## When it breaks

The policy breaks the assumption people carry from other ecosystems, which is that *deprecated* means
*emits a warning*. It does not, and confusing the two produces a specific, expensive mistake: a team
decides that because nothing is warning, nothing is deprecated.

There is no error text for this, because the failure is a silence. The closest thing to evidence is
the absence in [6.2](../06-in-production/6.2-the-deprecation-your-library-never-mentions.md), where
the installed SDK is asked directly whether it will tell you, and says no.

A second break is date arithmetic against the wrong thing. Write `(2027-07-28) - today` and you have
computed the time until a feature is **eligible** for removal, then talk about it as the time until it
*is* removed, and you will plan a migration for a deadline that does not exist. The specification is
explicit that removal *"is a Core Maintainer decision taken during release preparation and may happen
later"*, and it adds that *"features may remain Deprecated, without removal, for much longer than the
minimum deprecation window"*. The date is a floor.

The third is treating the registry as normative. It says of itself that it is *"a derived view kept
consistent with the per-feature deprecation notices and changelog entries, which are the normative
records"*. For a decision that matters, follow the row to the feature's own page.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead:** a dated snapshot of the registry, in the repository, checked
in a scheduled job against the live page. Not a note in a design document and not a person's memory —
a file with a fetch date, so that "has anything moved?" is a diff. That is the same discipline
`docs/PACKAGES.md` applies to versions and `docs/PAPERS.md` to citations, applied to a third axis.

**What degrades at scale:** the number of protocols. One lifecycle registry is a page you read once a
quarter. A platform team depending on a dozen specifications, each with its own deprecation policy and
its own page, has a genuine tracking problem, and the teams that survive it are the ones that turned
the reading into a job with an owner and a cadence rather than an act of diligence.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic:** the long-lived client. A desktop
application, an embedded agent, a partner integration that shipped once — those keep running against a
revision nobody is maintaining. Twelve months is generous for a service you deploy weekly and short
for a binary somebody installed and forgot, and the removal lands as a total outage for exactly the
users least able to update.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"before we implement this, which lifecycle state is
it in? Paste the row from the deprecated registry with the date you read it. If it says Deprecated,
we implement the migration path instead, and the reason goes in the ADR."*

**The interview question:** *"how do you decide whether to build on a protocol feature?"* An answer
that shows the habit: *"I check its state, not just whether it is documented. Since the 2026-07-28
revision MCP has a formal lifecycle — Active, Deprecated, Removed — with a minimum twelve-month window
before a deprecated feature is even eligible for removal, and one registry page listing everything
currently on the way out with its migration path. So the check is a lookup, not a judgement. The
detail I would flag is that eligible-for-removal is a floor, not a deadline: the registry says
'first revision released on or after' a date, and features can sit Deprecated for much longer. What it
buys you is that a removal becomes a scheduled event you can plan against instead of a surprise
someone finds by getting a 404."*

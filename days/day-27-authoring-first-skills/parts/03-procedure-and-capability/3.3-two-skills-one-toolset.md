# 3.3 — Two skills, one toolset, no overlap

## One-line answer

Both skills go on one shelf and one toolset, they can both be active in the same conversation, and the
thing that keeps them from competing is that their descriptions share **four** content words out of
forty-seven — which is a number you check rather than a property you hope for.

## The story

Two people were told to book the hall.

The programme secretary was told on Tuesday, and the treasurer was told on Wednesday by somebody who
had forgotten Tuesday. Both of them did it properly: rang the caretaker, wrote it in the diary,
confirmed by email. The hall has one booking, because the caretaker recognised the second call and
said so.

What did not happen is the thing everybody assumed had happened: nobody checked. The overlap was
caught by the caretaker, once, by luck. And the next time it will be a different hall with a different
caretaker, and the committee will find out at seven o'clock on the evening in question.

## The idea in plain language

Sutra now has two skills on one shelf. `load_shelf` picks up both, one `SkillToolset` holds both, and
one agent gets both ([3.2](3.2-honouring-the-contract.md)). Nothing about the wiring needed to change,
which is the good news.

The interesting question is what happens when the model has to choose. Three facts settle most of it:

**Both can be active at once.** The activation register is a **list**, appended to, never shrinking
([26.3.3](../../../day-26-loading-skills-into-adk/parts/03-wiring-the-toolset/3.3-activation-is-a-line-in-state.md)).
A conversation that triages a ticket and then drafts the reply activates both, and both bodies are in
the context from that point on.

**Both are in the index together.** One `list_skills` call returns both cards, in one XML block
([26.2.2](../../../day-26-loading-skills-into-adk/parts/02-the-four-tools/2.2-the-index-is-a-tool-call.md)),
and the model chooses between them by reading the descriptions.

**Only one has tools.** `ticket-triage` declares `lookup_ticket` and `search_kb`; `kb-answer-style`
declares nothing, because writing a reply needs no capability beyond what the model already has. So
activating the style skill adds a procedure and no tools, which is a perfectly normal shape and worth
seeing once.

That leaves the risk [1.2](../01-extraction/1.2-one-job-four-words.md) named: two descriptions that
both match the same request. The defence is not a rule, it is a measurement. Strip the stopwords from
both descriptions and count what is left in common:

| | count |
| --- | --- |
| content words unique to `ticket-triage` | 25 |
| content words unique to `kb-answer-style` | 18 |
| **shared** | **4** — `asks`, `base`, `id`, `knowledge` |

Four out of forty-seven, and all four are incidental: both descriptions mention the knowledge base
because both jobs touch it. Neither *"triage"*, *"severity"*, *"priority"* nor *"urgent"* appears in the
style skill, and neither *"draft"*, *"rewrite"*, *"reply"* nor *"word"* appears in the triage one.

That is what a clean split looks like as a number, and it takes ten lines to compute. Day 28 turns it
into a design tool; today it is a check on work already done.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because the split from [1.2](../01-extraction/1.2-one-job-four-words.md) was an argument, and an
argument is not evidence.

The reasoning was sound — different moment, different owner, independently wanted — and it would have
been just as persuasive if it were wrong. The overlap count is the first thing in this whole day that
could have come back and said *"these two are the same skill"*. It did not, which is worth knowing
rather than assuming.

There is also a sequencing question that only appears once there are two. `ticket-triage`'s step 5 says
*"explain the cause using only what the article says"*, and `kb-answer-style` says how to write that
explanation to a customer. Those are adjacent and they must not become the same instruction written
twice ([2.1](../02-what-leaves-the-prompt/2.1-one-fact-one-home.md)). The rule that keeps them apart is
audience: triage answers **the engineer**, style answers **the reporter**. Every sentence in either body
should be checkable against that.

## The mechanism

The second skill's body, written to the same six-question shape as the first
([1.3](../01-extraction/1.3-the-six-question-pass.md)):

```markdown
# KB answer style

Write a reply to the reporter that is grounded in one knowledge-base article and cites it.

## Steps

1. Open with the reporter's own words for the problem, in one line, so they know you read it.
2. Give the cause in one plain sentence. No jargon in the first sentence.
3. Name the article id the cause came from, in the form `KB-104`, in the same paragraph as the cause.
4. Give the fix as an instruction the reporter can carry out, or say who will do it and when.
5. Close with one sentence offering the next step. Do not apologise more than once.

## Edge cases

- If there is no article, say what is known and what is not, and say a person will follow up. Never
  explain a cause you cannot cite.
- If the article's fix needs access the reporter does not have, say who will do it instead of telling
  them to do it.
- Do not name a colleague. "The platform team" and "a support engineer" are the only people you name.
- Do not promise a time unless the engineer gave you one.
```

**Line by line:**

- The scope line is the one from [1.2](../01-extraction/1.2-one-job-four-words.md), and it says
  **reporter**. Every step below is addressed to that reader, which is the boundary against
  `ticket-triage` made operational rather than merely stated.
- Step 1 is *"the reporter's own words"* — the opposite of `ticket-triage`'s step 2, which insists on a
  one-word symptom for the keyword search. Same conversation, two different treatments of the same
  text, for two different purposes. Two skills, and no contradiction, precisely because each says which
  job it is doing.
- Step 3 gives the citation **format** (`KB-104`) rather than asking for "a citation". A model told to
  cite will invent a plausible format; a model shown one uses it, and a consistent format is greppable
  in a support inbox later.
- Step 5's *"do not apologise more than once"* is the kind of house rule that only exists because
  somebody read fifty replies. It is one line and it is worth more than a paragraph about tone.
- Four edge cases, and the first is the same rule as `ticket-triage`'s step 4 seen from the other
  side: triage says *do not explain a cause you cannot cite*, style says *say what is known and what is
  not*. Related, not duplicated — one is a prohibition on the diagnosis, the other is a shape for the
  reply.
- **No `adk_additional_tools`.** This procedure requires no tools, so it declares none, and the lint
  from [3.1](3.1-a-named-tool-is-a-contract.md) reports nothing for it. A skill with no tool
  requirement is the normal case, not a deficiency.
- The worked example is in the real file and omitted here for length; it is a three-paragraph reply for
  ticket 4521 built on KB-104, so that the two skills' examples describe **the same case** from the two
  audiences. That is deliberate: a reader who has seen the triage example recognises the ticket.

Now measure both, and the overlap:

```python
# days/day-27-authoring-first-skills/lab/two_skills.py
"""Both skills' sizes, the shared index, and how much their descriptions overlap."""

import os
import pathlib
import re

from google import genai
from google.adk.skills import load_skills_from_dir, prompt

from sutra.config import load_env

MODEL = "gemini-3.7-flash"
STOP = set(
    "a an and are as at be by for from has have how in is it its of on or that the to use "
    "used when where which with someone something you your do does not no".split()
)

load_env()
client = genai.Client(api_key=os.environ["GOOGLE_API_KEY"])


def cost(text: str) -> int:
    """Tokens for one string. Costs 0 generations."""
    return client.models.count_tokens(model=MODEL, contents=text).total_tokens


def content_words(text: str) -> set[str]:
    """Lowercase words with the stopwords removed."""
    return set(re.findall(r"[a-z]+", text.lower())) - STOP


skills = sorted(load_skills_from_dir(pathlib.Path("skills")), key=lambda s: s.name)
for skill in skills:
    print(
        f"{skill.name:18} body {len(skill.instructions):>5} chars  {cost(skill.instructions):>4} tokens"
    )

print("index, both      :", cost(prompt.format_skills_as_xml(skills)))

first, second = (content_words(s.frontmatter.description) for s in skills)
print("shared words     :", sorted(first & second))
print("unique           :", len(first - second), "/", len(second - first))
```

**Line by line:**

- `STOP` is a hand-written list rather than a library, because adding a dependency to remove forty
  common words would be exactly the anti-pattern this repository warns about. It is also visible, which
  matters: a stopword list decides what the overlap number means.
- `content_words` returns a **set**, so the comparison is about vocabulary rather than frequency. Two
  descriptions that both use *"ticket"* five times overlap on one word, which is the right accounting
  for a selection problem.
- `prompt.format_skills_as_xml(skills)` is the same function `list_skills` calls
  ([26.2.2](../../../day-26-loading-skills-into-adk/parts/02-the-four-tools/2.2-the-index-is-a-tool-call.md)),
  so the index figure is what the model will actually receive rather than an estimate.
- `sorted(..., key=lambda s: s.name)` because `load_skills_from_dir` returns filesystem order, and a
  number you are going to compare between runs needs a stable order.
- **Zero generations**; `count_tokens` is the separate endpoint.


worth knowing before Day 28 argues about how to reduce it.

## When it breaks

**Two descriptions that overlap on the words that matter.** The count above is only useful if you read
*which* words are shared, not just how many. Four shared stopword-ish nouns is fine; four shared
**trigger** words — `severity`, `urgent`, `priority`, `triage` — would be the same skill twice, and the
model would pick between them by coin flip.

**A rule written into both bodies.** The temptation is real: *"never explain a cause you cannot cite"*
belongs in both, because both jobs can do it. Sutra's answer is that it is written once as a
prohibition in `ticket-triage` and once as a **reply shape** in `kb-answer-style`, and the two
sentences are different sentences. If you find yourself pasting a line from one body into the other,
stop: it belongs in the instruction, which is the tier that always applies
([2.2](../02-what-leaves-the-prompt/2.2-what-never-leaves-code.md)).

**Both activated when only one was needed.** Cheap in tokens and expensive in requests: two
activations is two extra round trips on a budget of twenty
([26.5.1](../../../day-26-loading-skills-into-adk/parts/05-in-production/5.1-what-a-skill-really-costs-here.md)).
It is not an error, and it is not always waste — a triage that ends in a drafted reply legitimately
needs both. Watch the ratio rather than the event.

**A third skill that quietly overlaps both.** This is the failure that arrives in month three, and the
only defence that scales is running the overlap check between the **new** skill and every existing one
before it merges. Two skills is one comparison; ten is forty-five, which is a job for a script and not
for a reviewer.

## In production

**Compute the overlap on every added skill, and keep the matrix.** It is a few lines and it answers
the review question nobody can answer by reading: *which existing skill does this one compete with?*
The number to watch is the maximum pairwise overlap, and a new skill that scores high against an
existing one is either a section of it or a rename of it.

**Keep the shape of every body identical.** Same headings, same order, every time. It is worth the
small tyranny: a reviewer reading their fortieth skill can skip to the section they care about, and a
missing section becomes visible instead of merely absent. The six-question pass
([1.3](../01-extraction/1.3-the-six-question-pass.md)) is what produces the shape.

**Distinguish "related" from "duplicated" explicitly in review.** Two skills touching the same domain
will always have adjacent rules, and the correct outcome is adjacent sentences that say different
things from different angles. The review question is not *"do these overlap?"* — of course they do —
but *"could I delete one sentence and lose nothing?"*

**What changes at scale** is that the index becomes the product. With two skills the model reads both
cards and picks correctly almost always; with fifty it is doing information retrieval over your
descriptions, and the quality of that retrieval is the quality of your writing. That is when a registry
with a search tool stops being exotic
([26.2.2](../../../day-26-loading-skills-into-adk/parts/02-the-four-tools/2.2-the-index-is-a-tool-call.md)),
and it is also when a consistent description format stops being tidiness and starts being function.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"these two skills share five trigger words including
'severity'. Run them past the overlap check — if a request mentioning severity matches both, the model
picks one at random and we get half a procedure. Either sharpen the second description or admit this is
one skill."*

**The interview question:** *"how do you stop two skills from competing?"* The answer that shows you
measured rather than trusted: *"by checking, not by hoping. I strip stopwords from both descriptions
and look at the shared vocabulary — ours share four words out of forty-seven, and none of them is a
trigger word, so a request about severity matches one and a request about drafting a reply matches the
other. What I actually read is which words are shared, not how many: four incidental nouns is fine and
four shared trigger words is one skill written twice. With more than a handful of skills that check has
to run on every addition, because it is the question nobody can answer by reading."*

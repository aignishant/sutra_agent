# 1.2 — One job, four words: choosing a skill's scope

## One-line answer

A skill's scope is decided by whether you can name it in four words or fewer without using "and" — and
Sutra's inventory turns out to be **two** skills, not one, because triaging a ticket and writing the
reply are different jobs done at different moments.

## The story

The shop at the end of the road has a board above it with seven things on it.

Photocopy. Lamination. Passport photos. Mobile recharge. Courier. Cold drinks. And, in smaller letters
squeezed at the end because they ran out of board, *"Xerox also."*

It is a good shop. All seven things happen there and all seven are done properly. But if somebody asks
you *"what is that shop?"*, you do not have an answer — you have a list. And when you actually need a
passport photo you do not think of it, because the sign taught you nothing you could remember.

The place two doors down has one word on it: TAILOR. You have never been inside. You know exactly when
you will go.

## The idea in plain language

A skill is chosen by a model reading its name and description
([26.2.2](../../../day-26-loading-skills-into-adk/parts/02-the-four-tools/2.2-the-index-is-a-tool-call.md)),
so scope is not a filing decision. It is the thing that decides whether the skill is ever selected.

The test is small and it works: **name the job in four words or fewer, with no "and".**

- `ticket-triage` — three words, one job. Passes.
- `kb-answer-style` — three words, one job. Passes.
- `ticket-triage-and-reply-writing` — the "and" is the tell. Two jobs.
- `support-desk-helper` — three words, but the job is unnameable. Fails a different way: it is a
  category, not a job.

The reason "and" is fatal is mechanical rather than aesthetic. The description is the selection
interface, and a description covering two jobs has to describe both — which halves the word overlap
with any single request. Day 25 measured that overlap and found the good description scoring seven
against realistic requests where a vague one scored one
([25.2.3](../../../day-25-skills-the-open-spec/parts/02-the-frontmatter/2.3-the-description-field.md)).
A two-job description does not score zero; it scores middling for everything, which is worse than
scoring well for one thing.

There is a second test that catches what the first misses. **Do the two halves happen at the same
moment?** Sutra's procedure looks like one flow — look up, search, classify, draft — but the last verb
is a different kind of work:

| | triage | reply writing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| when | a ticket arrives with no severity | a reply is about to be sent |
| who asks | the support engineer | the support engineer, or a different agent |
| what it touches | tools, the KB, the rubric | tone, structure, citations |
| how often it changes | when the rubric changes | when the house voice changes |
| who owns it | support leadership | whoever owns customer communication |

Five rows and they disagree on all five. That is two skills.

And a third test, which is the one that stops you splitting forever: **would either half ever be
wanted without the other?** Yes — a reply to a ticket that was triaged last week still needs the house
style, and a triage that ends in an escalation rather than a reply still needs the rubric. Each half
has a life of its own. If the answer had been no, they would be one skill with two sections.

So Sutra gets two skills today:

- **`ticket-triage`** — the procedure: look up, search, classify, and say which rule decided.
- **`kb-answer-style`** — the house style for a grounded, cited reply.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because getting this wrong is expensive in a way that is invisible for weeks.

One over-broad skill fires for everything and is followed for nothing: the model activates it on a
question about refunds, pays 334 tokens
([26.2.3](../../../day-26-loading-skills-into-adk/parts/02-the-four-tools/2.3-what-activation-actually-costs.md)),
reads eleven steps of which two apply, and improvises. Nothing errors. The bill goes up and the
answers get slightly worse.

Too many narrow skills is the opposite failure and it is cheaper to fix: a longer index, more
descriptions competing, and a model that has to pick between `ticket-triage-severity` and
`ticket-triage-lookup` when both are half of one job. That one you notice, because the index gets
visibly silly.

Scope is also the unit of ownership, and that is the argument that survives contact with a real team.
`kb-answer-style` is a document the person who owns customer communication should be able to edit
without asking anybody. The moment it is a section inside `ticket-triage`, they have to negotiate with
whoever owns the rubric.

## The mechanism

Scope is written down before either folder exists, as two lines you can argue with.

```text
ticket-triage    — decide what a ticket is and how urgent, from the ticket and the KB.
kb-answer-style  — write a reply that is grounded in one KB article and cites it.
```

**Line by line:**

- Each line is **one sentence with one main verb**: *decide*, *write*. If a scope line needs two verbs
  joined by "and then", the split is wrong.
- Neither line mentions the other. A scope that has to refer to its sibling to be understood is a
  section, not a skill.
- Both name what they work **from** — the ticket and the KB, one KB article. Naming the inputs is what
  makes the boundary checkable later: a step that needs an input not on this line belongs to the other
  skill or to neither.

Then the folders, which are the cheapest part of the day:

```bash
mkdir -p skills/ticket-triage/references
mkdir -p skills/kb-answer-style/references
touch skills/ticket-triage/SKILL.md skills/kb-answer-style/SKILL.md
ls skills/
```

**Line by line:**

- `skills/` at the **repository root**, not in the day folder. This is Sutra's real shelf — the one
  `sutra/desk/skills.py` reads
  ([26.3.1](../../../day-26-loading-skills-into-adk/parts/03-wiring-the-toolset/3.1-a-toolset-not-a-tool.md))
  — and the layout in `CLAUDE.md` has had a `skills/` line waiting since Day 0. The day-26 lab shelf
  stays where it is, for experiments.
- The folder name **is** the `name` field, enforced by the loader
  ([26.1.1](../../../day-26-loading-skills-into-adk/parts/01-loading-a-folder/1.1-load-skill-from-dir.md)),
  so choosing the folder name is choosing the skill's identity. Choose it after the scope line, never
  before.
- `references/` is created empty on both. [1.6](1.6-edges-are-scar-tissue.md) and
  [3.3](../03-procedure-and-capability/3.3-two-skills-one-toolset.md) fill them; creating them now
  keeps the decision *"does this go in the body or behind a link?"* in front of you while you write.
- Day 25 already put a `ticket-triage` in the day-25 lab. This is a different folder with the same
  name in a different place, and that is fine — nothing loads both shelves at once. If you would
  rather avoid the collision entirely, rename the day-25 lab copy; the number is the identity, the
  slug is a label.

Measured on 2026-09-04:

```text
kb-answer-style
ticket-triage
```

Two folders, two scope lines, nothing written yet. That is deliberate: the next four parts fill them,
and every one of those parts is a decision that is easier to make once the boundary is fixed.

## When it breaks

**The "and" you did not notice because you used a comma.** *"Triage a ticket, then draft the reply"* is
the same two jobs with the conjunction disguised. Read your scope line aloud and count the verbs.

**The skill named after a noun instead of a job.** `severity-rubric` is a *thing*, not a job, and it
produces a skill whose description has to say *"use this when you need the severity rubric"* — which is
useless, because the model does not know it needs a rubric. It knows it has a ticket. Name the job, and
the rubric becomes a `references/` file inside it.

**Splitting because the file is getting long.** Length is not a scope signal. A body that is getting
long is usually a body with reference material in it
([25.3.1](../../../day-25-skills-the-open-spec/parts/03-the-body-and-the-folders/3.1-the-body-is-loaded-whole.md)),
and the fix is a link, not a second skill. Split when the *job* is two jobs — different moment,
different owner, independently wanted.

**Two skills whose descriptions both match the same request.** This is the failure the third test
exists to prevent, and it produces a model that picks one at random and then does half the work.
[5.2](../05-failure-lab/5.2-two-notices-on-one-door.md) is the version of that failure that actually
bit Sutra, and Day 28 turns the avoidance into a design rule.

## In production

**Write the scope line into the skill.** Not as a comment — as the first sentence of the description
and the first sentence of the body. When somebody later proposes adding a step, the scope line is what
the proposal is checked against, and a scope that exists only in a design document is a scope that
nobody checks.

**Let ownership decide close calls.** When the four-word test is genuinely ambiguous, ask who would be
annoyed to discover somebody else had edited it. Two different answers means two skills, whatever the
word count says. This is the same reasoning that puts a procedure in a folder rather than in a prompt
in the first place.

**What changes at scale** is that scope drift is the main way a skill collection rots. Nobody creates
a badly-scoped skill; they create a well-scoped one and then add a step to it every few weeks, each
addition individually reasonable. Six months later `ticket-triage` also handles escalation, refunds
and the weekly report. The cheap defence is the scope line at the top of the body and a review habit
of reading it before approving an addition.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"this skill's description says 'triage tickets and
draft replies'. Those are two jobs with two owners and they happen at different times. Split it, and
put the scope sentence at the top of each body so the next addition has something to fail against."*

**The interview question:** *"how do you decide how big a skill should be?"* The answer that shows you
have made the mistake: *"three tests. Can I name the job in four words with no 'and'? Do the halves
happen at the same moment, for the same asker? Would either half ever be wanted without the other? Our
triage procedure failed the second and third, so it became two skills — the procedure and the house
reply style — because the reply style is wanted for tickets triaged last week and is owned by a
different person. The real risk is not getting it wrong on day one; it is scope drift, so the scope
sentence goes at the top of the body and every proposed addition is checked against it."*

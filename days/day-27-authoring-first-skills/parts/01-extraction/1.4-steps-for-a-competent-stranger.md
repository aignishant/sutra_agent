# 1.4 — Steps a competent stranger can follow

## One-line answer

Question 3 of the pass produces numbered, imperative steps in the order they happen — each one a
single action, each one naming its tool in backticks, and each one carrying its own failure branch
rather than deferring it to a section at the bottom.

## The story

The courier calls because he cannot find the house.

You say *"turn at the temple"*, and there is a pause, because he does not know which building is the
temple. You say *"it's near the big tree"*, and he says there are trees. What eventually works is
this: *"go past the petrol pump, take the second left after the speed bump, it's the third gate on the
right, green."*

Four instructions, each one a thing that is either true or not true when you are standing in front of
it. He has never been to your street. He does not need to know anything about it. He needs each step
to be checkable at the moment he is at it.

The temple was a perfectly good landmark — for somebody who already knew the way.

## The idea in plain language

The body's numbered steps are written for a **competent stranger**: somebody who is good at the
general job and knows nothing about yours.

That is not a rhetorical device, it is close to literally true. A language model is competent at
reading, summarising and choosing between options, and knows nothing about your KB, your tools, or
what your team means by *high*. Writing for that reader means five rules, and each one has a reason
that is about the reader rather than about style.

**One action per step.** *"Look the ticket up and pull out the symptom"* is two actions, and a model
that does the first and forgets the second has followed the step. Splitting is free.

**Imperative, not descriptive.** *"The ticket is looked up first"* describes; *"Call `lookup_ticket`
with the ticket id"* instructs. Descriptive prose is read as background and background is not
followed.

**Name the tool in backticks.** A step that says *"look the ticket up"* leaves the model to match your
sentence against a tool declaration
([4.1.3](../../../day-04-tools-by-hand/parts/01-the-schema/1.3-the-description-is-the-prompt.md)).
`lookup_ticket` in backticks, spelled exactly as the tool is named, removes the matching problem
entirely — and makes the dependency greppable, which
[3.1](../03-procedure-and-capability/3.1-a-named-tool-is-a-contract.md) turns into a lint.

**Put the failure branch inside the step.** Not in an "Edge cases" section at the bottom. *"If it
returns a not-found message, stop and say the ticket does not exist"* belongs on step 1, because step
1 is where the reader is when it happens. Failure handling gathered at the bottom of a document is
failure handling that gets skimmed, and section 5 of this day is what that costs.

**End with what to say.** The last step is almost always the one people leave out, because it feels
obvious: *tell the engineer which severity you chose and which rule chose it*. A procedure that ends
when the work is done produces an agent that does the right thing silently, and silence is
indistinguishable from having done nothing.

There is one more property of numbered steps that is worth naming precisely, because it is the reason
this works at all and not merely why it reads well: **explicit steps change what the model does, not
just what it knows.** A model given a procedure as a numbered sequence produces intermediate results
in that sequence, and each intermediate result is in its context when it takes the next step. That is
the mechanism the chain-of-thought literature described, arriving here as document structure rather
than as a prompting trick.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because Sutra's procedure has a step whose failure branch is the whole point, and it currently lives
nowhere.

`lookup_ticket` returns a **message** on a miss, not an exception — a deliberate decision from Day 4,
recorded in a docstring
([4.2.3](../../../day-04-tools-by-hand/parts/02-the-round-trip/2.3-the-tool-result-turn.md)). So a
model that asks for ticket 9999 gets back the string `No ticket with id '9999'.` and has to decide
what that means. Nothing in the system currently tells it. The honest answer — *say the ticket does not
exist, and say nothing else about it* — has been in the instruction as a general honesty rule, which
is a category rather than a step.

The same is true of the KB. `search_kb` returns `No KB article matched '...'` and the correct
behaviour is to say so rather than to explain the cause anyway. That is a rule with real consequences,
it is one sentence, and it belongs beside the step that can trigger it.

## The paper behind it

> **Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models** · `arXiv:2201.11903` ·
> 2022 · <https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.11903>

It showed that prompting a model to produce intermediate reasoning steps, rather than an answer
directly, substantially improves multi-step tasks — which is why a procedure written as an ordered list
of single actions outperforms the same procedure written as a paragraph.

Taught in full on Day 2 —
[Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models](../../../day-02-llm-mechanics/papers/02-chain-of-thought-prompting.md).

## The mechanism

The steps for `ticket-triage`, written into the body under the frontmatter from
[1.3](1.3-the-six-question-pass.md).

```markdown
# Ticket triage

Decide what a ticket is about and how urgent it is, from the ticket text and the knowledge base.

## Steps

1. Call `lookup_ticket` with the ticket id. If it returns a message beginning `No ticket with id`,
   stop: tell the engineer the ticket does not exist, and say nothing else about it.
2. Read the ticket text and write down the symptom in **one or two words** - `logout`, `export`,
   `billing`. Do not use the reporter's full sentence; the knowledge base matches on keywords.
3. Call `search_kb` with that one-word symptom.
4. If `search_kb` returns a message beginning `No KB article matched`, say the knowledge base has no
   match and recommend escalation. Do not explain a cause you cannot cite.
5. Otherwise, explain the cause using **only** what the article says, and name the article id in the
   answer.
6. Choose a severity from [references/severity-rubric.md](references/severity-rubric.md).
7. Tell the engineer the severity and the rubric row that decided it, in one sentence.
```

**Line by line:**

- The first line after the heading is the **scope sentence** from
  [1.2](1.2-one-job-four-words.md), verbatim. It is not a duplicate of the description: the
  description was written to be *selected on*, this is written for a model that has already been given
  the file and is about to act. It is also what a future addition gets checked against.
- **Step 1 carries its own failure branch**, and the branch quotes the tool's actual output —
  `No ticket with id` — rather than describing it. A model comparing a string it has just received
  against a string in its instructions is doing something easy. A model deciding whether a sentence
  "counts as" a failure is doing something hard.
- *"say nothing else about it"* is there because the interesting failure is not silence, it is a
  fluent paragraph about a ticket that does not exist. The rule has to forbid the plausible thing, not
  just require the correct one.
- **Step 2 is the edge from [1.1](1.1-where-the-procedure-lives-now.md), promoted into a step.** The KB
  is keyword-matched, so `keeps getting logged out` returns nothing and `logout` returns KB-104. That
  is a fact about the tool, it is not discoverable from the tool's description, and putting it in an
  "Edge cases" section at the bottom would mean the model reads it *after* deciding what to search
  for. Some edges are steps.
- **Steps 3 and 4 are separate** for the same reason as 1 and 2: the call and the failure branch are
  one action each, and step 4's *"do not explain a cause you cannot cite"* is the rule that stops the
  most damaging behaviour available here.
- **Step 5 has two obligations and both are honesty rules**: explain only from the article, and name
  it. They are in one step because they are one act — writing the explanation — and splitting them
  would produce a step nobody can perform on its own.
- **Step 6 is a link, not a table.** The rubric is reference material: it will grow, and the body is
  loaded whole on every activation
  ([25.3.1](../../../day-25-skills-the-open-spec/parts/03-the-body-and-the-folders/3.1-the-body-is-loaded-whole.md)).
  The link is written as `references/severity-rubric.md` because that is exactly the string the model
  has to pass to `load_skill_resource`, and a path without the `references/` prefix is refused
  ([26.2.4](../../../day-26-loading-skills-into-adk/parts/02-the-four-tools/2.4-the-third-rung-and-its-fence.md)).
- **Step 7 is the one people leave out.** It also does something subtle: *"and the rubric row that
  decided it"* forces the model to have actually consulted the rubric, because it cannot name a row it
  did not read. A step that requires evidence of an earlier step is cheap and effective.
- There are **seven steps for five decisions**, because three failure branches got their own numbers.
  That is the right trade: a step you can skip by accident is worse than a list that is two lines
  longer.

Check that it loads and that the body is now doing something:

```python
# days/day-27-authoring-first-skills/lab/read_the_body.py
"""Count the steps and collect every backticked span in a skill body."""

import pathlib
import re

from google.adk.skills import load_skill_from_dir

skill = load_skill_from_dir(pathlib.Path("skills") / "ticket-triage")
body = skill.instructions

print("body chars :", len(body))
print("steps      :", len(re.findall(r"^\d+\.\s", body, re.M)))
print("backticked :", sorted(set(re.findall(r"`([^`]+)`", body))))
```

**Line by line:**

- `re.findall(r"^\d+\.\s", body, re.M)` counts the steps — a digit, a dot and a space at the start of
  a line, with `re.M` so `^` means *start of line* rather than *start of string*. It is a regex rather
  than a Markdown parser because the number is being read once, by a person, and a parser would be
  more machinery than the question deserves.
- `re.findall(r"`([^`]+)`", body)` collects every **span** between backticks, not every backticked
  word. That distinction matters: `No ticket with id` is three words inside one pair of backticks, and
  a word-level scan would miss it entirely while also tripping over the comma after `logout`,.
- `set(...)` then `sorted(...)` because the same tool is named more than once and the output is being
  compared against a previous run.
- This scan is the seed of the lint in
  [3.1](../03-procedure-and-capability/3.1-a-named-tool-is-a-contract.md): the tools a procedure names
  are the tools it requires, and requiring them is a claim that has to be honoured somewhere.
- **Zero model calls.**

Measured on 2026-09-04:

```text
body chars : 1013
steps      : 7
backticked : ['No KB article matched', 'No ticket with id', 'billing', 'export', 'logout', 'lookup_ticket', 'search_kb']
```

Read that last line carefully, because it is a problem in disguise. Seven backticked spans, and only
**two** of them are tools. Backticks in this body are doing three different jobs: naming a tool
(`lookup_ticket`, `search_kb`), quoting a string the tool returns (`No ticket with id`), and giving an
example value (`logout`). A lint that treats every backticked span as a required tool would report five
false positives on a perfectly good skill — which is exactly the kind of check that gets switched off
in its second week. [3.1](../03-procedure-and-capability/3.1-a-named-tool-is-a-contract.md) is where
that gets solved, and it is solved by declaring the tools rather than by guessing them.

## When it breaks

**A step that describes rather than instructs.** The failure is silent and looks like partial
compliance:

> *"The knowledge base is searched using a short keyword."*

A model reads that as context. Rewrite as `Call search_kb with a one-word symptom.` and the same
sentence becomes an action.

**A step that names a tool that does not exist.** Nothing fails at load time; the model reads the step,
looks for the tool, and does not find it. What happens next depends on the model and is never good.
[5.1](../05-failure-lab/5.1-the-drill-at-a-locked-gate.md) runs this deliberately.

**A failure branch in the wrong place.** Move step 1's branch to an "Edge cases" heading at the bottom
and the procedure still reads correctly to a human. It reads correctly to the model too — which is the
problem: it reads the branch after it has already written a paragraph about a ticket that does not
exist.

**A step that assumes state from earlier in the conversation.** *"Search the KB for the symptom"* is
fine on turn two and meaningless on turn six of a conversation about three different tickets. Steps
that refer to *"the ticket"* rather than to *"the ticket id you were given"* work in the worked example
and drift in real use.

**Braces in the body, if state injection is ever switched on.** A body containing `{severity}` is
inert today and becomes a template the moment somebody sets `adk_inject_state: true` in the metadata
([26.2.3](../../../day-26-loading-skills-into-adk/parts/02-the-four-tools/2.3-what-activation-actually-costs.md)).
Sutra does not set it; knowing that the switch exists is what stops a JSON example in a body from
becoming a mystery later.

## In production

**Write the steps in the order they happen, and never in the order they were thought of.** Extraction
tends to surface rules in order of memorability — the dramatic edge case first, the boring first step
last. Reordering is the cheapest edit in the whole exercise and the one most often skipped.

**Quote the real strings your tools return.** `No ticket with id` is in the body because it is what
`lookup_ticket` actually produces. That couples the skill to the tool's wording, which is a real cost:
change the message and the skill needs updating. It is worth it, because the alternative couples the
skill to the model's judgement about what counts as a failure. Pin the coupling with a test — a test
asserting that `lookup_ticket("9999")` still starts with the string the skill quotes is three lines and
catches the drift.

**Keep the steps at one screen.** Not because of a token budget — the body is small next to the
frontmatter dump
([26.2.3](../../../day-26-loading-skills-into-adk/parts/02-the-four-tools/2.3-what-activation-actually-costs.md))
— but because a procedure longer than a screen is a procedure whose middle is skimmed, by people and
by models both
([19.3.3](../../../day-19-context-engineering-selection/parts/03-selection/3.3-position-is-not-presence.md)).
When it grows past that, the honest question is whether it is two jobs
([1.2](1.2-one-job-four-words.md)).

**What changes at scale** is that steps accumulate conditionals. A procedure that starts as seven
imperatives becomes seven imperatives and eleven *"if the customer is on the Enterprise plan"*
qualifiers, and the model has to evaluate all of them on every activation. At that point the fix is
usually to split by the condition rather than to add another clause — one skill for the standard
path, one for the exception — because a skill's selection interface can express *"use this when the
customer is Enterprise"* far better than a step buried on line forty can.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"step 2 says 'search the KB with the symptom' and our
KB is a keyword match, so the reporter's phrasing will miss every time. Say one or two words, and give
three examples in the step — that is the kind of thing that has to be in the procedure because it is
not discoverable from the tool."*

**The interview question:** *"how do you write the instructions inside a skill?"* The answer that
shows you have watched one fail: *"numbered, imperative, one action per step, in the order they happen,
with the tool named in backticks exactly as it is declared. The two things people get wrong are
writing descriptions instead of instructions, and collecting the failure branches into a section at
the bottom — a branch belongs inside the step that triggers it, because that is where the model is
when it happens. And the last step is always 'tell the person what you decided and why', because
otherwise you get an agent that does the right thing silently."*

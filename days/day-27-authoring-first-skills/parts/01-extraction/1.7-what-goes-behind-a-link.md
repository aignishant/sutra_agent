# 1.7 — What goes behind a link, and what a fetch really costs

## One-line answer

Question 6 sends *true but long* material into `references/` — and the rule for the split is not size,
it is **how often it is needed**, because on Sutra's tier a reference fetch costs a whole model round
trip and not merely a hundred and forty tokens.

## The story

The log tables are at the back of the maths textbook, and you used them twice.

Twenty pages of tiny four-figure numbers, printed on thinner paper, after the index. For two years
they were dead weight in your bag every single day. Then, in the week the chapter came up, they were
the most important pages in the book — and having them bound into the same book was exactly right,
because the alternative was a separate booklet you would have left at home.

Nobody ever suggested printing the log tables inside chapter three, where the formula that needs them
is. They would have been in the way of everything else for two years, to save you flipping to the back
twice.

## The idea in plain language

`references/` is a folder inside the skill. Its files are loaded into the process at startup along
with everything else
([26.1.1](../../../day-26-loading-skills-into-adk/parts/01-loading-a-folder/1.1-load-skill-from-dir.md)),
and reach the model only when the model calls `load_skill_resource` with the path
([26.2.4](../../../day-26-loading-skills-into-adk/parts/02-the-four-tools/2.4-the-third-rung-and-its-fence.md)).

Day 25 gave the rule as *"the body is loaded whole, so move the bulky things out"*
([25.3.1](../../../day-25-skills-the-open-spec/parts/03-the-body-and-the-folders/3.1-the-body-is-loaded-whole.md)).
That is right and it is only half the picture, because Day 26 measured the other half.

Here is the arithmetic, on Sutra's model and Sutra's tier, measured on 2026-09-04
([26.5.1](../../../day-26-loading-skills-into-adk/parts/05-in-production/5.1-what-a-skill-really-costs-here.md)):

| | In the body | In `references/` |
| --- | --- | --- |
| tokens on activation | its full size, every time | 0 |
| tokens when needed | 0 extra | its size, once |
| tokens on later turns | its size, every turn | its size, every turn after the fetch |
| **model round trips** | **0** | **1** |
| requests spent, of 20 a day | 0 | **1** |

The last two rows are the ones Day 25 could not know about, and they change the answer. Sutra's
severity rubric measures **142 tokens** on `gemini-3.7-flash` (2026-09-04), against a body of 654. A
hundred and forty tokens in the body, on every activation, is a fifth of what is already there — real,
and not the thing that hurts. One extra round trip, on a budget of twenty generations a day, is five
per cent of the day's capacity, spent on fetching those hundred and forty tokens.

So the rule is not *"is it big?"*. It is:

**In the body:** anything needed on **every** run of the procedure, regardless of size, and anything
small enough that fetching it would cost more than carrying it.

**In `references/`:** anything needed **sometimes**, and anything that will **grow**.

The rubric fails the first test and passes the second, and the second is why it goes behind a link
anyway. Three rows today; twelve rows within a year, plus the notes about which row applies to
enterprise customers, plus the exception for security reports. A body that grows every time somebody
improves the policy is a body whose activation cost grows with its own usefulness.

That is the honest statement of the trade, and it is worth being able to say out loud: **Sutra is
paying one round trip per triage to keep the rubric editable without touching the body.** Not to save
a hundred and forty tokens. Day 28 revisits the decision with more evidence; today the reasoning is
what matters.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because the rubric is the part of this procedure that support leadership owns, and it is the part most
likely to change.

Everything else in the body is mechanics: call this, then that, quote the article. The rubric is
policy — a judgement about what *high* means, which will be argued about, revised after an incident,
and eventually have an exception added for security reports. Policy that lives in the same file as
mechanics is policy whose every edit risks the mechanics.

There is also a reviewability argument that matters more than the tokens. A pull request that changes
`references/severity-rubric.md` and nothing else is a pull request the support lead can read and
approve in thirty seconds. The same change buried inside a body edit is a pull request they will skim.

## The mechanism

The rubric, written as the file the body's step 6 links to.

```markdown
# Severity rubric

| Severity | When | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| high | money moved wrongly, data lost, or nobody can sign in | "all logins fail since the deploy" |
| normal | one customer blocked, or one feature wrong for one customer | ticket 4521 - logging back in works |
| low | a question, a typo, or a feature request | "can we have dark mode?" |

Rule of thumb: if the reporter can still get their work done, it is at most `normal`.

If two rows both fit, choose the **lower** severity and say which two rows you were between.
```

**Line by line:**

- **Three columns, not two.** *When* is the rule and *Example* is what makes it usable: a model
  choosing between `normal` and `high` for a login problem needs to see that *"all logins fail"* is the
  high one. An example column costs a dozen tokens and removes most of the ambiguity.
- The `normal` row's example is **ticket 4521**, the same ticket as the worked example in
  [1.5](1.5-one-worked-example.md). Reusing the case across the body and the reference is deliberate:
  a reader who fetches the rubric after seeing the example lands on something familiar.
- The rule of thumb is one line and it resolves most of the cases the table does not. It is the kind
  of sentence that lives in a support lead's head and nowhere else, which is the whole reason for
  today.
- The last line handles the tie, which is the case that produces inconsistent triage across a team.
  *"Say which two rows you were between"* makes the ambiguity visible in the answer rather than hidden
  in a choice.
- Severity names are in backticks in the rule of thumb and plain in the table, which is a small
  inconsistency and worth fixing in your copy — a reference file is read by the model as text, and
  gratuitous formatting variation is one more thing it has to decide is not meaningful.

Write it and confirm the loader sees it:

```bash
uv run python -c "
import pathlib
from google.adk.skills import load_skill_from_dir
s = load_skill_from_dir(pathlib.Path('skills') / 'ticket-triage')
print('references :', sorted(s.resources.references))
print('rubric len :', len(s.resources.get_reference('severity-rubric.md')))
print('linked as  :', 'references/severity-rubric.md' in s.instructions)
"
```

**Line by line:**

- The reference key is `severity-rubric.md` — **relative to `references/`**, with no prefix
  ([26.1.2](../../../day-26-loading-skills-into-adk/parts/01-loading-a-folder/1.2-the-skill-object.md)).
  The link in the body is `references/severity-rubric.md` **with** the prefix, because that is the
  string the model passes to `load_skill_resource` and a path without it is refused. Two different
  spellings of the same file, and both are required.
- The third line is the check that matters and it is the one nobody runs: **does the body actually link
  to the file that exists?** A reference nobody links to is never fetched, and a link to a file that is
  not there produces a dead end at request time
  ([26.2.4](../../../day-26-loading-skills-into-adk/parts/02-the-four-tools/2.4-the-third-rung-and-its-fence.md)).
  It is a `in` test today and it is the lint Day 31 wires into `./m check`.
- **Zero model calls.**

Measured on 2026-09-04:

```text
references : ['severity-rubric.md']
rubric len : 541
linked as  : True
```

Five hundred and forty-one characters — **142 tokens**, counted on `gemini-3.7-flash` the same day —
carried by nobody until step 6 asks for it, at the cost of one round trip.

## When it breaks

**A reference nothing links to.** It loads, it costs memory, and the model has no way to know it
exists — `load_skill_resource` is not a directory listing, it is a fetch by exact path. The check
above catches it; nothing else will.

**A link that does not match the key.** Write `[the rubric](severity-rubric.md)` without the prefix and
the model copies exactly that into the tool call:

```text
{'error': "Path must start with 'references/', 'assets/', or 'scripts/'.", 'error_code': 'INVALID_RESOURCE_PATH'}
```

The model then, obeying the framework's own preamble, reports the error and stops — so a missing
prefix in a Markdown link ends the turn.

**A renamed file and an unchanged body.** The failure is at request time, once, per conversation:

```text
{'error': "Resource 'references/severity-rubric.md' not found in skill 'ticket-triage'.", 'error_code': 'RESOURCE_NOT_FOUND'}
```

Nothing in `agentskills validate` catches this
([25.5.1](../../../day-25-skills-the-open-spec/parts/05-in-production/5.1-validating-before-you-ship.md)).
It is one of the two checks this day is handing to Day 31.

**Moving something out that was needed every time.** The symptom is an extra round trip on every
single triage, which on twenty requests a day is visible immediately. If a reference is fetched on
essentially every activation, it is body content wearing a link.

## In production

**Split by rate of change and by owner, then check the cost.** The two questions that decide it in
practice are *who edits this?* and *how often is it needed?* — and only when those agree should the
token count get a vote. A reference owned by a different team is worth a round trip; a reference
nobody has edited in a year and that every run needs is worth inlining.

**Measure the fetch rate before optimising it.** Day 26's activation record tells you which skills
fire ([26.3.3](../../../day-26-loading-skills-into-adk/parts/03-wiring-the-toolset/3.3-activation-is-a-line-in-state.md));
the tool result tells you which references were fetched. A reference fetched on ninety per cent of
activations is a body section, and a reference fetched on two per cent is doing its job. Neither number
is guessable.

**One reference per decision, not one per topic.** The temptation is a single `POLICY.md` holding
everything. It is one fetch, which sounds efficient, and it delivers the whole policy to answer one
question — which is the body problem moved one level down. Small files, each linked from the step that
needs it, is what makes the mechanism worth having.

**What changes at scale** is that references become a small documentation site inside a folder, with
its own staleness problem and no reviewer. The teams that keep them honest treat a reference file the
way they treat a runbook: an owner in the skill's metadata
([1.3](1.3-the-six-question-pass.md)), a date, and a review whenever the thing it describes changes.
The failure is not a wrong reference; it is a reference that was right two policies ago and is still
being cited by id in customer replies.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"the rubric is 142 tokens and we are spending a
round trip to fetch it. That is fine if the reason is that support owns it and edits it — say so in
the commit message. If the reason was 'the body was getting long', put it back."*

**The interview question:** *"how do you decide what goes in a skill's reference files?"* The answer
that shows you have priced it on a real tier: *"not by size. By how often it is needed and who owns
it. Anything needed on every run stays in the body whatever it costs, because fetching it is a whole
extra model round trip, and on a rate-limited tier requests are scarcer than tokens. What goes behind a
link is what is needed sometimes and what will grow — for us, the severity rubric at 142 tokens,
because support leadership owns it and it will have twelve rows by next year. We spend one round trip
per triage for that, deliberately, and we wrote down why."*

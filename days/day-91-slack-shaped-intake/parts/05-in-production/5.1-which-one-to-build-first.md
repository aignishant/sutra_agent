# 5.1 — Which one to build first

## One-line answer

The survey's ten rows sort three ways — by effort, by what they can see, and by whether anybody
would use them — and the three orders disagree, so the useful answer is to build the narrowest
channel somebody is already asking for, which here is the one this day built.

## The story

One window open and three shut.

A counter with four positions. Three have chairs, monitors and a sign each. One has a person at it,
and a queue of nineteen people.

Somebody looking at this decides the answer is obvious: open the other three. It is not, and the
reason is visible from the queue itself — most of the nineteen are there for one thing, the thing
that takes the longest, and the other three positions are equipped for work that hardly anyone in
this queue needs. Opening them moves three people and leaves sixteen.

The question that was never asked is what the queue is *for*. Everybody was looking at the counters.

## The idea in plain language

Part [1.2](../01-what-an-integration-is/1.2-the-ways-in.md) produced ten rows. Ten rows is a
roadmap, and a roadmap needs an order. There are three plausible ones and they disagree with each
other, which is the whole difficulty.

**By effort.** Cheapest first. This is the order a team defaults to and it is the worst of the three,
because effort is uncorrelated with value and, as part 1.2 measured, *inversely* correlated with
blast radius — the widest data surface in the survey is a token and an afternoon.

**By what it can see.** Narrowest first. This is the order a security review would choose, and it is
the right tiebreaker, but on its own it builds a very safe integration nobody asked for.

**By demand.** What somebody is actually waiting on. This is the only order with information in it
that is not already in the table, and it is the one that cannot be derived by looking at the
repository — you have to ask.

The workable rule is to sort by demand, then break ties by narrowness, and refuse to let effort
into the ordering at all except as a tiebreaker of last resort. Cheap and unwanted is still unwanted;
cheap and wide is a liability that arrives faster.

Applied to this survey, that produces a first row of *chat intake*, which is why the plan's
assignment for this day names it. Somebody wants to talk to the desk where they already work. It
sees one channel. And the parts of it that cost nothing to build — the endpoint, the verification,
the fixtures — are the parts that are hard to get right, while the part that needs an account is the
part that is mechanical.

That last split is the genuinely useful observation of this day, and it generalises: **for most
integrations, the free half is the difficult half.** Signature verification, replay windows, raw-body
handling, idempotence and scoping are all buildable against fixtures on a machine with no network.
Connecting to a live workspace is forms.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because this repository has one contributor and a free-tier budget, and four of the ten rows are
🅿️ parked for the duration. The decision is not which to build eventually — it is which to build
with the time there is.

And because Day 93 makes the repository public with this survey in it. A survey ordered by effort
reads as a wish list; a survey ordered by demand with the reasoning attached reads as a plan, and
somebody deciding whether to depend on this project can tell the difference.

## The mechanism

The ordering is not in code, and it should not be — it depends on a fact about people. What *is* in
code is the evidence each row carries, which is what makes an ordering argument possible at all:

```python
    budget = [s for s in SURVEY if s.status == BUDGET]
    print(f"\n  built and proven:      {sum(1 for s in rows if s.status == BUILT) - len(unproven)}")
    print(f"  parked, requires budget: {len(budget)}  ({', '.join(s.name for s in budget)})")
```

**Line by line:**

- The two summary numbers are the two ends of the roadmap: what exists, and what is out of reach for
  reasons that are not engineering. Everything between them is the actual decision space, and it is
  four rows.
- `built and proven` subtracts `unproven`, so the count is of rows that passed the filesystem check
  rather than of rows that claim to have. A summary that counts claims is part
  [1.3](../01-what-an-integration-is/1.3-a-survey-that-can-go-red.md)'s notice board.
- The parked rows are **named**, not just counted. A number invites "can we do one of them?"; a list
  invites "which one, and what would it cost?", which is the conversation worth having.

Run it and read the bottom:


**Line by line:**

- `tail -5` for the summary; the table itself is part

Measured on 2026-09-07:

```text
  built and proven:      4
  parked, requires budget: 4  (ticketing platform, SMS or messaging gateway, voice telephony, managed agent platform)

  every surface claimed as built has something on disk behind it
```

**Four and four**, with two rows in between needing an account rather than money. Those two are the
whole roadmap, and both of them are the mechanical half of work whose difficult half is already
done.

## When it breaks

The ordering breaks when demand is guessed instead of asked, and it has a recognisable failure: the
integration nobody uses, built well.

It is expensive in a way that does not show up as a cost. The endpoint exists, it is correct, it has
tests, and it appears in the survey as `built` — so the survey's check stays green and the row keeps
claiming a capability. Every subsequent planning conversation treats that row as done, and the
absence of traffic through it is never information anybody collects.

The second failure is the opposite and worse: building the wide one because it is cheap. The
ticketing row in this survey sees every ticket in the project and needs a token and a base URL. It
is genuinely the least work in the table. And the day it exists, the desk's blast radius includes
every ticket, which is a change no security review was asked about, because from the outside it
looked like a configuration task.

There is no run to show for either, because both are decisions rather than behaviours. The check
that would catch the first is a usage count, which this repository has nowhere to put yet. The check
that catches the second is a review rule — a new integration is a change to the blast-radius table
in part [4.1](../04-blast-radius/4.1-what-a-forged-request-reaches.md), and a change to that table
needs a reviewer.

## In production

**What a professional does.** Asks for one concrete use before building a channel, and writes it into
the row. Not "the team wants Slack" — *"the support lead wants to ask the desk about a ticket without
leaving the thread"*, which is specific enough to say what tools the runner needs and therefore what
the scope is. A row without a sentence like that is a row somebody imagined.

**What degrades at scale.** Every channel is permanent. Integrations acquire users quietly and
removing one is a conversation with those users, so the cost of a channel is not the building — it
is that you now have it for ever, including through every refactor of the desk it fronts. Ten
channels is ten adapters to keep working, which is part
[1.1](../01-what-an-integration-is/1.1-somebody-elses-shape.md)'s argument arriving as a maintenance
bill.

**The failure that only shows with real traffic.** Two channels for the same conversation. Somebody
asks in chat, gets an answer, and then the same question arrives by email because that is where the
customer wrote. Neither channel knows about the other, so the desk answers twice with no shared
session — and session identity across channels is a genuinely hard problem this survey does not
mention, because it only appears when the second channel exists.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Before we build the ticketing one because it is
easy: it sees every ticket in the project. Put that in the ticket description and let whoever
approves it approve that sentence, not the estimate."*

**The interview question:** *"We have budget for one integration this quarter. How do you choose?"*
The answer that shows experience asks who is waiting and what each option can see, and explicitly
declines to choose on effort — then names the tiebreaker, which is narrowness.

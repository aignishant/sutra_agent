# 5.2 — What a real integration layer adds

## One-line answer

The lab verifies a signature correctly, refuses replays, keeps the raw bytes and separates
authenticity from safety — and the distance between that and something a team can point at a
workspace is nine items, three of which take an afternoon between them and one of which is not code.

## The story

The spares drawer with each compartment labelled by what it fits.

A workshop drawer, divided, and every compartment has a strip of tape on it with a name written in
marker. Not "screws" — *the screws for the blue machine's side panel*. Somebody spent a slow
afternoon doing that, and it looked like tidying.

It is not tidying. It is the difference between a drawer you search and a drawer you use. The label
records a fact that lives nowhere else: which thing this fits. Without it, everything in the drawer
is still there, still perfectly good, and every job starts with a five-minute identification
problem that somebody has already solved once and not written down.

## The idea in plain language

The lab does five things properly. It reads the raw body before anything touches it. It builds the
base string in one place, from the vendor's documented recipe. It checks the timestamp before doing
the expensive work. It compares digests in constant time. And it keeps authenticity and safety as
separate questions with separate answers.

Here is what is between that and a real intake.

**1. The secret from the environment, with no default.**
Part [4.2](../04-blast-radius/4.2-the-secret-itself.md): `os.environ[...]`, indexed not `.get`, so a
missing secret fails at boot. This is first because it is the only item that is a *security*
difference rather than a robustness one, and it is one line.

**2. Acknowledge, then work.**
Part [2.1](../02-verifying-it/2.1-what-a-vendor-sends.md): the vendor retries anything it thinks
failed, so the handler must return before calling a model. That turns the endpoint into
verify-and-enqueue and makes every later item possible.

**3. Idempotence on the vendor's event identifier.**
Part [3.1](../03-three-ways-it-goes-wrong/3.1-the-signature-that-never-expires.md) bounds replay to
a window and does nothing inside it, and item 2 guarantees duplicates. Day 73's mechanism, keyed on
`event_id`, with a short-lived store.

**4. An allowlist of event types.**
Part [2.1](../02-verifying-it/2.1-what-a-vendor-sends.md): route the types you have designed for and
refuse the rest, rather than an `else` that treats an unknown envelope as a message. Day 40's
filtering argument on the envelope.

**5. A body size limit.**
Part [4.1](../04-blast-radius/4.1-what-a-forged-request-reaches.md) named this as an honest gap: the
handler reads an unbounded body from a public endpoint because the signature scheme requires the
whole thing. A maximum, refused before reading, is a few lines and closes it.

**6. The chat runner's own toolset.**
Part [4.1](../04-blast-radius/4.1-what-a-forged-request-reaches.md) again, and the largest item on
the list. A runner whose tools are the chat scope rather than the desk scope, so widening is a diff.

**7. The vector as a test, not a script.**
Part [2.3](../02-verifying-it/2.3-checked-against-the-vendor.md): move `vector.py` into
`tests/test_intake.py`. It is the only test that checks this repository against somebody else, and
it is the one that survives a refactor of the base-string assembly.

**8. A grep test for vendor field names.**
Part [1.1](../01-what-an-integration-is/1.1-somebody-elses-shape.md): assert that no module outside
the adapter mentions the vendor's field names. Five lines, and it is what keeps the decomposition
true after everybody has forgotten why it was that way.

**9. 🅿️ Parked — the live workspace.**
A real app, a real signing secret and a public HTTPS URL. Needs an account rather than budget, and
it is the mechanical half of the work: the hard half is already done and tested against fixtures.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because Day 92 is a hardening pass over this repository and items 1, 4 and 5 are exactly what it
will look for on a public endpoint. Doing them first turns that review into a confirmation.

And because item 6 is the one that gets deferred. Items 1 to 5 are small and obviously correct;
item 6 requires deciding what a chat message may cause, which is a design conversation with somebody
else in it. That is why it is on this list with a size rather than in a comment.

## The mechanism

The list, with what each costs and what it closes, so it can be argued with:

| # | Item | Where it lands | Roughly | Closes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | secret from the environment, no default | `sutra/intake.py` | one line | part 4.2 |
| 2 | acknowledge, then enqueue | the handler | an afternoon | part 2.1 |
| 3 | idempotence on `event_id` | a small store | an afternoon | part 3.1 |
| 4 | event-type allowlist | the handler | a few lines | part 2.1 |
| 5 | body size limit | the handler | a few lines | part 4.1 |
| 6 | the chat runner's own toolset | `sutra/` and a design call | a conversation | part 4.1 |
| 7 | the vector as a test | `tests/test_intake.py` | a few lines | part 2.3 |
| 8 | grep test for vendor field names | `tests/test_intake.py` | five lines | part 1.1 |
| 9 | 🅿️ the live workspace | not built | needs an account | — |

**Items 1, 4, 5, 7 and 8 are an afternoon between them** and they are the ones that turn the lab from
a demonstration into something that could face the internet. Items 2, 3 and 6 are the real work, and
6 is the only one that needs somebody else's agreement.

Item 8 is the one worth writing out, because it is the smallest and the most easily lost:

```python
    vendor_names = {"event", "user", "text", "channel", "ts"}
    for path in Path("sutra").rglob("*.py"):
        if path.name == "adapters.py":
            continue
        source = path.read_text(encoding="utf-8")
        assert not (vendor_names & set(re.findall(r'"(\w+)"', source))), path
```

**Line by line:**

- `rglob("*.py")` over the product package only. The lab and the tests are allowed to mention
  whatever they like; the rule is about the code that does the work.
- The adapter is skipped by name, and that exemption is the entire design being asserted — one file
  is allowed to know the vendor, and the test names it.
- `re.findall(r'"(\w+)"', source)` finds double-quoted words, which is crude and deliberately so: it
  will produce a false positive on any module with an unrelated `"text"` in it. That is a
  conversation about naming rather than a flaw in the test, and it is cheaper than an AST walk.
- `assert ... , path` so a failure names the file. The demo in
  [papers/01](../../papers/01-the-module-that-owns-the-vendor.md) does the AST version properly if
  the crude one becomes annoying.

## When it breaks

The list breaks by being read as improvements to make later, and the composite has been measured
piece by piece across this day:

```text
1. the intake verifies signatures correctly                      (parts 2.2, 2.3)
2. it calls the model inline, because that is the simple version (part 2.1)
3. a slow answer makes the vendor retry                          (part 2.1)
4. the duplicate is inside the replay window, so it verifies     (part 3.1)
5. nothing is keyed on event_id, so the desk acts twice          (item 3)
6. the runner has the desk's tools, so acting twice moves money  (part 4.1)
7. every request in the sequence was authentic
```

Seven steps, no error at any of them, and the endpoint's signature verification — the part that got
all the attention — is working perfectly throughout. Step 7 is the one that makes it hard to
investigate: there is no forged request in the logs, because there was never a forged request.

The step that breaks the chain most cheaply is 5, and it is item 3 on the list.

## In production

**What a professional does.** Ships items 1, 4, 5 and 8 with the first version, because they are
minutes and they are the ones that are embarrassing to be missing. Then item 2, which is the
architectural one, before any real traffic — retrofitting acknowledge-then-enqueue into a handler
that calls a model inline touches everything.

**What degrades at scale.** Items 3 and 5 get more important and more expensive. An idempotence
store that is a dictionary works until there are two instances of the service, at which point it has
to be shared, and Day 86 measured exactly that failure in a different guise — two replicas, a
session created on one, a `404` on the other.

**The failure that only shows with real traffic.** Item 6's absence is invisible until somebody asks
the bot for something surprising and it works. Nothing logs "an over-scoped tool was available"; the
only signal is a capability nobody meant to ship being discovered by a user, which is a bad way to
find out and the normal way.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Items 1, 4, 5 and 8 in this PR please — they are
an afternoon. Item 6 needs a conversation about what a chat message may cause, and I would rather
have that before we point it at a workspace than after."*

**The interview question:** *"What would you add to a webhook endpoint before trusting it?"* The
answer that shows experience starts with acknowledge-then-enqueue and idempotence rather than with
verification, because verification is the part everybody has already done and duplicate delivery is
the part that bites.

# 3.2 — 💥 The comparison you cannot benchmark

## One-line answer

Comparing digests with `==` can leak where the comparison stopped, so the guard is
`hmac.compare_digest` — and this machine cannot measure the difference at all, which is the honest
and more useful finding: the guard is adopted on documented grounds, because a benchmark here
produces noise with a sign that changes between runs.

## The story

💥 **Trying to hear a whisper next to a running generator.**

🎬 **The scene.** Somebody is trying to work out whether a machine in the next room is running. They
stand at the door and listen. The building's generator is going, as it does every afternoon.

😬 **The naive fix.** Listen harder. Cup a hand behind the ear, hold your breath, get closer to the
door.

💥 **Why it fails.** They hear something, and then nothing, and then something again. They come away
saying "I think it is running" and the next person doing the same thing comes away saying the
opposite. Neither of them is lying and neither has learned anything. The generator is louder than
the thing they are listening for, so the answer they get is a fact about the generator.

💡 **The insight.** **A measurement that cannot separate its subject from its noise does not return
a weak answer — it returns no answer.** And the dangerous version is not the person who says "I
could not tell". It is the one who happened to catch a quiet moment, heard the machine, and now
believes something on the strength of one reading.

## The idea in plain language

Comparing two strings is usually done character by character, stopping at the first difference. That
is the fast and obvious implementation, and every language does it.

For a secret comparison it leaks. A guess that differs at the first character is rejected after one
comparison; a guess that matches the first sixty and differs at the sixty-first takes sixty-one. If
an attacker can measure how long the rejection took, they learn how much of their guess was right —
and can then find the correct value one character at a time, turning an impossible search into a
short one.

The fix is a comparison that always takes the same path regardless of where the inputs differ.
Python provides it as `hmac.compare_digest`, and it is one function call.

Now the part everybody skips, and the reason this part exists. Having explained a timing attack, the
natural instinct is to *demonstrate* it — time both comparisons and show the difference. So this
lab tries, honestly, and reports what it gets.

It cannot see it. Neither arm shows a stable difference. The gap between "differs early" and
"differs late" changes sign between trials, in both the safe comparison and the unsafe one.

That is not a disappointing result. It is the correct result and it carries a real lesson: **you
cannot validate a constant-time property with a benchmark like this**, so anybody who shows you one
that "proves" the vulnerability on a laptop is showing you noise they liked the shape of.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because the endpoint compares a digest on every request, and this repository has two more days of
signature verification ahead of it in Phase 14. The habit — reach for `compare_digest` without
arguing about it — has to be installed once.

And because this repository has a rule about honesty in measurement (Principle 10) that is easiest
to break when the measurement would have been *rhetorically convenient*. A timing-attack
demonstration would have made this part more persuasive. It would also have been a lie about what
was measured, and part [2.3](../02-verifying-it/2.3-checked-against-the-vendor.md) has just spent a
whole document on the difference between evidence and self-agreement.

## The mechanism

The switch in the endpoint is one expression:

```python
            ok = hmac.compare_digest(expected, supplied) if constant_time else expected == supplied
```

**Line by line:**

- `hmac.compare_digest(a, b)` returns a `bool` and is documented to avoid the content-based short
  circuit. It still leaks *length*, which is why it is used on fixed-length hex digests rather than
  on arbitrary user input.
- Both arguments are `str` here, and `compare_digest` accepts either `str` (ASCII-only) or
  `bytes-like` — but not a mixture. Passing one of each raises `TypeError`, which is the loud and
  therefore good failure.
- `expected` is computed by this endpoint; `supplied` came from the request. Order does not matter
  for correctness, and putting the computed value first is a habit worth keeping for the schemes
  where it does.

And the measurement, which reports its own spread rather than a single number:

```python
    gaps = []
    for trial in range(1, TRIALS + 1):
        early, late = per_call_ns(EARLY, naive), per_call_ns(LATE, naive)
        gaps.append(late - early)
```

**Line by line:**

- Seven trials, each timing two hundred thousand comparisons. One trial would produce a number; the
  point of seven is to see whether the number means anything.
- `early` and `late` are measured **inside** the same trial, back to back, so a machine that gets
  busy halfway through affects both rather than biasing one.
- The two guesses differ from the real digest at the first character and at the last respectively —
  the maximum possible difference in work for a short-circuiting comparison.
- `gaps` keeps every trial rather than an average, because the interesting property is whether the
  sign is stable, and an average destroys exactly that.

Run it:

```bash

```

**Line by line:**

- No flag: the constant-time comparison, the one the endpoint actually uses.

Measured on 2026-09-07:

**From minus thirty-four to plus twenty-five nanoseconds, sign changing between trials.** The
per-call times swing from seventy-four to a hundred and twenty-six nanoseconds *for the same work*,
so a gap of a few nanoseconds is invisible inside that.

## When it breaks

Run the unsafe comparison and the picture does not improve:

```bash
uv run python timing.py --naive; echo "exit: $?"
```

The same sign-changing noise, from a run measured on 2026-09-07: differences of `-18.6`, `+6.7`,
`-3.8`, `-16.8`, `-13.0`, `-2.6`, `-8.7` nanoseconds. If anything the *majority* of trials show the
late-differing guess being rejected **faster**, which is the opposite of the effect being looked
for.

So the honest summary is: this experiment does not distinguish the two comparisons, in either
direction. Three things are in the way, and all three are structural rather than fixable by trying
harder:

- **The interpreter dominates.** A Python loop iteration costs tens of nanoseconds. The comparison
  being studied is a fraction of that, so the measurement is mostly timing the loop.
- **The scheduler is louder than the signal.** Per-call times vary by fifty nanoseconds between
  trials on an otherwise idle machine.
- **Sixty-four characters is short.** The maximum extra work a short-circuit can avoid is sixty-three
  character comparisons, which is genuinely tiny.

Real timing attacks are demonstrated with statistical treatment over enormous sample counts, on
quiet hardware, usually with the network removed from the picture. A laptop benchmark is not a small
version of that; it is a different thing that produces numbers.

**The conclusion does not depend on any of this.** `==` is not documented to take constant time, the
alternative is one function call, and the cost of using it is nothing. That argument was complete
before the first measurement and is unaffected by all of them — which is exactly why the script
exits `1` on the naive arm regardless of what the numbers did.

That is the part worth carrying: **a mitigation whose justification is documented rather than
measured is still justified.** Refusing to adopt it until you have personally observed the attack is
a way of being wrong with extra steps.

## In production

**What a professional does.** Uses the constant-time comparison for every secret comparison —
signatures, tokens, password hashes, reset codes — without benchmarking any of them, and spends the
attention saved on the failures that *can* be observed. The rule is cheap enough that it is applied
by policy, and a code review that asks "did you measure it?" about `compare_digest` has
misunderstood what kind of claim it is.

**What degrades at scale.** Nothing about this comparison. What changes is that the endpoint becomes
worth attacking, and remote timing attacks over a network are far harder than local ones — the
network jitter is larger than the generator in the story. Published work has still extracted
secrets across networks with enough samples, which is why "it is remote, so it is fine" is a
comforting argument rather than a sound one.

**The failure that only shows with real traffic.** The comparison is not the only thing that varies
with the input. An endpoint that returns `401` immediately for a malformed signature and does the
full hash for a well-formed one has a timing difference measured in *microseconds*, not nanoseconds
— thousands of times larger than anything on this page, and produced by the control flow rather than
the comparison. Getting `compare_digest` right and then branching early on a cheap check is a common
shape, and it is the version that is actually measurable.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Use `compare_digest` and do not put a benchmark in
the PR. If you want to spend time on timing, look at whether we return early before hashing —
that difference is microseconds and it is real."*

**The interview question:** *"Why not just use `==` to compare the signature?"* The answer that shows
experience explains the short circuit, names the function, and then — the part that separates people
who have read about it from people who have tried it — says that you should not expect to be able to
demonstrate the attack on your own machine, and that this is not a reason to skip the guard.

# 1.4 — The word that tells you nothing

## One-line answer

A word that appears in every document cannot tell any two of them apart, so weight each word by how
**rare** it is and the common words fall out of the score by themselves — no hand-written stopword
list required.

## The story

The lost-and-found desk at a railway station, on a Monday morning.

Six people are queuing and every one of them says the same first sentence: *I lost a bag.* The
clerk has heard it four hundred times this month. It is completely true, it is completely useless,
and if she wrote down "bag" for each of them she would have six identical entries.

So she does not write down "bag". She waits for the next sentence.

*It is black.* Better, but half the bags in the room are black. *It has a red zip.* Now she is
moving — she turns to the shelf, because in six hundred bags there are maybe four with a red zip.
*There is a school badge sewn on the front.* That is the one. She has it in her hand.

Nobody taught her a list of banned words. She learned which words are worth something by hearing
them: *bag* every day, *red zip* sometimes, *school badge sewn on the front* almost never. The
rarer the description, the more it narrows the shelf.

And notice what makes a word common — it is not the word itself, it is the room. In the lost-and-found
room, "bag" is worthless. In a hardware shop it would be the most useful word anyone said all day.

## The idea in plain language

The counting from [1.2](1.2-a-ticket-as-a-list-of-numbers.md) treats every word as equally
important. It is not. In this day's archive, the word `the` appears in all eight tickets, so knowing
that a query contains `the` tells you nothing about which ticket to return — exactly like "bag" at
the lost-and-found desk.

The repair has a name and it is one line of arithmetic.

**Document frequency** of a word is the number of documents that contain it — not how many times in
total, just how many documents. `the` has a document frequency of 8 out of 8. `samesite` has a
document frequency of 1.

**Inverse document frequency**, or **idf**, turns that around:

```
idf(word) = log(total documents / documents containing the word)
```

Work through the two ends of it, because the behaviour is the whole point:

- a word in **every** document: `log(8 / 8)` = `log(1)` = **exactly 0.0**;
- a word in **one** document out of eight: `log(8 / 1)` = `log(8)` ≈ **2.079**;
- a word in half of them: `log(8 / 4)` = `log(2)` ≈ **0.693**.

Then multiply each of a document's word counts by that word's idf. The result is called **tf-idf** —
*term frequency times inverse document frequency* — and it is the oldest genuinely good idea in
information retrieval.

Two consequences, both worth saying out loud.

**A word in every document contributes exactly zero.** Not "a little", not "less" — zero, because
its weight is multiplied in and the weight is `0.0`. Stopwords remove themselves. You did not have
to write a list, and — critically — the list you *would* have written would have been wrong, because
`the` and `and` are not the only useless words in a ticket archive. `fix` and `kb` are useless too,
in this archive, and no general-purpose stopword list contains them.

**Rarity is measured against your own collection.** This is the lost-and-found clerk's insight and it
is why tf-idf beats a fixed list. Move the same archive into a company where every ticket is about
billing and the word `invoice` becomes worthless overnight, automatically, with no code change.

The logarithm is there so that rarity has diminishing returns: a word in 1 document out of 10,000 is
more useful than one in 100, but not a hundred times more useful. Without the log, a single typo
appearing once would dominate every score it touched.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because without it the desk's retrieval is decided by the word `the`.

That is not a figure of speech. In [2.1](../02-the-meaning-test/2.1-the-score-that-came-back-zero.md)
you will see the incoming ticket 4521 score `0.100` against its true match under raw counts, and the
only reason it is not zero is `the`. Weighting removes that noise, and what is left is an honest
measurement of how much real vocabulary two tickets share — which turns out to be **nothing**, and
that honest zero is the finding the rest of the day is built on.

Weighting is also the thing that makes a **cutoff** possible. A score built mostly out of common
words never reaches zero and never reaches one; everything sits in a grey band and no threshold can
be drawn. This day's first paper demo measures exactly that: with weighting, one document is above
the cutoff and it is the right one; without it, four are, and three are wrong.

## The paper behind it

> **A vector space model for automatic indexing** · `doi:10.1145/361219.361220` · 1975 ·
> `https://doi.org/10.1145/361219.361220`

Beyond representing documents as vectors, it argued that the **weight** given to a term should
reflect how well that term separates documents from one another, rather than treating all terms
alike.

Read it after the parts: [`papers/01-vector-space-model.md`](../../papers/01-vector-space-model.md).

## The mechanism

Two more functions, both still stdlib only.

```python
# days/day-49-retrieval-and-embeddings/lab/_archive.py
def idf(documents: list[str]) -> dict[str, float]:
    """How rare each word is across the archive. A word in every ticket scores 0.0."""
    total = len(documents)
    seen: dict[str, int] = {}
    for text in documents:
        for word in set(tokenize(text)):
            seen[word] = seen.get(word, 0) + 1
    return {word: math.log(total / n) for word, n in seen.items()}


def tfidf(text: str, weights: dict[str, float]) -> dict[str, float]:
    """A term-count vector with every count multiplied by that word's rarity."""
    return {word: count * weights.get(word, 0.0) for word, count in counts(text).items()}
```

**Line by line:**

- `for word in set(tokenize(text))` — the `set` is essential and it is the difference between
  document frequency and total frequency. A ticket that says `the` four times still counts as **one**
  document containing `the`. Drop the `set` and a single repetitive document would make a word look
  common across the whole archive.
- `seen` counts documents, not occurrences, which is why its values are `int` while everything else
  in this day is `float`.
- `math.log(total / n)` uses the natural logarithm. Base 10 or base 2 would work equally well and
  give different absolute numbers with identical rankings, because changing the base multiplies every
  weight by the same constant and cosine is scale-free ([1.3](1.3-direction-not-size.md)). Pick one
  and never change it, because the numbers in your dashboards will move if you do.
- `weights.get(word, 0.0)` in `tfidf` is the quiet, important line. A word in the **query** that
  appears in no indexed document has no weight, so it is given `0.0` and contributes nothing. Without
  the default this raises `KeyError` on the first unusual query a user types. This is also the correct
  behaviour rather than a convenient one: a word the archive has never seen genuinely carries no
  information about which archived document to return.
- `idf` takes the whole document list. That is a real coupling and it is worth naming now: **the
  weights belong to the collection, not to the document.** Add a ticket and every weight moves, so
  every stored vector is stale. [5.2](../05-when-retrieval-lies/5.2-the-index-nobody-rebuilt.md)
  measures that.

**Line by line:**

- `weights.py` computes `idf` over the eight tickets and prints the six lowest-weighted words, then

- **Zero model calls.**

Measured on 2026-09-05:

```text
  kb                8           0.000
  the               8           0.000
  330               2           1.386

```

Three weights are exactly `0.000`, and only one of them is a word any stopword list would have
caught.

`the` is obvious. `fix` and `kb` are not — they are zero because **every ticket in this archive ends
with "Fix KB-nnn"**, which is a house convention, not a fact about English. A hand-written stopword
list would have kept both, and every query containing the word "fix" — which is most of them, since
support agents ask what the fix was — would have matched all eight tickets on that word alone. The
archive told the weighting what was useless. Nobody had to know in advance.

At the other end, `cookie`, `blank` and `samesite` each appear in exactly one ticket and carry the
maximum weight this archive can give, `log(8) ≈ 2.079`. A query containing `samesite` is almost a
lookup: one word, one document.

## When it breaks

**A small collection makes idf noisy, and the noise looks like a signal.**

Eight documents is a tiny collection, and with a tiny collection a word that happens to appear once
gets the maximum weight whether or not it means anything. Here is that failure, measured, from
[4.4](../04-the-adk-socket/4.4-the-cap-that-finally-means-something.md)'s run:

```bash
cd days/day-49-retrieval-and-embeddings/lab
uv run python -c "from _archive import *; q='auth redirect leaves the user on a sign-in screen'; w=idf([t for _,t in ARCHIVE]); print([(x, round(w[x],3)) for x in sorted(set(tokenize(q)) & set(tokenize(body('4188'))))])"
```

```text
[('on', 2.079), ('the', 0.0)]
```

That query ranked ticket 4188 second out of eight and looked like a success. It was not. The two
words it shares with 4188 are `on` and `the`; `the` contributes nothing, so the **entire** score came
from the preposition `on`, which happens to appear in exactly one ticket in this archive and
therefore carries the highest weight available. The right answer was found by a coincidence about a
preposition.

This is the honest limitation of weighting on small data, and it is why the numbers in this day are
always read as *rankings and gaps* rather than as absolute quantities. It also means a system's
retrieval quality genuinely improves as the archive grows, which is the opposite of the precision
story in [5.1](../05-when-retrieval-lies/5.1-nearest-is-not-near.md) and both are true at once.

**The second break is a word that appears nowhere.** Add `weights[word]` instead of
`weights.get(word, 0.0)` and the first query containing a new product name gives:

```text
KeyError: 'samesite'
```

— not from the archive's words, but from any query word the index has never seen. It is an easy line
to write and an easy failure to reproduce: search for a word you have just invented.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead of the teaching version.** They use **BM25**, which is tf-idf
with two corrections that matter on real text. The first is **saturation**: under plain tf-idf a
document that says "invoice" twenty times scores twenty times as much on that word, which is
nonsense, so BM25 makes the contribution flatten out after the first few occurrences. The second is
**length normalisation with a tuning knob** rather than the fixed division cosine performs, because
different corpora want different amounts of it. Both Elasticsearch and OpenSearch default to BM25,
and it remains the baseline every dense retrieval paper has to beat.

They also **store the weights in the index file, alongside the vectors**, exactly as
[1.5](1.5-the-work-you-do-once.md) does, because a query cannot be scored without the same weights
the documents were scored with. An index without its weights is unusable, and the failure mode when
they drift apart is silent.

**What degrades at scale or under concurrency.** The weights themselves become the bottleneck.
Recomputing idf over a growing archive means reading every document, so a production system computes
it incrementally, or on a schedule, or accepts a slightly stale set of weights. Under concurrency the
danger is a **partial rebuild**: half the index written with new weights and half with old, which
produces scores that are not comparable to each other and a ranking that is quietly meaningless. That
is why the index in [1.5](1.5-the-work-you-do-once.md) is written as one file in one write.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"You are computing idf on every search. That reads
the whole corpus per query and it also means the weights change under a user mid-session — the same
query returns a different order after somebody files a ticket. Compute the weights when the index is
built, write them into the index file, and version the file so we can tell which weights a score
came from."*

**The interview question:** *"What is tf-idf and why not just use a stopword list?"* An honest
answer: *"tf-idf weights each word by how rare it is in your collection — the count multiplied by
`log(total documents / documents containing the word)` — so a word in every document contributes
exactly zero and removes itself. A stopword list can only remove the words you thought of. In my
archive the three zero-weight words were `the`, `fix` and `kb`, and the last two were zero because
every ticket ends with 'Fix KB-something'. That is a house convention no English stopword list
contains, and it would have made the word 'fix' match all eight tickets. The collection knows which
of its words are worthless; a list written by a person does not. In production I would use BM25,
which is the same idea with saturation and a tunable length normalisation."*

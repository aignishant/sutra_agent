# 1.2 — A ticket as a list of numbers

## One-line answer

Counting how often each word appears turns any piece of text into a list of numbers over a fixed
list of words, and that list is already a vector — no model, no training, no library.

## The story

Two grocery bills, from the same shop, two different weeks.

You want to know whether you shopped for the same kind of week. Not whether the bills are identical
— they never are — but whether they are the *same sort of shopping*. A festival week looks different
from an ordinary one. A week when guests came looks different again.

Reading the two bills top to bottom does not answer it. The items are in whatever order the person
at the till scanned them, one bill has fourteen lines and the other has nine, and half the lines are
the same three things bought twice.

So you do what anybody does. You take a sheet of paper, you write down every item that appears on
either bill, one per line, and beside each one you write how many of it each bill had.

```
                week 1   week 2
rice               1        1
oil                1        0
sugar              0        2
milk               4        3
biscuits           2        0
tea                1        1
```

Now the two weeks are two columns of numbers over the same list of rows, and the question *"were
these the same kind of week?"* has become a question about two columns. Which is a question you can
actually answer.

The important move was not the counting. It was **agreeing on the rows first** — one fixed list of
items, in one fixed order, used for both bills. Without that the two columns cannot be compared at
all.

## The idea in plain language

That sheet of paper is a **term-count vector**, and building one is the whole of this part.

The vocabulary of a collection of documents is every distinct word that appears anywhere in it. In
the grocery example the vocabulary is `rice, oil, sugar, milk, biscuits, tea` — the rows of the
sheet. In a ticket archive it is every word any ticket used.

A document's vector is then one number per vocabulary word: how many times that document used it.
A word the document never used gets a `0`. The word "vector" is doing no work beyond that: it is a
list of numbers in an agreed order.

Two things follow immediately, and they are worth stating because both surprise people.

**The order of the words in the document is thrown away.** "The cookie dropped the browser" and
"The browser dropped the cookie" produce exactly the same vector. This is called a **bag of words**
— you tipped the document into a bag and shook it, and all you kept was what was in there and how
much. It is a real loss. It is also why this approach is so cheap, and it is enough for a great deal
of work.

**Almost every number is zero.** The archive in this day has eight tickets and eighty distinct
words, so every ticket's vector is eighty numbers long and about seventy of them are zero. Storing
eighty numbers when thirteen are non-zero is wasteful, so the code stores a **sparse vector**: a
dictionary holding only the words the document actually used. `{"invoice": 1.0, "pdf": 1.0}` and
`[0, 0, 1, 0, ..., 1, 0]` are the same vector written two ways.

One more definition, because the code below uses it. **Tokenising** is splitting a piece of text
into the units you will count. It sounds trivial and it is a decision with consequences: whether
`KB-104` is one token or two, whether `Sign-in` and `sign in` are the same thing, whether case
matters. This day tokenises exactly the way ADK's own memory service does, so that the two can be
compared fairly, and you will see the consequences of that choice in
[2.1](../02-the-meaning-test/2.1-the-score-that-came-back-zero.md).

## Why Sutra needs it

Because Sutra never adopts a mechanism it has not built by hand first (Principle 4), and the vector
is the mechanism underneath every retrieval system in existence — including the embedding lane in
[section 3](../03-meaning-as-geometry/3.1-the-dish-you-know-by-taste.md) and every managed vector
store named in [6.1](../06-in-production/6.1-the-store-we-did-not-build.md).

When you meet `VertexAiRagMemoryService` and its `similarity_top_k` argument, or a pgvector column
of type `vector(768)`, the only difference from what you are about to write is where the numbers
came from. The scoring, the sorting and the cutting are identical. Day 46 parked those managed
services in
[6.2](../../../day-46-sessions-vs-memory/parts/06-in-production/6.2-the-two-services-we-park.md)
precisely so that this day could explain their arguments rather than accept them.

## The paper behind it

> **A vector space model for automatic indexing** · `doi:10.1145/361219.361220` · 1975 ·
> `https://doi.org/10.1145/361219.361220`

It proposed that documents and queries be represented as vectors over a shared term space, so that
retrieval becomes a geometric comparison rather than an exact match.

Read it after the parts: [`papers/01-vector-space-model.md`](../../papers/01-vector-space-model.md).

## The mechanism

Two functions. Neither imports anything but `re` and `math`.

```python
# days/day-49-retrieval-and-embeddings/lab/_archive.py
def tokenize(text: str) -> list[str]:
    """Split text into lowercase words, the same way ADK's memory service does."""
    return [word.lower() for word in re.findall(r"\w+", text, re.UNICODE)]


def counts(text: str) -> dict[str, float]:
    """One document as a sparse vector: every word it uses, and how often."""
    vector: dict[str, float] = {}
    for word in tokenize(text):
        vector[word] = vector.get(word, 0.0) + 1.0
    return vector
```

**Line by line:**

- `re.findall(r"\w+", text, re.UNICODE)` is copied deliberately, character for character, from
  `_extract_words_lower` in
  `.venv/Lib/site-packages/google/adk/memory/in_memory_memory_service.py`. Using the same tokenizer
  as the thing you are trying to beat is the only way the comparison in
  [4.4](../04-the-adk-socket/4.4-the-cap-that-finally-means-something.md) means anything. If the
  two split words differently, any difference in the results could be the tokenizer rather than the
  method.
- `\w+` means "one or more letters, digits or underscores". It **splits on the hyphen**, so
  `KB-104` becomes the two tokens `kb` and `104`, and `sign-in` becomes `sign` and `in`. Remember
  that: it comes back in [2.2](../02-the-meaning-test/2.2-the-repairs-that-fix-one-pair.md).
- `word.lower()` makes `The` and `the` the same word. Without it a capitalised sentence opening
  would be a different token from the same word mid-sentence, and every document would look more
  distinctive than it is.
- ADK's version wraps the result in `set(...)`; this one does not, because **counting needs the
  repeats**. That single difference is the entire distance between ADK's matcher and a vector: ADK
  asks *does this word appear*, and a vector asks *how many times*.
- `counts` returns a `dict[str, float]`, not `dict[str, int]`. The values become fractions the
  moment [1.4](1.4-the-word-that-tells-you-nothing.md) multiplies them by a weight, and starting
  with floats avoids a silent integer-division bug later.
- `vector.get(word, 0.0) + 1.0` is the sparse part: a word that never appears never gets a key, so
  the dictionary holds only the non-zero numbers.


Three things to look at in that output.

**Fourteen tokens, thirteen axes.** The difference is `the`, which appears twice. That `2` is the
only number in the whole vector that is not a `1`, and — read the ticket again — it is the least
informative word in the sentence. That observation is [1.4](1.4-the-word-that-tells-you-nothing.md)
in one line, and it is the first crack in the counting approach.

**`KB-201` became `kb` and `201`.** The tokenizer split it, so the article number is now two
unrelated tokens. A query for `KB-201` will match any ticket containing `kb` — which is all of them.

**Sixty-seven zeros out of eighty.** On eight tickets that is already 84% waste. On a real archive
the vocabulary runs to tens of thousands of words while a ticket still uses twenty, so the dense
form would be over 99% zeros. This is why every real term-based index is stored sparsely, usually as
an **inverted index**: a map from each word to the documents containing it, which is the data
structure a search engine is built on and which
[6.1](../06-in-production/6.1-the-store-we-did-not-build.md) measures the absence of.

## When it breaks

The bag-of-words loss is easy to state and hard to feel until you see it. Two sentences that mean
opposite things:

```bash
cd days/day-49-retrieval-and-embeddings/lab
uv run python -c "from _archive import counts; a = counts('the cookie dropped the browser'); b = counts('the browser dropped the cookie'); print(a == b)"
```

That prints `True`. Two sentences with different meanings have identical vectors, and no amount of
weighting or scoring later in this day can recover the difference, because the information was
destroyed at the counting step.

The second break is the tokenizer, and it is the one that will actually cost you an evening. Because
`\w+` splits on punctuation:

- `KB-104` and `KB 104` and `kb104` are three different things. The first two produce `kb` + `104`;
  the third produces the single token `kb104`, which matches neither.
- `SameSite` stays one token, but `Same-Site` becomes two, and a ticket written the second way will
  not match a query written the first way.
- An email address, a URL and a stack trace all shatter into dozens of near-useless tokens, each of
  which is now a word in your vocabulary inflating the size of every vector.

None of these raise an error. They produce a slightly worse score, silently, for the lifetime of the
system. When a retrieval system mysteriously misses a document that obviously contains the phrase,
the tokenizer is the first place to look, and the way to look is to print `tokenize(document)` and
`tokenize(query)` side by side and read them.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead of the teaching version.** They do not write `counts` at all.
Term frequency, document frequency and the inverted index are what Lucene — and therefore
Elasticsearch and OpenSearch — has done for two decades, and the scoring function they use is not
raw counts but **BM25**, a refinement of the weighting in [1.4](1.4-the-word-that-tells-you-nothing.md)
that adds two things this day does not: saturation, so the tenth occurrence of a word adds much less
than the second, and length normalisation tuned by a parameter rather than fixed by the geometry.
BM25 is the direct descendant of the model in this day's first paper and it is still the strongest
purely term-based baseline in the field.

What a professional also does, and a tutorial never does, is **pin and version the tokenizer**.
Changing how text is split changes every vector in the index, which means every score, which means
every ranking. A tokenizer change is an index rebuild, and shipping one without a rebuild leaves
documents indexed under one set of tokens being searched with another.

**What degrades at scale.** Vocabulary growth. Eight tickets gave eighty words; the vocabulary of a
large archive grows roughly with the square root of the text and never stops, because product names,
customer names, error codes and typos are all words. Dense vectors become impossible, sparse ones
stay fine, and the failure — if anyone builds this densely — is memory rather than a wrong answer.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Your index and your query path call two different
tokenizers. The indexer lowercases and strips punctuation; the query path only lowercases. That is
why `KB-104` never matches. Both sides must call the same function, and that function needs a
version number stored in the index file so we know when a rebuild is due."*

**The interview question:** *"What does a bag-of-words representation throw away, and when does that
matter?"* An honest answer: *"Word order, and therefore everything that depends on it — negation,
who did what to whom, and any phrase whose meaning is not the sum of its words. I checked it
directly: 'the cookie dropped the browser' and 'the browser dropped the cookie' produce byte-identical
vectors. It matters most for short texts and for queries containing 'not'. It matters much less for
whole documents, because a long document about one subject uses that subject's words repeatedly no
matter what order they are in, which is why bag-of-words search worked well enough to run the web for
years."*

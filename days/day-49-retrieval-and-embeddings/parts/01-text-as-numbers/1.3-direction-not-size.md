# 1.3 — Direction, not size

## One-line answer

Score two documents by the **angle** between their vectors rather than by how big the overlap is, or
the longest complaint wins every time regardless of what it is about.

## The story

Two customers report the same fault on the same morning.

The first one writes: *invoice pdf empty*. Three words. She is busy, she has reported things before,
and she knows that is all anybody needs.

The second one writes four sentences. He apologises for bothering you. He explains that he clicked
print, and that the PDF opened, and that the PDF was empty, and that this is the third time this
week, and he mentions the invoice twice more before he finishes.

Both are describing exactly the same problem, and one of them is fifteen times longer than the
other.

Now put yourself at the desk with a hundred of these in front of you and a rule that says *whoever
gave the most matching detail gets handled first*. You will work down that list from the longest
complaint to the shortest, and you will believe you are working by relevance. You are working by
verbosity. The person who typed three exact words goes to the bottom, under a long complaint about
something else entirely that happened to use a lot of the same ordinary words.

## The idea in plain language

Once two documents are lists of numbers ([1.2](1.2-a-ticket-as-a-list-of-numbers.md)), there is an
obvious way to score how much they have in common: multiply the two lists together position by
position and add up the results. That is called the **dot product**, and it is one line of code:

```
dot = sum(a[word] * b[word] for word in shared_words)
```

It is also wrong for this job, for exactly the reason the story gives. A long document has bigger
numbers in more positions, so its dot product with *anything* is larger. The dot product measures
overlap **and** size together, and it cannot tell you which of the two it is reporting.

The repair is to divide by the sizes. The **length** of a vector is the square root of the sum of
its squared numbers — the same Pythagoras you would use for the distance from a corner of a room to
the opposite corner, extended to more than three numbers. Divide the dot product by both vectors'
lengths and you get the **cosine similarity**:

```
cosine = dot(a, b) / (length(a) * length(b))
```

The name comes from geometry: for vectors made of non-negative numbers, that expression is exactly
the cosine of the angle between them. What matters is the behaviour, and it is worth memorising:

| Cosine | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| `1.0` | the two vectors point in the same direction — the same words in the same proportions |
| `0.0` | they share no word at all; the vectors are at right angles |
| in between | some shared words, weighted by how much of each document they are |

The useful sentence is: **cosine measures direction and ignores length.** Doubling every number in a
vector — writing the same complaint twice — does not change its cosine with anything, because both
the dot product and the length double and they cancel.

That is why the answer to *"which of these documents is most like my question?"* is a cosine and not
a dot product, and it is the sentence that turns up in every vector database's documentation as
"cosine distance".

## Why Sutra needs it

Because Sutra's archive is exactly the shape that breaks the dot product. Support tickets vary
enormously in length — a one-line summary sits beside a four-paragraph escalation — and the length
has nothing at all to do with which one answers today's question.

It matters again in [section 3](../03-meaning-as-geometry/3.1-the-dish-you-know-by-taste.md), where
the numbers stop being word counts and start coming from an embedding model. The scoring code does
not change. Cosine over a hand-built vector and cosine over a model's vector are the same function,
which is why [3.3](../03-meaning-as-geometry/3.3-the-scale-does-not-know-what-it-weighs.md) can swap
one for the other without touching the ranking.

And it is the number that finally answers Day 46. Day 46's
[5.1](../../../day-46-sessions-vs-memory/parts/05-failure-lab/5.1-the-past-that-matched-on-nothing.md)
ended on *"the absence of a score is the root problem"*. This is the score.

## The paper behind it

> **A vector space model for automatic indexing** · `doi:10.1145/361219.361220` · 1975 ·
> `https://doi.org/10.1145/361219.361220`

It argued that similarity between a document and a query should be measured as the closeness of two
vectors in a shared term space — an angle — rather than as the presence or absence of matching
terms.

Read it after the parts: [`papers/01-vector-space-model.md`](../../papers/01-vector-space-model.md).

## The mechanism

```python
# days/day-49-retrieval-and-embeddings/lab/_archive.py
def cosine(a: dict[str, float], b: dict[str, float]) -> float:
    """Cosine similarity of two sparse vectors: 1.0 same direction, 0.0 nothing shared."""
    shared = a.keys() & b.keys()
    dot = sum(a[word] * b[word] for word in shared)
    size_a = math.sqrt(sum(value * value for value in a.values()))
    size_b = math.sqrt(sum(value * value for value in b.values()))
    if size_a == 0.0 or size_b == 0.0:
        return 0.0
    return dot / (size_a * size_b)
```

**Line by line:**

- `a.keys() & b.keys()` is a set intersection over the two dictionaries' keys. Only shared words can
  contribute to the dot product, because every other position has a zero on one side and anything
  times zero is zero. Iterating the intersection instead of the whole vocabulary is what makes the
  sparse representation fast.
- `sum(a[word] * b[word] for word in shared)` is the dot product, and it is the *only* line that
  looks at both documents together. Everything else is per-document.
- The two `math.sqrt` lines compute each vector's length **over all its values**, not just the
  shared ones. This is the step people get wrong: normalising by the shared part would defeat the
  whole purpose, because a long document's unshared words are exactly what should count against it.
- `if size_a == 0.0 or size_b == 0.0: return 0.0` guards the empty vector. A query of pure
  punctuation tokenises to nothing, and without this line the function raises
  `ZeroDivisionError: float division by zero` on a perfectly ordinary input. Returning `0.0` is
  honest here: an empty query is similar to nothing, which is true.
- There is no `numpy` and no `import` beyond `math`. The whole of "AI search" scoring is these seven
  lines, and knowing that is worth more than knowing which library wraps it.

Now the two complaints from the story, scored against the ticket that answers them and against a
different ticket that does not:

```python
# days/day-49-retrieval-and-embeddings/lab/angle.py
SHORT = "invoice pdf empty"
LONG = (
    "Hello, I am sorry to bother you again about this, but the invoice will not print "
    "at all. I click print, the PDF opens, and the PDF is completely empty. This is the "
    "third time this week that the invoice has been empty when the PDF opens."
)
RIGHT = "4402"  # cannot print an invoice; the PDF opens empty
WRONG = "4508"  # the nightly export finishes but the file is empty
```

**Line by line:**

- `SHORT` and `LONG` are the same report. Nothing in `LONG` is about a different fault; it is
  politeness, repetition and context.
- `RIGHT` and `WRONG` are both real archive rows and both contain the word `empty`. `WRONG` is
  chosen precisely because it shares an ordinary word with the complaint while being about something
  else — a nightly export, not an invoice. That is the realistic near-miss.
- The comparison is deliberately **two complaints against two documents**, four numbers, rather than
  one complaint against one document. One number proves nothing; the question is what happens when
  all four go into a single ranked list.

Run both arms:


**Line by line:**

- The default arm scores with `cosine`.
- `--dot` is the ablation: it swaps in the raw dot product and changes nothing else — same texts,

- **Zero model calls in either arm.**

Measured on 2026-09-05:

```text
scoring by: cosine

4402 (the right answer): Cannot print an invoice from the portal; the PDF opens empty. Fix KB-201.
4508 (a different fault): Nightly export finishes but the file is empty. Fix KB-512.

complaint   words   length   vs 4402   vs 4508
short           3     1.73     0.433     0.174
long           47    10.05     0.572     0.330

the long complaint against 4508 scores 0.330
the short complaint against 4402 scores 0.433
one ranked list over both would put: RIGHT over WRONG
```

```text
scoring by: raw dot product

4402 (the right answer): Cannot print an invoice from the portal; the PDF opens empty. Fix KB-201.
4508 (a different fault): Nightly export finishes but the file is empty. Fix KB-512.

complaint   words   length   vs 4402   vs 4508
short           3     1.73     3.000     1.000
long           47    10.05    23.000    11.000

the long complaint against 4508 scores 11.000
the short complaint against 4402 scores 3.000
one ranked list over both would put: WRONG over RIGHT
```

Read the last line of each run. That is the whole part.

Under the **dot product** the long complaint's score against the *wrong* ticket is `11.000` and the
short complaint's score against the *right* ticket is `3.000`. Put those four pairs in one ranked
list and a wrong match on a wordy report beats a right match on a terse one, by a factor of nearly
four. The list is sorted by how much somebody typed.

Under **cosine** the same four pairs give `0.330` for the wrong-and-wordy and `0.433` for the
right-and-terse. The order is correct.

Look at the `length` column to see the mechanism. `10.05` against `1.73` is a factor of 5.8, and the
dot products differ by roughly the same factor. Dividing by the lengths is what removes it. Notice
too that cosine does not remove the length effect *entirely* — the long complaint still scores
`0.572` against 4402, above the short one's `0.433`, because repeating "invoice" and "empty" genuinely
is more evidence about the subject. Cosine removes the part of the advantage that comes from bulk
and keeps the part that comes from emphasis. That is the right behaviour, and it is not the same as
"length no longer matters".

## When it breaks

The empty vector is the break you will meet first, and it happens on input a user really types.

```bash
cd days/day-49-retrieval-and-embeddings/lab
uv run python -c "from _archive import counts, cosine, body; print(cosine(counts('???'), counts(body('4402'))))"
```

```text
0.0
```

With the guard, `0.0`. Delete the guard and the same call raises:

```text
ZeroDivisionError: float division by zero
```

Neither is an error in the retrieval sense — the user typed three question marks — but one of them
crashes the desk mid-turn and one of them returns a score. Principle 10 says errors must surface
rather than be swallowed, and this is the case where the honest answer genuinely is a number: an
empty query has no direction, so it points at nothing, so its similarity to everything is zero. What
must **not** happen is the third option, returning `1.0` or skipping the guard and letting the
exception escape into a tool call.

The second break is subtler and it is the reason cosine is not the last word. Cosine is
scale-free, which means it cannot tell a document that mentions your subject once from a document
that is *about* your subject, if the two are the same length. A one-line ticket that says "cookie"
and a one-line ticket that is entirely about cookies score identically. Fixing that needs term
weighting, which is [1.4](1.4-the-word-that-tells-you-nothing.md).

## In production

**What a professional writes instead of the teaching version.** They store **pre-normalised**
vectors. Since the length of every indexed document never changes, dividing by it once at index time
turns every later score into a plain dot product, which is both faster and less error-prone. Every
serious vector store does this, and several embedding endpoints do it for you: the Ollama
documentation states that `/api/embed` *"returns L2-normalized (unit-length) vectors"*, which means
the cosine of two of its outputs is their dot product with no division at all. Read that sentence
carefully when you meet it, because it changes what your scoring code should do.

They also **choose the metric deliberately and write down why**. Cosine, Euclidean distance and dot
product give different rankings, and a vector store makes you pick one at index creation time —
pgvector's operator classes, FAISS's index factory string, Qdrant's `Distance` enum. Picking cosine
because it was the default and then storing un-normalised vectors is a real and common bug.

**What degrades at scale or under concurrency.** Nothing about cosine itself degrades; it is seven
lines and it is exact. What degrades is doing it in Python once per row. At 20,000 rows of dense
768-number vectors this day measures **876.6 ms** for one query, which is a whole second of one CPU
per question ([6.1](../06-in-production/6.1-the-store-we-did-not-build.md) has the table). Under
concurrency that becomes a queue, and the first symptom is not a wrong answer but a timeout in a
tool call, which [Day 44](../../../day-44-client-hardening/LESSON.md) taught you to recognise.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"We normalise at query time but not at index time,
so we recompute every stored vector's length on every single search. Normalise once when the row is
written, store the unit vector, and make the score a dot product. Also, our score is now
un-comparable with the one in the old index file — bump the index version so nobody mixes them."*

**The interview question:** *"Why cosine similarity rather than a dot product or Euclidean
distance?"* An honest answer: *"Because document length is not evidence about the subject and the
dot product cannot separate the two. I measured it on two reports of the same fault, one three words
long and one forty-seven: under the dot product the long report scored 11 against a ticket about a
different fault, and the short one scored 3 against the ticket that actually answered it, so a
single ranked list put the wrong answer first. Under cosine it was 0.330 against 0.433 and the order
was right. Euclidean distance has the same problem in a different coat — a long document sits far
from everything simply because it is long. In production I would normalise the vectors at index time
and use a dot product, which is the same thing computed once instead of on every query."*

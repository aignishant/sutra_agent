# 5.1 — 💥 Nearest is not near

## One-line answer

Asked about SOC 2 evidence — a subject the archive has never covered once — the retriever returned a
**refund ticket at `0.369`**, higher than any score it gave the question it could genuinely answer's
second and third results, and a `0.20` cutoff did not remove it.

## The story

Three in the morning, and you open the taxi app.

It finds you a car. It always finds you a car. The screen says the nearest driver is on his way, and
you stand on the pavement and watch a little icon crawl towards you, and after nine minutes you
realise it has not got much closer, and you look properly and the car is on the other side of the
city.

At three in the afternoon there are eleven cars within two minutes of you and *nearest* means what
you think it means. At three in the morning there are two cars in the whole city and the app said
exactly the same word.

It did not lie. It found the nearest one. What it never said — what there was no room on the screen
to say — is *there is nothing near you*.

## The idea in plain language

Every retriever in this day, and every vector store in production, answers the same question:
**which of these documents is closest to the query?**

That question always has an answer. If the archive has eight tickets, one of them is closest, even
when all eight are about completely different subjects. Sort and take three, and you get three, with
scores on them, formatted exactly like a good result.

This is the deep asymmetry between a lookup and retrieval, and
[1.1](../01-text-as-numbers/1.1-the-counter-that-takes-a-description.md) put it on the table in the
first part: a lookup gives you `KeyError` for free, and retrieval gives you *nothing found* only if
you build it.

The obvious repair is a **threshold**: a minimum score below which a result is discarded. `TOP_K`
says *at most this many*; a threshold says *only if this good*. They are different controls and you
need both.

The uncomfortable part of this part is that the threshold does not work as well as it should, and
this is where the day gets honest. The number `0.369` in the one-line answer is a **wrong** result on
a question the archive cannot answer, and it is higher than the `0.100` a **right** result got on a
question the archive answers perfectly. There is no single cutoff that keeps one and drops the other.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because Day 46's memory story ends in the wrong place without this part.

Day 46's

Rule 4 of Day 46's policy — a miss is *reported, not interpreted* — was written for an empty result.
This part is where you find out that under retrieval, **the empty result never arrives on its own**,
and the rule needs code behind it.

## The mechanism

Four questions. One the archive can answer, three it cannot.

```python
# days/day-49-retrieval-and-embeddings/lab/nearest.py
TOP_K = 3
THRESHOLD = 0.20  # chosen below, from the numbers this same script prints

ABSENT = [
    "we need SOC 2 evidence for a security questionnaire",
    "the barcode scanner in the warehouse app crashes on Android 15",
    "can we get an invoice in a different currency",
]
```

**Line by line:**

- `ABSENT` is three questions about subjects the archive genuinely does not contain. Read the eight
  tickets in `_archive.py` and confirm it: there is nothing about compliance evidence, nothing about
  a warehouse app or Android, and nothing about currency.
- They are chosen at **three distances**. The first shares almost no vocabulary with anything. The
  second shares ordinary words like `app` and `crashes`. The third shares `invoice`, which is a real
  archive word. Three degrees of near-miss, which is what makes the threshold question interesting
  rather than obvious.
- `THRESHOLD = 0.20` is stated as a constant with a comment admitting where it came from: reading
  this script's own output. That is honest — thresholds are always chosen by looking at data — and it
  is also the weakness [Day 50](../../../day-50-chunking-and-top-k/LESSON.md) exists to address, by
  choosing it from a judgement set instead of by eye.

Run both arms:

```bash
cd days/day-49-retrieval-and-embeddings/lab
uv run python nearest.py
uv run python nearest.py --threshold
```

**Line by line:**

- The default arm is what [4.2](../04-the-adk-socket/4.2-same-tool-new-answer.md)'s service actually
  does: sort, take three, return them.
- `--threshold` adds one filter — drop anything below `0.20` — and changes nothing else.
- **Zero model calls in either arm.**

Measured on 2026-09-05, without the threshold:

```text
archive: 8 tickets, all of them about this product
cap: top 3

a question the archive CAN answer: 'invoice pdf opens empty'
   0.589  4402  Cannot print an invoice from the portal; the PDF opens emp
   0.100  4508  Nightly export finishes but the file is empty. Fix KB-512.
   0.081  4533  Invoice total disagrees with the plan price; rounding in t
    returned: 3

a question the archive CANNOT answer: 'we need SOC 2 evidence for a security questionnaire'
   0.369  4455  Refund requested for the annual plan after two months. Fix
   0.000  4188  Login loop on the staff portal. Session cookie written wit
   0.000  4239  Password reset mail never reaches the customer. Their gate
    returned: 3

a question the archive CANNOT answer: 'the barcode scanner in the warehouse app crashes on Android 15'
   0.212  4571  Mobile app keeps showing the old profile photo. Fix KB-402
   0.180  4533  Invoice total disagrees with the plan price; rounding in t
   0.152  4188  Login loop on the staff portal. Session cookie written wit
    returned: 3

a question the archive CANNOT answer: 'can we get an invoice in a different currency'
   0.320  4402  Cannot print an invoice from the portal; the PDF opens emp
   0.287  4533  Invoice total disagrees with the plan price; rounding in t
   0.000  4188  Login loop on the staff portal. Session cookie written wit
    returned: 3
```

Four questions, twelve results, `returned: 3` every single time. Nothing was empty. Nothing errored.

**The SOC 2 row is the one to sit with.** `0.369` on a refund ticket, for a question about a security
questionnaire. That is the **second-highest score anywhere in this run**, beaten only by the genuinely
correct `0.589`. The entire score comes from one word — `for` — which appears in exactly one of the
eight tickets and therefore carries the **maximum weight this archive can give**,
`log(8/1) ≈ 2.079` ([1.4](../01-text-as-numbers/1.4-the-word-that-tells-you-nothing.md)). A
preposition produced the most confident wrong answer of the day.

**The barcode question is the plausible one, which makes it worse.** `0.212` on *"Mobile app keeps
showing the old profile photo"*. Both are about a mobile app. A human skimming would take a second
look before rejecting it, and a model summarising three results would very reasonably say *"we have
seen mobile app issues before, see KB-402"*, which is true and useless.

**The currency question is the trap that will actually happen.** `0.320` and `0.287` on two invoice
tickets. The archive has no answer, the retriever found the two documents nearest in subject, and the
desk will now discuss invoices with somebody who asked about currency. This is the shape of almost
every real RAG failure: not an absurd result, a **topically adjacent** one.

Now the threshold:

```text
archive: 8 tickets, all of them about this product
cap: top 3, cutoff 0.2

a question the archive CAN answer: 'invoice pdf opens empty'
   0.589  4402  Cannot print an invoice from the portal; the PDF opens emp
    returned: 1

a question the archive CANNOT answer: 'we need SOC 2 evidence for a security questionnaire'
   0.369  4455  Refund requested for the annual plan after two months. Fix
    returned: 1

a question the archive CANNOT answer: 'the barcode scanner in the warehouse app crashes on Android 15'
   0.212  4571  Mobile app keeps showing the old profile photo. Fix KB-402
    returned: 1

a question the archive CANNOT answer: 'can we get an invoice in a different currency'
   0.320  4402  Cannot print an invoice from the portal; the PDF opens emp
   0.287  4533  Invoice total disagrees with the plan price; rounding in t
    returned: 2
```

**The threshold helped, and it did not solve it.**

What it did well: the noise is gone. Twelve results became five, the two `0.000` rows under the SOC 2
question disappeared, and the answerable question now returns exactly one result — the right one —
instead of one right one and two pieces of furniture. That is a real improvement and it costs one
comparison.

What it did not do: **not one of the three unanswerable questions returned zero results.** All three
still return something above the cutoff. Raise the cutoff to `0.40` and the SOC 2 answer disappears
— and so does every result for the currency question, and so does the second and third result of the
answerable one, and so, on a day when the right answer scores `0.30`, does the right answer.

There is no cutoff on this archive that separates *right* from *nearest*, because a wrong answer
scored `0.369` and a right answer scored `0.100`. That is not a bug in the threshold. It is what a
similarity score is: a measure of overlap, not a measure of correctness, and no single number
converts one into the other.

## When it breaks

The break has already happened four times above. What matters is how it reaches a person, and it is
worth writing out as a scene because it is the failure this day exists to prevent.

> 🎬 **The scene:** a support agent asks the desk *"has anything like this come up before?"* about a
> customer asking for invoices in a different currency.
>
> 😬 **The naive fix:** trust the top-k. It came back with results and the results have scores on
> them. The draft reply cites ticket 4402.
>
> 💥 **Why it fails:** the retriever is a **sorting** machine, not a relevance oracle. `0.320` does
> not mean *related*; it means *the closest thing on a shelf that contains nothing close*. Keyword
> search fails loudly, with zero rows. Vector search fails **politely**, with three plausible rows,
> and polite failures go straight into drafts.
>
> 💡 **The insight:** top-k always returns k, so *"nothing found"* is a decision the caller must make
> and nobody else can make for them. Ship the score with every result, tell the agent that a weak
> neighbour is absence and not evidence, and measure where the honest cutoff sits for your own
> corpus — which is [Day 50](../../../day-50-chunking-and-top-k/LESSON.md)'s work.

The other failure shape is the one that hides inside the fix. Set a threshold, watch it clean up your
test queries, and ship it. Six months later somebody reports that the desk says *"nothing on file"*
for a case they know is in there. The threshold has silently become a recall problem, and unlike the
original failure it produces **no output at all**, so it is invisible in the logs unless you count
empty results deliberately. Any threshold you add needs a metric on how often it fires, from the day
you add it.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead of the teaching version.** Three things, in increasing order of
cost, and the first two are cheap enough that there is no excuse.

**Say the number, in words.** Never hand a bare `0.32` to a model or a person, because neither has
any calibration for it ([4.3](../04-the-adk-socket/4.3-the-score-has-to-be-written-down.md)). Band
it — *strong match*, *weak match, verify before using*, *nothing close enough* — with the bands taken
from a judgement set rather than from taste.

**Make the absence explicit.** When the best score is below the floor, do not return an empty list and
hope. Return a result that says, in text the model will read, *"no past case in the archive is close
enough to this question"*. Day 46's rule 4 said a miss must be reported rather than interpreted; this
is that rule with a number behind it. An empty list is silence, and a model handed silence will fill
it.

**Use the gap, not the level.** The most reliable single signal available for free is `top_score -
second_score`. A confident retrieval has one result well clear of the rest; the currency question
above scored `0.320` and `0.287`, a gap of `0.033`, which is the shape of *the archive has several
mediocre candidates and no good one*. The answerable question scored `0.589` and `0.100`, a gap of
`0.489`. **The gap separated these cases where the absolute level did not.** It is not a complete
answer either, and it costs nothing.

The expensive and correct answer, at real scale, is a **second stage**: retrieve generously with the
cheap method, then re-score those candidates with something that actually judges relevance — a
cross-encoder model, or a model asked directly *"does this document answer this question?"*. That is
what turns *nearest* into *relevant*, and it is the reason production RAG is two stages and not one.

**What degrades at scale.** Precision, and it degrades in a specific direction. A bigger archive makes
the nearest neighbour *nearer* — with fifty thousand tickets there is almost certainly something that
shares real vocabulary with any question — so the wrong answers get **higher scores** and become
harder to reject. The threshold you tuned at eight rows is wrong at fifty thousand and it will be
wrong in the dangerous direction. Thresholds are corpus-specific and corpus-size-specific, and they
need re-measuring, which nobody ever schedules.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"We show the top three with scores and no floor. I
asked the desk about SOC 2 evidence, which we have never handled, and it returned a refund ticket at
0.369 — higher than the second-best result on a question it can actually answer. Add a floor, add an
explicit 'nothing close enough' response below it, log the top score and the gap on every search, and
put those three unanswerable questions in the test set with an assertion that they return no
citations."*

**The interview question:** *"What is the most dangerous failure mode of RAG?"* An honest answer:
*"That it always returns something. Top-k gives you k results whether or not the corpus contains an
answer, and they arrive with scores on them, which makes them look measured. I asked an eight-ticket
support archive about SOC 2 compliance evidence — a subject it has never contained — and got a refund
ticket back at 0.369, which was higher than the second-best result on a question the archive answers
perfectly. So a similarity threshold cannot separate right from nearest: a wrong answer scored 0.369
and a right answer scored 0.100 in the same run. What I actually rely on is three things: band the
score into words instead of showing a float, return an explicit 'nothing close enough' rather than an
empty list, and watch the gap between the first and second score, because a confident retrieval has
one result well clear and a bluffing one has three bunched together. At scale you add a second-stage
re-ranker, because that is the only thing that turns nearest into relevant."*

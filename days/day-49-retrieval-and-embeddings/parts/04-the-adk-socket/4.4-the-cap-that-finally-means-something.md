# 4.4 — The cap that finally means something

## One-line answer

The same `TOP_K = 3` that Day 46 called a cost control and not a precision control puts the right
answer first **6 times out of 7** over a ranked list and **2 times out of 7** over an unranked one —
the cap did not change, the thing underneath it did.

## The story

You have asked for three CVs.

The first time, somebody walks to the cabinet, opens the drawer marked *applications*, takes the top
three off the pile and brings them to you. You now have three CVs. You have saved yourself reading
sixty, and you have learned nothing about the other fifty-seven, and the three in your hand are the
three that were posted first.

The second time, somebody has read all sixty against the job description, put them in order, and
brought you the top three. You have the same three sheets of paper in your hand and the same amount
of reading to do.

The difference is not in what you are holding. It is in what "three" cost you. In the first case,
three was a limit on your effort. In the second, three was a **decision**, and everything you did not
receive was excluded by somebody who had looked at it.

## The idea in plain language

Day 46 built a cap and was honest about what it bought. Its
[4.2](../../../day-46-sessions-vs-memory/parts/04-the-choice/4.2-sutras-memory-policy.md) made
`TOP_K` a named constant, and its
[5.1](../../../day-46-sessions-vs-memory/parts/05-failure-lab/5.1-the-past-that-matched-on-nothing.md)
then said, in as many words:

> **The cap made the response smaller and no more correct**, because you cannot take the top three of
> a list that has no top. A cap is a cost control. It is not a precision control.

That is still true. What changed today is what the cap is applied to. `[:3]` over a list sorted by
similarity is a completely different operation from `[:3]` over a list in insertion order, and this
part measures the difference on seven questions instead of arguing about it.

Two numbers are worth naming before the run, because they are the standard vocabulary:

**hit@1** — how often the correct document is the *first* result. This is the one that matters most,
because a model reading a list top to bottom, and a formatter showing "the closest past case", both
take the first.

**hit@3** — how often the correct document is *anywhere* in the top three. This is the number that
tells you whether a larger cap would help.

The gap between them is diagnostic. A high hit@3 and a low hit@1 means your retrieval finds the right
document and cannot rank it, which is a ranking problem. A low hit@3 means the document is not being
found at all, which is a matching problem — and matching problems are
[section 2](../02-the-meaning-test/2.1-the-score-that-came-back-zero.md)'s subject, not this one.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because this is the part where Day 46's open finding closes, and closing it is what ADK-30 is for.

The desk carries `load_memory` and a cap. Before today the cap threw away four arbitrary results and
kept three arbitrary ones. After today it throws away the five worst and keeps the three best. The
constant is the same, the tool is the same, and the desk got measurably better — that is the whole
claim, and a claim like that needs a number rather than a paragraph.

The number also sets up [Day 50](../../../day-50-chunking-and-top-k/LESSON.md), which asks what
`TOP_K` should actually be. That question is only answerable once the list is ordered, so today is the
day that makes tomorrow's question meaningful.

## The mechanism

Seven questions, each with exactly one right answer, asked of both services.

```python
# days/day-49-retrieval-and-embeddings/lab/capped.py
QUESTIONS: list[tuple[str, str]] = [
    ("the invoice PDF opens empty", "4402"),
    ("reset mail is not reaching one company", "4239"),
    ("customer asking for a refund on the annual plan", "4455"),
    ("dashboard is blank after the upgrade", "4467"),
    ("nightly export produced an empty file", "4508"),
    ("invoice total disagrees with the plan price", "4533"),
    ("auth redirect leaves the user on a sign-in screen", "4188"),
]
```

**Line by line:**

- Seven `(question, correct ticket)` pairs. This is a **relevance judgement set**, and it is the
  smallest honest version of the thing every retrieval team maintains: queries with known right
  answers, so a change to the system can be measured rather than felt.
- Six of the seven are written in **paraphrase** — *"reset mail is not reaching one company"* against
  a ticket that says *"Password reset mail never reaches the customer"*. Not the ticket's own words,
  and not deliberately obscure either. That is what a support agent types.
- The seventh, `4188`, is [2.1](../02-the-meaning-test/2.1-the-score-that-came-back-zero.md)'s
  question. It is included knowing that no word-based method in this day can answer it, because a
  judgement set that only contains questions you can answer measures nothing.
- One right answer per question keeps the arithmetic honest. Real judgement sets have graded
  relevance and use measures like nDCG; one correct document and hit@k is the version you can hold in
  your head, and it is enough to see a factor-of-three difference.

```python
# days/day-49-retrieval-and-embeddings/lab/capped.py
        found = await service.search_memory(app_name=APP, user_id=USER, query=question)
        returned = refs(found.memories)
        kept = returned[:TOP_K]
        at_one += kept[:1] == [answer]
        at_three += answer in kept
```

**Line by line:**

- `search_memory` is called on both services through the **same interface**, which is the whole point
  of [4.1](4.1-two-methods-and-a-list.md). The measuring code has no idea which one it is holding.
- `returned[:TOP_K]` applies the cap **outside** the service, identically to both, so the comparison
  is of the lists and not of two different capping policies. The ranked service also caps internally
  at three, so its `returned` is already three long; slicing again changes nothing and keeps the two
  paths symmetrical.
- `kept[:1] == [answer]` rather than `kept[0] == answer`, because `kept` can be empty — ADK's keyword
  service returns nothing at all for some queries, and indexing an empty list would raise
  `IndexError` in the middle of a measurement.
- `at_one += ...` on a boolean works because `True` is `1` in Python. It is idiomatic and it is worth
  reading twice if you have not seen it.
- The character count is accumulated alongside, because a retrieval change that improves accuracy and
  triples the prompt is not obviously an improvement
  ([4.3](4.3-the-score-has-to-be-written-down.md)).

Run both arms:

```bash
cd days/day-49-retrieval-and-embeddings/lab
uv run python capped.py
uv run python capped.py --keyword
```

**Line by line:**

- Same seven questions, same cap of three, same measurement code.
- `--keyword` swaps in ADK's `InMemoryMemoryService` with the identical eight tickets filed into it.
- **Zero model calls in either arm.**

Measured on 2026-09-05 — the ranked list first:

```text
service: InMemoryMemoryService, cap: first 3 results

  hit  want 4402  got ['4188', '4239', '4402']  (of 8 returned)  the invoice PDF opens empty
  hit  want 4239  got ['4239', '4467', '4508']  (of 3 returned)  reset mail is not reaching one company
  MISS want 4455  got ['4188', '4239', '4402']  (of 8 returned)  customer asking for a refund on the annual plan
  MISS want 4467  got ['4188', '4239', '4402']  (of 8 returned)  dashboard is blank after the upgrade
  hit  want 4508  got ['4402', '4508']  (of 2 returned)  nightly export produced an empty file
  MISS want 4533  got ['4188', '4239', '4402']  (of 8 returned)  invoice total disagrees with the plan price
  hit  want 4188  got ['4188', '4239', '4402']  (of 8 returned)  auth redirect leaves the user on a sign-in screen

right answer first        : 2/7
right answer in the top 3 : 4/7
characters sent to the model: 2058
```

**hit@1: 6/7 against 2/7. hit@3: 7/7 against 4/7.** Same eight tickets, same seven questions, same
cap of three. The only difference is whether the list was sorted before it was cut.

Now read the individual rows, because they say more than the totals.

**Look at the `(of N returned)` column in the keyword arm.** Four questions returned **eight of
eight** — the whole archive — and in every one of those four the top three are `4188, 4239, 4402`.
Not because those three are relevant; because they are the first three filed. Three different
questions about refunds, dashboards and invoice totals all received the same three tickets, and three
of the four were wrong. That is what capping an unranked list does: it does not sample the archive, it
returns the front of it, over and over.

**One row returned two results.** *"nightly export produced an empty file"* matched only two tickets,
so the keyword arm returned two — and got it right. When the keyword matcher is *selective* it is
fine. It is only useless when it matches everything, and it matches everything on any question phrased
as a sentence.

**The ranked arm's one miss at rank 1 is the honest one.** *"auth redirect leaves the user on a
sign-in screen"* put `4533` — the invoice-rounding ticket — first, and `4188` second. It is counted as
a hit@3, and you should not trust it: [1.4](../01-text-as-numbers/1.4-the-word-that-tells-you-nothing.md)
showed that the only weighted word 4188 shares with that query is the preposition `on`, which happens
to appear in exactly one archive ticket and therefore carries the maximum weight this archive can
give. **The right answer was found by an accident about a preposition.** Ranking improved the ordering
of what the matcher found; it did not give the matcher a way to find things it could not see.

**2,392 characters against 2,058.** That is what three results cost the prompt in each arm — the
ranked service costs about 16% more, because it writes the ticket reference and the similarity into
every entry's text ([4.3](4.3-the-score-has-to-be-written-down.md)). Three hundred and thirty-four
characters bought hit@1 going from 2/7 to 6/7, which is the cheapest quality improvement in this
whole day. And Day 46's
[4.1](../../../day-46-sessions-vs-memory/parts/04-the-choice/4.1-pricing-both-in-tokens.md)
established that a tool result is an event, so it is re-sent on every later turn. Roughly eight
hundred characters per result is the number to hold when
[Day 50](../../../day-50-chunking-and-top-k/LESSON.md) asks whether `TOP_K` should be five.

## When it breaks

**The cap hides a matching failure.** Look at the ranked arm's seventh row again: `hit`, in the top
three, counted as a success. The score behind it is the one
[2.1](../02-the-meaning-test/2.1-the-score-that-came-back-zero.md) measured at `0.000` against the
plain incoming ticket. A judgement set that only reports hit@k will tell you this system works. **A
judgement set that also reports the score will not.** Report both, or you will ship a retriever whose
success is made of prepositions.

**A cap over a ranked list still returns k things when the answer is not there.** Every row above has
three results and none of them says "none of these is good enough". That is
[5.1](../05-when-retrieval-lies/5.1-nearest-is-not-near.md), and it is the failure that survives
everything this section built.

**The cap is applied in two places.** In the run above the service caps at `TOP_K` and the measuring
code slices again. That is harmless here and it is exactly how a bug gets in: a service capped at
three inside a caller that wanted five silently gets three, and nothing anywhere says so. The cap
belongs in **one** place, named, and the hub's build brief makes `TOP_K` a module constant for that
reason.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead of the teaching version.** They keep the judgement set in the
repository as a test, not in a script. Twenty to fifty `(query, expected document)` pairs, taken from
real logged searches, with an assertion that hit@3 does not drop below a recorded baseline. That test
is the only thing standing between you and a tokenizer change, a model swap or a chunking change
quietly making retrieval worse, and every one of those changes is silent by nature.

They also **separate the cap from the budget**. `TOP_K` in a config, the token cost of `TOP_K`
results in a dashboard, and a rule that says which one wins when they disagree. Day 46's finding was
that the cap mattered more than the policy — a factor of six against a factor of two — and that
remains true; what today adds is that the cap now also has a quality dimension, so it can no longer be
tuned on cost alone.

And at real scale they stop using a single k. **Two-stage retrieval** is the standard shape: retrieve
a generous k — fifty, a hundred — with the cheap method, then re-score just those with something
expensive and accurate (a cross-encoder, or a model asked to judge), and keep three. The cheap stage
optimises hit@50, which is a recall problem; the expensive stage optimises hit@1, which is a ranking
problem. Splitting them is what lets each be measured and tuned on its own.

**What degrades at scale or under concurrency.** hit@1 falls as the archive grows, because more
documents means more near-misses competing for first place, and it falls fastest for the queries that
were already marginal. The measurement that survives is the **gap between the top score and the second
score**: a wide gap means the retriever is confident, and a narrowing average gap across a quarter is
the earliest warning that quality is decaying, well before anybody complains.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"hit@3 is 7/7 and I do not believe it. Print the
score alongside every hit — the seventh row scores near zero and passes on a tie. Add the raw score to
the report, add ten more queries from last month's logs, and record the baseline in the test so a
regression fails CI instead of being noticed by a customer."*

**The interview question:** *"You added ranking to a retriever that already had a top-k. What did
that actually buy you?"* An honest answer: *"I measured it on seven queries with known answers. With
the unranked keyword service, capping at three gave hit@1 of 2 out of 7 and hit@3 of 4 out of 7 —
and the interesting detail is that four of the seven queries matched all eight documents, so the top
three were literally the first three ever filed, the same three for every question. With ranking, the
same cap gave 6 out of 7 and 7 out of 7. The cap did not change; what it was applied to did. The
honest caveat is that one of those hits was luck: the right document ranked second because it shared
the preposition 'on' with the query, and 'on' happened to be rare in that archive. So I report hit@k
and the score together, because hit@k alone would have told me the system worked."*

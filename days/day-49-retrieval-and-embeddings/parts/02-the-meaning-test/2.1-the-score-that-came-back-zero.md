# 2.1 — 💥 The score that came back zero

## One-line answer

Ticket 4521 and ticket 4188 are the same fault, and the index you just built scores them at
**0.000** — not low, not weak, exactly zero — while it puts a ticket about a stale browser cache at
the top with `0.270`.

## The story

You go to the doctor because the room tips sideways when you stand up.

You say it the way it feels. *My head spins when I get up. It goes away if I sit down again.* She
listens, asks two questions, writes something on the card, and tells you it is common and usually
settles.

Three years later, a different doctor, a different clinic, and it comes back. You describe it the
same way, because it is the same thing and you are the same person. She looks for your old record
and asks: *have you had this before?* You say you think so, years ago. She searches your file for
the words you have just used — *head*, *spins*, *standing* — and finds nothing.

Your old card is in that file. It says **vertigo, positional, settled without treatment**. Not one of
the words you said is on it. The record of the exact same complaint is sitting in front of her and
neither of you can see it, because the words she was searching with belong to you and the words on
the card belong to the doctor who wrote it.

Nobody made a mistake. Nobody was careless. The information is present, it is correct, and it is
unreachable, because the only bridge between the question and the record was a set of matching
letters, and there were none.

## The idea in plain language

That is the failure this part measures, and it is the reason embeddings exist.

Every method in [section 1](../01-text-as-numbers/1.1-the-counter-that-takes-a-description.md) — word
counting, cosine, tf-idf weighting — scores two documents by the words they **share**. Sharing is the
only channel. If two texts describe one thing using different words, there is no channel at all, and
the score is not "small". It is arithmetically zero, because the dot product runs over the
intersection of the two word sets and the intersection is empty.

Read the two tickets. This is Sutra's archive and today's incoming ticket, unchanged:

> **4188** — *Login loop on the staff portal. Session cookie written without SameSite, so the
> browser dropped it. Fix KB-104.*
>
> **4521** — *Auth redirect bug: after the identity provider hands control back, our user lands at a
> blank sign-in screen.*

They are the same bug. The 2025 engineer called it a **login loop** and named the cause. The 2026
engineer called it an **auth redirect bug** and described the symptom. One says *portal*, the other
says *identity provider*. One says *page reloads*, the other says *lands at a blank sign-in screen*.

The only word they have in common is **the**.

And [1.4](../01-text-as-numbers/1.4-the-word-that-tells-you-nothing.md) already established what
`the` is worth in this archive: it appears in all eight tickets, so its idf is `log(8/8)` = exactly
`0.0`. The one bridge between the question and the answer is a word worth nothing. Multiply it in and
the whole score is zero.

That is the sentence to keep: **the better your weighting, the more honestly it reports that word
matching cannot do this job.** tf-idf did not make retrieval worse here. It removed the fake `0.100`
that raw counting produced out of the word `the` and showed you the truth underneath.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because this is the failure the addendum that created this day names, in these words:

> *"Has anything like ticket #4521 happened before?"* — keyword search misses it because the old
> ticket says "login loop" not "auth redirect bug".

And because it closes the loop on Day 46. Day 46's
[2.2](../../../day-46-sessions-vs-memory/parts/02-the-interface/2.2-the-silence-that-lets-you-swap.md)
measured a query for *"sign-in trouble"* returning zero memories against a store that held the
answer, and left it there as a property of the interface. Day 46's
[5.1](../../../day-46-sessions-vs-memory/parts/05-failure-lab/5.1-the-past-that-matched-on-nothing.md)
then measured the opposite failure — everything matching — and concluded that a cap over an unranked
list buys nothing.

This part is where those two findings meet. Ranking fixed the second problem, and it did nothing at
all for the first, because both failures have the same cause: **words are the wrong unit for
"about"**. The desk needs a vectoriser that does not go through words, and that is
[section 3](../03-meaning-as-geometry/3.1-the-dish-you-know-by-taste.md).

## The mechanism

One script, two arms, the whole archive scored against the incoming ticket.

```python
# days/day-49-retrieval-and-embeddings/lab/meaning.py
def main() -> None:
    weighted = "--counts" not in sys.argv
    texts = [text for _, text in ARCHIVE]
    weights = idf(texts)

    def vector(text: str) -> dict[str, float]:
        return tfidf(text, weights) if weighted else counts(text)

    index = [(ref, vector(text)) for ref, text in ARCHIVE]
    results = rank(vector(INCOMING), index)
```

**Line by line:**

- `weights = idf(texts)` is computed **once**, over the archive, and used for both the documents and
  the query. That is the rule from [1.4](../01-text-as-numbers/1.4-the-word-that-tells-you-nothing.md);
  scoring a query with different weights would make every number below meaningless.
- The local `vector` closure is what makes the ablation honest. Both arms use the *same* index-build
  loop, the *same* `rank`, the *same* archive; only the function that turns text into numbers
  changes.
- `rank(vector(INCOMING), index)` scores the whole archive rather than taking a top three. This part
  is about a score, not about a shortlist, so nothing is cut.
- There is no threshold and no filtering anywhere. Every row that comes out is shown, including the
  zeros, because the zeros are the finding.

Run both arms:

```bash
cd days/day-49-retrieval-and-embeddings/lab
uv run python meaning.py
uv run python meaning.py --counts
```

**Line by line:**

- The default arm uses tf-idf weighting — the index from
  [1.5](../01-text-as-numbers/1.5-the-work-you-do-once.md), which worked perfectly on a query written
  in the archive's own words.
- `--counts` turns weighting off, so you can see where the residual score in the weighted arm went.
- **Zero model calls in either arm.**

Read that table one row at a time, because every row is doing something.

**The right answer scored `0.000`.** Not `0.02`. Not "low". The same number the retriever would give
for a ticket written in a language it has never seen. There is no threshold anywhere that recovers
this, no `top_k` large enough to help, and no ranking to improve — 4188 is tied at the bottom with
three tickets that genuinely have nothing to do with 4521.

**The top hit is 4467, a caching bug, at `0.270`.** An agent reading the first result learns that the
last time something like this happened, the fix was to clear a stale bundle from the browser cache.
That is a plausible, professional, completely wrong answer to an authentication bug, and it will be
delivered with a similarity score attached, which makes it look measured.

Why 4467? Because it shares `blank` and `after` with the incoming ticket. `blank` appears in exactly
one archive ticket, so [1.4](../01-text-as-numbers/1.4-the-word-that-tells-you-nothing.md)'s weighting
gives it `log(8/1) ≈ 2.079`, the highest weight available. The single most confident number in this
run comes from a coincidence of adjectives.

**The word `the` is the whole of the raw-count score.** Here is the ablation:

Under raw counts, 4188 scores `0.100` and ranks **sixth**. That `0.100` looks like a signal and is
made entirely of the word `the`, and the ranking is worse — the right answer has fallen from fifth to
sixth, below an invoice-printing ticket. Weighting improved the *reporting* and could not improve the
*retrieval*, because there was nothing to retrieve on.

## When it breaks

It has broken; this part is the break. What matters is what the break **looks like from outside**,
because that is what a support agent will see.

There is no traceback. There is no empty result. The desk returns three past cases with similarity
scores on them, in confident order, and the first one is about a browser cache. Compare that with
what Day 46's keyword service does with the same question:

```text
service: InMemoryMemoryService
memories returned: 8 of 8 filed
    [ticket 4188] Login loop on the staff portal. Session cookie written without SameSite, so th
    [ticket 4239] Password reset mail never reaches the customer. Their gateway drops our sender
    ...
```

Eight of eight, unranked — and by pure accident of insertion order, 4188 is *first*. Reorder the
archive and it is not. So the keyword service and the ranked service both fail this question, in
opposite directions: one returns everything with no way to choose, and one chooses confidently and
chooses wrong. Neither errors.

The dangerous version of this failure is not the wrong article. It is what the wrong article does to
a human. An agent who asks *"have we seen this before?"* and is told *"yes, ticket 4467, clear the
cache"* now stops looking. The search was answered. The archive contains the fix — cookie flags, and
KB-104, which Day 46 filed by hand — and nobody will read it, because the system already said
something.

That is worse than the empty result the doctor got. An empty result sends you looking somewhere else.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead of the teaching version.** They do not choose between word
matching and meaning matching; they run **both** and merge the results. That is called **hybrid
search**, and it is what every serious retrieval system in production does, because the two methods
fail in opposite directions. BM25 nails exact identifiers — a customer says `KB-104` or an order
number and you want the exact-match hit, which an embedding will happily blur away — and dense
retrieval catches *login loop* against *auth redirect*. The merge is usually **reciprocal rank
fusion**: take each method's ranked list, score each document by one over its rank in each list, and
add. It needs no calibration between two score scales that are not comparable, which is why it is the
default choice.

They also **keep a set of query-answer pairs as a test**. This part is exactly that: one query, one
document that must be in the top three, an assertion that can go red. Ten of those, run on every
change to the tokenizer, the weighting or the model, is the difference between knowing your retrieval
works and hoping.

**What degrades at scale.** Vocabulary mismatch gets **worse** as the archive grows and ages, and
this is unintuitive. More documents means more chances that one of them is phrased your way — that is
the improvement people expect. But the archive also spans more years, more teams and more product
names, and the language drifts: *portal* becomes *console*, *sign-in* becomes *auth*, an internal
codename replaces a feature name. The documents most likely to answer a hard question are the old
ones, and they are the ones written in vocabulary nobody uses any more.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Our regression set has one query in it and it is
written in the same words as the document. Add the five queries support actually typed last month,
including the two that use different vocabulary from the tickets, and assert the right ticket is in
the top three. Right now we have a retrieval system with no evidence that it retrieves anything."*

**The interview question:** *"What is the vocabulary mismatch problem?"* An honest answer: *"Two
people describing the same thing use different words, and any method that scores by shared words
gives them a similarity of zero. I hit it deliberately: a 2025 ticket said 'login loop, session
cookie without SameSite' and the 2026 ticket for the identical fault said 'auth redirect bug, blank
sign-in screen'. The only word they share is 'the', which has an idf of exactly zero in that archive,
so tf-idf scored them at 0.000 and ranked a caching ticket first at 0.270 because it happened to
share the word 'blank'. What made it dangerous rather than merely wrong is that it returned a result
with a score on it, so it looked measured. The fix is dense retrieval — embeddings — and in production
I would run it alongside BM25 and fuse the ranks, because embeddings are worse than exact matching
when the query is an identifier."*

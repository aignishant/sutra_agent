# 2.2 — The repairs that fix one pair

## One-line answer

Stopword lists, stemming and a hand-written synonym map are the three repairs everybody tries after
[2.1](2.1-the-score-that-came-back-zero.md); the first two move the score by exactly nothing, the
third takes it to `0.523` and rank 1, and the very next sentence a support agent types is back to
`0.000`.

## The story

There is a slow drip under the kitchen sink.

You wrap the joint in tape. It stops. You are pleased with yourself for about four days, and then
there is a patch of damp on the wall behind the cupboard, which was never wet before. So you wrap
that joint too.

By the end of the month there are five wrapped joints under there, and you know all five by heart —
which one goes first when the pressure is high, which one you must not touch when tightening
another. The tape is not the problem. Each piece of it genuinely stopped a leak, and every piece is
still doing its job.

The problem is that the pipe is old, and you are now maintaining a list of its weak points instead of
replacing it. Nobody decided to do that. It happened one reasonable repair at a time.

And the tell is this: when a new leak appears, you cannot predict where. Your five repairs taught you
nothing about the sixth.

## The idea in plain language

After [2.1](2.1-the-score-that-came-back-zero.md), three repairs suggest themselves, and every team
that meets this problem tries them in this order.

**Stopwords.** Remove the words that carry no information — `the`, `a`, `and`, `after`. Since `the`
was the only shared word, removing it should at least stop the noise.

**Stemming.** Cut words down to a common root so that `sign`, `signs`, `signing` and `signed` all
become the same token. If two documents differ only by grammar, stemming makes them match. (The
proper name for doing this with a dictionary rather than by chopping suffixes is **lemmatisation**;
the difference does not matter for what follows.)

**Synonyms.** Write down that `auth` means the same as `login`, that `redirect` relates to `loop`,
and expand every query and document with the alternatives. This is the repair that feels like it
addresses the real problem, because it does — for the pair you wrote down.

Each of these is a piece of tape. Each one works. And the thing to measure is not whether it works,
but what it costs to keep working, which is: somebody must notice each new leak and write another
rule.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because this is the argument Sutra must be able to make. The next section installs a local embedding
model, and that is a dependency — a daemon to run, a model to pull, an outage to handle. A decision
like that needs a reason stronger than "embeddings are what people use".

The reason is in the numbers below. The synonym map does fix ticket 4521, completely, better than the
embedding lane is likely to. It fixes nothing else, it must be maintained by a person, and the person
maintaining it must know in advance which words the next engineer will type. That is the argument, and
it is only convincing because you ran the arm that works.

There is a second, quieter reason this part exists. Day 46's
[5.1](../../../day-46-sessions-vs-memory/parts/05-failure-lab/5.1-the-past-that-matched-on-nothing.md)
already tried the stopword repair on the *opposite* failure — too many matches rather than none — and
concluded that *"stopword removal is a filter tuned to one archive by hand. It is not a retriever."*
The same sentence is true here from the other direction, and seeing it fail twice, in two opposite
ways, is what makes it stick.

## The mechanism

One script applies the three repairs cumulatively to the same query and the same archive.

```python
# days/day-49-retrieval-and-embeddings/lab/patches.py
STOPWORDS = {
    "a", "after", "an", "and", "at", "back", "because", "but", "for", "from",
    "in", "is", "it", "our", "so", "the", "their", "this", "to", "with", "without",
}  # fmt: skip

SYNONYMS = {
    "auth": ["login", "signin"],
    "redirect": ["loop"],
    "sign": ["login", "signin"],
    "screen": ["page", "form"],
    "user": ["staff"],
    "identity": ["session"],
}


def stem(word: str) -> str:
    """Crude suffix stripping. Nothing clever, and that is the point."""
    for suffix in ("ing", "ed", "es", "s"):
        if len(word) > 4 and word.endswith(suffix):
            return word[: -len(suffix)]
    return word
```

**Line by line:**

- `STOPWORDS` is twenty-one ordinary English function words. It is deliberately a *general* list —
  the kind you would copy from anywhere — and it does **not** contain `fix` or `kb`, the two words
  [1.4](../01-text-as-numbers/1.4-the-word-that-tells-you-nothing.md) found were worthless in this
  particular archive. That gap is the first argument against hand-written lists.
- `SYNONYMS` has six entries and every one of them was written **after reading tickets 4188 and
  4521 side by side**. That is not cheating; it is exactly how these maps get written in real
  systems, and it is why they are so convincing in the demo and so disappointing in production.
- `"redirect": ["loop"]` is the entry that does the heavy lifting, and read it honestly: `redirect`
  and `loop` are not synonyms in English. They are synonyms *in this pair of tickets*. Somebody who
  had not read both tickets would never write that line.
- `stem` strips four suffixes and refuses to touch words of four characters or fewer, so that `is`
  does not become `i`. It is not a real stemmer — a real one is a few hundred rules — and it is
  included at this quality on purpose, because the finding below is that even a *perfect* stemmer
  could not help here.

```python
# days/day-49-retrieval-and-embeddings/lab/patches.py
def prepare(text: str, *, drop_stopwords: bool, stemming: bool, expand: bool) -> str:
    words = tokenize(text)
    if drop_stopwords:
        words = [word for word in words if word not in STOPWORDS]
    if stemming:
        words = [stem(word) for word in words]
    if expand:
        extra: list[str] = []
        for word in words:
            extra.extend(SYNONYMS.get(word, []))
        words = words + extra
    return " ".join(words)
```

**Line by line:**

- `prepare` is applied to **both** the archive rows and the query, with the same flags. Applying a
  repair to only one side is a real and common bug: expand the query but not the documents and half
  your synonyms do nothing.
- The order is fixed and it matters. Stopwords are dropped **before** stemming, because stemming
  `their` to `thei` would stop it matching the stopword list. Synonyms are expanded **last**, on
  already-stemmed words, so the map's keys must be written in stemmed form or they will never fire.
  Three flags, and their order is already a source of bugs.
- `words = words + extra` **adds** the synonyms rather than replacing the originals, so a document
  keeps its own vocabulary as well as the alternatives. This inflates every document's length, which
  cosine then divides out, which quietly weakens every score — a cost nobody notices.
- The whole function returns a **string** rather than a vector, so that `idf` can be recomputed over
  the repaired archive. Repairing the text and then using the *old* weights would be the
  index-mismatch bug from [1.5](../01-text-as-numbers/1.5-the-work-you-do-once.md).

Run it:

```bash
cd days/day-49-retrieval-and-embeddings/lab
uv run python patches.py
```

**Line by line:**

- One script, four arms inside it, so the four numbers can be read as a column. Each arm rebuilds the
  archive's weights from the repaired text, which is what a real deployment of the repair would do.
- **Zero model calls.**

Measured on 2026-09-05:

```text
incoming 4521: Auth redirect bug: after the identity provider hands control back, our user lands at a blank sign-in screen.

4188 is the same fault. Where does each repair put it?

plain tf-idf                 4188:  0.000 rank 5/8 (tied with 3)   top hit: 4467 at 0.270
+ stopword list              4188:  0.000 rank 2/8 (tied with 6)   top hit: 4467 at 0.394
+ stemming                   4188:  0.000 rank 2/8 (tied with 6)   top hit: 4467 at 0.394
+ synonym map                4188:  0.523 rank 1/8 (tied with 0)   top hit: 4188 at 0.523
```

Four rows, and three separate lessons in them.

**Stopwords changed the score by nothing.** `0.000` before, `0.000` after. This is worth sitting with,
because the stopword repair is the one everybody reaches for first and it was already done: tf-idf
gave `the` a weight of exactly zero in [1.4](../01-text-as-numbers/1.4-the-word-that-tells-you-nothing.md),
so removing it removed a word that was already contributing nothing. **Proper weighting makes a
stopword list redundant**, and any measurable improvement you see from adding one to a tf-idf system
is telling you something is wrong with the weighting.

Look at what *did* change: `rank 2/8 (tied with 6)`. The right answer appears to have climbed from
fifth to second — and the tie count says seven of the eight tickets now score identically at `0.000`,
so "rank 2" is an artefact of sorting by ticket id. A rank with no score behind it is not a rank. This
is Day 46's finding wearing a different hat: you cannot take the top of a list that has no top.

**Stemming changed nothing at all** — the same three numbers. There is no shared root here to find.
`auth` and `login` are not two forms of one word, and no amount of suffix stripping will make them
one. Stemming solves a real problem, and it is not this problem.

**The synonym map worked completely.** `0.523`, rank 1, no ties, and the top hit is the right ticket.
Six hand-written lines took the score from zero to the best result anywhere in this day. That is the
honest result and it must not be softened: **for this pair, the tape held perfectly.**

Now the next leak:

```text
now the next agent types a different sentence about the same fault:
    'customers are kicked out to the log-in prompt every time'
        4188: 0.000, rank 2/8; top hit 4239 at 0.302
    'the token handshake fails and nobody can get past it'
        4188: 0.000, rank 1/8; top hit 4188 at 0.000
```

Both sentences describe the same bug. Both go back to `0.000`.

The first one is worse than a miss: the top hit is `4239` at `0.302` — the ticket about password
reset mail being dropped by a customer's gateway. A question about people being kicked out to the
login prompt is answered with an email-deliverability ticket, confidently, at a score three times
higher than anything the right answer got.

The second one is the tell that gives this part its name. `rank 1/8` at a score of `0.000` — the
whole archive tied at zero, and 4188 is "first" only because `4188` sorts before `4239`. The synonym
map covered `auth`, `redirect`, `sign`, `screen`, `user` and `identity`. It does not cover `kicked`,
`prompt`, `token` or `handshake`, and nobody could have known to write those lines, because those are
the words somebody has not typed yet.

## When it breaks

The repairs have a failure mode of their own, and it is the one Day 46 warned about from the other
side: **every added word is another chance to match something unrelated.**

Watch the top-hit score climb across the four arms above: `0.270`, then `0.394`, then `0.394`, then —
in the arm that worked — `0.523` on the right answer. The rise from `0.270` to `0.394` is not an
improvement. It is the same wrong ticket, 4467, scoring **higher** because removing stopwords made
every document shorter, which made the shared rare words a larger fraction of each vector. The wrong
answer got more confident.

Expansion does this harder. Adding `login`, `signin`, `page`, `form`, `staff` and `session` to every
document that contains one of the six keys means those documents now match queries about pages, about
forms, about staff and about sessions — none of which they are about. On this eight-ticket archive
that is invisible. On a large one it is the standard way a synonym-expanded index turns into a system
that returns something for everything.

And there is the maintenance failure, which does not show up in any run. Six synonym entries are
readable. Two hundred are not. Nobody can tell you what a two-hundred-entry map does to a query
without running it, the entries interact, and removing one is frightening because some search
somewhere depends on it. That is the wall of tape under the sink.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead of the teaching version.** They keep the synonym map — and they
keep it **small, versioned and tested**. Elasticsearch and OpenSearch both have first-class synonym
support, and the good use of it is narrow and high-value: product names, brand aliases, internal
codenames, deliberate abbreviations. `SSO` = `single sign-on` is worth a line, forever. `redirect` =
`loop` is not; that is a guess about how two people happened to write.

The rule a senior engineer applies is: **a synonym entry must be a fact about your domain, not an
observation about two documents.** Facts are stable and few. Observations are infinite.

Everything else goes to the embedding lane, and the two run together as the hybrid search described
in [2.1](2.1-the-score-that-came-back-zero.md)'s production section.

**What degrades at scale.** The map itself. Expansion multiplies index size and query cost — every
expanded word is another posting to store and another term to score — and the quality of the map
decays because language moves and nobody removes entries. The measurable symptom is that precision
falls slowly over quarters while everybody is looking at recall, and the cause is buried in a config
file that no test covers.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"I am fine with the six synonyms, but three of them
are not synonyms — they are notes about ticket 4188. Keep `sso` and `single sign-on`, delete the rest,
and put the rest of the job on the embedding index. Also, every entry in this file needs a test that
fails if it is removed, or we will never be able to delete one."*

**The interview question:** *"Why not just add synonyms instead of using embeddings?"* An honest
answer: *"Because I did, and it worked, and that is what makes the argument. Six hand-written entries
took my failing query from a cosine of 0.000 to 0.523 and rank one — better than a general-purpose
model would have done on that pair. Then I wrote two more sentences describing the same bug and both
went straight back to 0.000, and one of them returned a completely unrelated ticket at 0.302. The map
covers the words somebody already thought of, so it can only ever fix the past. It also grows without
limit and nobody can safely delete an entry. I would keep a short synonym list for domain facts —
product names, official abbreviations — and let a dense retriever handle paraphrase, because
paraphrase is unbounded and a list is not."*

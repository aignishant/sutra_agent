# 5.2 — 💥 The read that filled the window

## One-line answer

A resource is context you chose to spend, so `archive://all` is not a convenience — it is a design that
works on two tickets, fails outright on twenty thousand, and does its worst damage in between, where it
fits and buries the answer in the middle.

## The story

🎬 **The scene.** You ask a friend for the one photograph from the wedding — the one with your
grandmother in it, taken outside, before the food.

😬 **The naive fix.** They share the whole album. It is easier for them, it is one action instead of
scrolling, and it definitely contains the photo you asked for. *Everything is in there*, they say, and
they are right.

💥 **Why it fails.** There are three thousand photographs. You scroll. The first forty are the ones
you already have. The last forty are the dancing at the end, which you remember. Somewhere in the
middle is your grandmother, and after a long scroll through thumbnails that all look alike you give up and
send back one of the last few, which is a nice photo and is not the one. Nobody withheld anything. The
photo was there. It was surrounded by two thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine others, and being
surrounded is what made it unfindable.

💡 **The insight.** Handing over everything is not the same as handing over the answer, and the
difference is not politeness — it is that attention is finite and the middle of a large pile is where
things go to be missed.

## The idea in plain language

Day 19 established the shape of this problem for prompts: context is a budget, and what you put in it
displaces something else
([19 hub](../../../day-19-context-engineering-selection/LESSON.md)). Resources are where that budget
becomes somebody else's to spend on your behalf, which changes the failure in two ways.

**A resource is spent by the host, not by the model.** A tool call costs a model decision
([1.2](../01-who-initiates/1.2-what-a-door-costs.md)) and the model can decline to make it. A resource
the host attaches is already in the window before the model has said anything. There is no *decline*.

**The server designs the size.** When you publish `archive://all`, you have decided that one read
returns the entire archive. The host asked for the URI you advertised. Whatever comes back is what you
chose to make that address mean.

Two terms, because both get used loosely.

**A context window** is the maximum number of tokens a model can be given in one call. Everything —
instruction, attached resources, conversation, the tool declarations — shares it. Day 2 defined a token
([2.2.1](../../../day-02-llm-mechanics/parts/02-tokens-the-meter/2.1-what-a-token-is.md)) and Day 24 built the
accounting ([24 hub](../../../day-24-token-accounting-and-budgets/LESSON.md)).

**Burying** is the specific failure this part is named for: content that *fits* in the window and is
positioned where the model attends to it least. It is worse than not fitting, because not fitting is an
error and burying is a wrong answer.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because the archive summary resource in [2.1](../02-the-shelf/2.1-an-address-not-a-call.md) names every
ticket id it holds, and there are two of them.

At two ids that is a helpful line. At two hundred it is a paragraph nobody reads. At twenty thousand it
is a resource that cannot be listed, cannot be read, and should never have been designed that way — and
the code that produces it is identical at all three sizes, so nothing on the way from two to twenty
thousand will tell you when it stopped being a good idea.

This is also the argument that decides [3.4](../03-the-card/3.4-the-card-that-points-at-the-shelf.md)'s
embed-or-link question at scale, and it is the reason Phase 8's triage graph will attach
`ticket://{id}` rather than anything that starts with `archive://`.

## The paper behind it

> **Lost in the Middle: How Language Models Use Long Contexts** · `arXiv:2307.03172` · 2023 ·
> `https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172`

It showed that a model's ability to use a piece of information depends on **where** in a long input it
sits: performance is highest when the relevant content is at the beginning or the end, and measurably
lower when it is in the middle — so a longer context is not simply a larger container.

The paper is taught in Day 19's
[`../../../day-19-context-engineering-selection/papers/01-lost-in-the-middle.md`](../../../day-19-context-engineering-selection/papers/01-lost-in-the-middle.md).

## The mechanism

Three shelf designs answering one question — *what is wrong with ticket 4521?* — priced against a real
archive:

```python
# days/day-35-resources-and-prompts/lab/context_cost.py  (extract)
TICKET_CHARS = 180  # measured off the two tickets in sutra/loop.py
ARCHIVE_TICKETS = 20_000  # a real support desk after two years
CHARS_PER_TOKEN = 4  # a rough English estimate, NOT a tokenizer - see part 5.2
WINDOW_TOKENS = 128_000  # the context window the host must fit everything inside

DESIGNS = {
    "archive://all - the whole shelf in one read": ARCHIVE_TICKETS * TICKET_CHARS,
    "archive://recent - the last 200 tickets": 200 * TICKET_CHARS,
    "ticket://{id} - the one ticket asked about": TICKET_CHARS,
}
```

**Line by line:**

- `TICKET_CHARS = 180` is measured, not guessed — it is the length of the ticket bodies that have been
  in `sutra/loop.py` since Day 3. A constant with a comment saying where it came from can be argued
  with; one without cannot.
- `ARCHIVE_TICKETS = 20_000` is the assumption doing the work and it is stated as one. Underscores
  rather than `20000` so nobody counts zeros.
- `CHARS_PER_TOKEN = 4` is **an estimate and the comment says so.** Day 2 established that a token is
  not a word and not a fixed number of characters
  ([2.2.1](../../../day-02-llm-mechanics/parts/02-tokens-the-meter/2.1-what-a-token-is.md)). Four is the
  usual English rule of thumb. It is the right tool here because the conclusion is about orders of
  magnitude, and it would be the wrong tool for a billing calculation.
- `WINDOW_TOKENS = 128_000` is a window size the design must fit inside. It is a **planning figure**,
  not a claim about a specific model's current limit — the point of the table is the ratio, and a
  design that needs seven times the window is broken at any plausible number.
- `DESIGNS` maps a human-readable label to a character count, so the printed table reads as three
  designs rather than three integers. Each value is the arithmetic written out — `200 * TICKET_CHARS`
  rather than `36000` — because the reader should be able to see the shape of each design in its own
  row.

Run it:

```bash
uv run python days/day-35-resources-and-prompts/lab/context_cost.py; echo "exit: $?"
```

**Line by line:**

- `echo "exit: $?"` because this is a check: it exits non-zero when a design does not fit, and it is
  meant to be red.
- **Zero generations**, zero network. Multiplication and a comparison.

Measured on 2026-09-04:

```text
20,000 tickets, 180 characters each
window 128,000 tokens, estimated at 4 characters per token

shelf design                                          chars    ~tokens  % of window  fits
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
archive://all - the whole shelf in one read       3,600,000    900,000       703.1%  NO
archive://recent - the last 200 tickets              36,000      9,000         7.0%  yes
ticket://{id} - the one ticket asked about              180         45         0.0%  yes

designs that do not fit: 1
and the two that fit are not equal: one of them buries the answer in the middle
```

**703% of the window.** `archive://all` is not slow or expensive, it is impossible: a host that reads
it cannot use the result at all, and what it actually gets is a truncation or an error from its model
provider, at the far end, with nothing pointing back at your server.

The interesting row is the middle one, and it is the reason the last line of the output exists. Nine
thousand tokens **fits**. Nothing errors. The host attaches two hundred tickets, the one that matters
is somewhere among them, and the model answers about a different one — or hedges, or blends two
tickets into a reply that describes neither. That is the failure the paper above measured, and it is
the one you cannot detect from a status code.

**45 tokens** for the design that answers the question. The ratio between the second and third rows is
two hundred to one, and both of them "work".

## When it breaks

The three breaks, in increasing order of how long they take to find.

**It does not fit.** The host's model call fails, at the provider, with something like a context-length
error. Somebody looks at the host. Eventually somebody looks at the resource. This is the good case,
because it is loud.

**It fits and buries.** No error anywhere. The reply is confident, plausible and about the wrong
ticket. The only way to notice is to read the reply against the ticket, which is a human doing quality
assurance, which is the thing agents were supposed to reduce.

**It fits, buries, and is cached.** The host cached the nine-thousand-token read under
`archive://recent` for as long as `ttlMs` allows
([4.1](../04-freshness/4.1-every-read-carries-its-expiry.md)), so the same buried pile is attached to
every turn for the rest of the window, including turns about tickets that are not in it. Now the model
has stale context *and* irrelevant context, and both look like evidence.

The smallest fix is a design rule rather than a code change: **a resource returns the thing that was
asked for, and a summary is a different resource from the thing it summarises.** Concretely, for
Sutra's shelf:

- `ticket://{id}` — one ticket, always small, always the answer to a specific question.
- `archive://summary` — counts and how to start, deliberately tiny, and it stops naming individual ids
  the moment there are more than a handful.
- there is no `archive://all`, and the reason is written next to the registration.

If a caller genuinely needs many tickets, the answer is a **search tool** — the model chooses the
query, the server returns matches — which is [1.1](../01-who-initiates/1.1-the-hand-that-reaches-first.md)'s
rule arriving from the other side. Search is not reading, so it is not a resource.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead:** every resource has a documented size bound, and the handler
enforces it — a hard cap with an explicit truncation marker in the returned text, or a refusal. The
`size` field in `resources/list` exists for exactly this: a host that can see a resource is four
megabytes can decline before it reads. A server that publishes an unbounded resource has moved a
capacity decision into a caller who cannot see it coming.

**What degrades at scale:** everything about this failure is size-dependent and nothing about the code
is. The handler that returns the whole archive is three lines at two tickets and three lines at twenty
thousand. There is no gradual degradation to watch for either — it works, it works, it works, and then
a customer gets an answer about the wrong ticket.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic:** the fit is *per host*. The same nine-thousand
token read is nothing to a host with a large window and most of the budget to a host running a small
model with its own system prompt and ten other servers' tool declarations already in place. You cannot
see any of that. A resource that is fine in your test host is the thing that pushes a different host
over its limit, and the report you get says *"your server broke our agent"*.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"`archive://recent` returns two hundred tickets and
we tested it with three. Put the cap in the handler and put the byte count in the `size` field, because
right now the only thing bounding this is that our fixture is small."*

**The interview question:** *"What is the risk in exposing your database as MCP resources?"* The honest
answer: *"that a resource is context somebody else spends, and the server chose how much. We priced it:
the whole archive is seven times a hundred-and-twenty-eight-thousand-token window, so that one fails
loudly. The dangerous design is the one in between — a couple of hundred tickets fits comfortably and
buries the relevant one in the middle, which is exactly where *Lost in the Middle* showed retrieval
degrades. No error, a confident answer about the wrong ticket. So resources get size bounds and the
`size` field gets populated, and anything that needs a subset is a search tool, not a resource,
because choosing what is relevant is a decision and decisions belong on the tool surface."*

# 4.3 — The list that outlived the deploy

## One-line answer

`ttlMs` on `resources/list` is also **how long clients keep offering an address you deleted**, so
removing a resource is two deployments — shorten the TTL, wait it out, then remove — and doing it in
one produces a burst of `-32602` that looks like a client bug and is not.

## The story

The visiting card in your wallet is two years old and the number on it belongs to somebody else now.

You call it. Someone answers, and it is not the office. They are perfectly polite about it and they
have clearly had this conversation before, because the number was reassigned months ago and the cards
are still out there in wallets and pinned to noticeboards.

Nobody did anything wrong. The office printed correct cards. The phone company reassigned a number
that had been given up, which is what phone companies do. You dialled what was written down. The only
thing that failed is that a piece of paper cannot be recalled, and the office had no way of knowing how
many were still in circulation or where.

The offices that handle this well do one extra thing before the number goes: they leave it ringing
with a recorded message for a few months first. *This number has changed; the new one is…* It costs
them a line they are no longer using, and it converts a wrong number into a redirection.

## The idea in plain language

Day 32 established that list results are cacheable and that a client may keep one for as long as
`ttlMs` says
([32.3.3](../../../day-32-mcp-stateless-core/parts/03-headers-and-caches/3.3-lists-you-may-keep.md)).
The same sentence read from the other direction is the one this part is about: **for `ttlMs`
milliseconds after you delete something, clients are still entitled to believe it exists.**

They are not misbehaving. They are doing exactly what your own response instructed them to do. The
caching page is explicit that the freshness window is a promise you made:

> "If `ttlMs` is positive, the client **SHOULD** consider the result fresh for that many milliseconds
> after receiving the response."

And that a TTL is a hint about re-fetching, not a guarantee about content:

> "TTL is a **freshness hint**, not a guarantee. Servers MAY change the underlying data before the TTL
> expires. The TTL tells the client how long it can reasonably avoid re-fetching, not how long the
> data is guaranteed to remain unchanged."

So the protocol tells you plainly that this window exists. What it cannot do is close it for you.

There is a second mechanism that *shortens* the window and does not remove it. A server that declares
`listChanged` may send `notifications/resources/list_changed`, and the caching page says what a client
should do with it:

> "When a relevant notification is received while a cached response is still fresh, the notification
> **invalidates** the cached response and it should be considered immediately stale."

An immediate invalidation is exactly what you want — and after the 2026-07-28 revision a client only
receives it if it has an open `subscriptions/listen` stream with `resourcesListChanged: true` on it
([4.2](4.2-subscribing-with-nobody-on-the-line.md)). Clients that are not holding a stream get nothing.
Which is most of them, and by design, because holding streams is the thing statelessness was trying to
stop.

That leaves the **two-deployment rule**, and it is not a workaround, it is the procedure:

1. Deploy a change that lowers `ttlMs` on the affected list to something small. The resource is still
   there and still works.
2. Wait until the *old* TTL, the long one, has expired everywhere. Every client has now re-fetched and
   holds the short-TTL version.
3. Deploy the removal. The window in which anyone can be wrong is now the short TTL, not the long one.

Three steps, two deploys, one waiting period, and the waiting period is bounded by a number you chose
months earlier.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because Sutra's shelf is going to change, and the first thing that changes on any shelf is a name.

`kb://logout` and `kb://export` are the two knowledge-base keys today. The moment the knowledge base is
real, those keys become slugs, and slugs get renamed — an article is retitled, merged, or split in two.
Every rename is a deletion of one URI and a creation of another, and every deletion has this window in
front of it.

The number to pick is a decision Day 32 already asked for and today's build brief asks again for the
resource lists. The two answers are related but not equal: `tools/list` changes when you ship a tool,
which is rare, so a long TTL is cheap. `resources/list` on a live archive changes whenever the archive
does — and a summary resource that names the ticket ids it holds
([2.1](../02-the-shelf/2.1-an-address-not-a-call.md)) is stale the moment a ticket arrives.

It also decides what Day 44's client does. A client with a retry policy has to know that `-32602` on a
resource it read from a cached list means *my list is old*, not *the server is broken* — so it
re-fetches the list once and retries, rather than backing off and alerting.

## The mechanism

The arithmetic over one deployment:

```python
# days/day-35-resources-and-prompts/lab/stale_list.py  (extract)
CLIENTS = 40
READS_PER_CLIENT_PER_MS = 4 / 3_600_000  # four reads an hour, expressed per millisecond
TTL_CHOICES = {"one day": 86_400_000, "one hour": 3_600_000, "one minute": 60_000, "none": 0}


def doomed_reads(ttl_ms: int) -> int:
    """Reads of a deleted URI, from caches that were still fresh when it went away."""
    window = ttl_ms / 2  # on average a client is halfway through its TTL at deploy time
    return round(CLIENTS * READS_PER_CLIENT_PER_MS * window)
```

**Line by line:**

- `READS_PER_CLIENT_PER_MS` is written as a division rather than as a decimal so the reader can see
  where it came from — four reads, one hour, in milliseconds. A number like `1.111e-6` in the source
  is a number nobody can check.
- `TTL_CHOICES` is a dictionary from a readable label to the actual millisecond value, because the
  point of the table is precisely that `86_400_000` and "one day" are the same thing and only one of
  them is obvious.
- `window = ttl_ms / 2` is the assumption doing the work, and it is stated in a comment rather than
  hidden. Clients fetched the list at different moments, so at the instant you delete something, the
  average client is halfway through its freshness window. Halving is the simplest honest model; it is
  not a measurement.
- The multiplication is clients × rate × window, which is the whole calculation. There is no
  simulation and no randomness, because the shape of the answer does not need either.
- `round(...)` to an integer, because a fractional doomed read is not a thing and printing `1919.999`
  would suggest a precision this model does not have.

Run it:

```bash
uv run python days/day-35-resources-and-prompts/lab/stale_list.py
```

**Line by line:**

- From the repository root. **Zero generations**, zero network — three multiplications per row.

Measured on 2026-09-04:

```text
40 clients, four reads each an hour, one resource deleted in one deploy

ttlMs on resources/list           value    reads of a dead URI
--------------------------------------------------------------
one day                      86,400,000                   1920
one hour                       3,600,000                     80
one minute                        60,000                      1
none                                   0                      0

the two-deployment rule: shorten the TTL, wait it out, THEN delete.
the single-deployment version is the top row, and every one of those is a -32602
```

**1920 against 80.** The only difference between those two rows is a number you chose when you wrote
the handler, probably without much thought, probably a day because a day sounded generous.

The bottom row is the honest control. With no caching at all there are no doomed reads, and that is
what `mcp==1.29.1` gives you today by accident: it emits no `ttlMs` at all, so a conformant client
treats every list as immediately stale ([4.1](4.1-every-read-carries-its-expiry.md)) and re-fetches
constantly. Zero doomed reads and 2400 requests an hour. The bottom row is not free, it is the bill
paid in a different currency.

What arrives at the server during that window is not mysterious. It is the miss path from
[2.3](../02-the-shelf/2.3-the-miss-that-must-be-an-error.md), at volume:

```text
{"jsonrpc": "2.0", "id": 12, "error": {"code": -32602, "message": "Resource not found", "data": {"uri": "kb://logout"}}}
```

Which is why that part insisted the code survives to the wire. A thousand of these with `code: -32602`
and a `uri` in `data` is a graph you can read: *clients are holding a list from before the deploy.* A
thousand with `code: 0` and no data is a page of noise.

## When it breaks

The break is that it looks like somebody else's bug.

Your monitoring shows an error spike starting a minute after a deploy. The errors are `-32602`, which
is *Invalid params*, which reads as *the client sent something wrong*. The client team is paged. They
look at their code, which has not changed, and find it requesting a URI that their cached list told
them existed. Both teams are correct and the incident is a conversation.

The second break is worse and quieter: the resource was not deleted, it was **renamed**. Now
`kb://logout` is gone and `kb://unexpected-logouts` exists, and a client holding the old list reads the
old URI and gets `-32602`. A retry policy that re-fetches the list on `-32602` recovers gracefully; one
that treats `-32602` as permanent gives up, and the model answers without the article. Same error code,
two completely different client behaviours, and you cannot control which one your callers wrote.

The third break is a rollback. You deleted the resource, then rolled back the deploy because something
else went wrong. The resource is back. Clients that re-fetched during the gap are now holding a list
without it, for as long as *their* TTL says, and they will not ask again. A rollback does not roll back
a cache, and the staleness now runs in the direction nobody plans for.

The smallest fix is the procedure, written down where deploys are described rather than in a code
comment:

```text
removing or renaming a resource URI:
  deploy 1  lower ttlMs on resources/list (and templates/list) to 60000
  wait      the previous ttlMs, in full, from the moment deploy 1 is everywhere
  deploy 2  remove the URI; restore ttlMs
```

## In production

**What a professional writes instead:** `ttlMs` is chosen per list, written as a named constant in
milliseconds, and paired with a note saying what the removal procedure costs at that value. A list that
changes often gets a short TTL and accepts the traffic; a list that changes at deploy time gets a long
one and accepts the two-deploy dance. The choice is recorded because the person removing the resource
in eight months is not the person who picked the number.

**What degrades at scale:** the doomed-read count is linear in clients and in TTL, and clients grow.
Forty clients is a team; four hundred is a product; the 1920 in the table becomes 19 200 with the same
one-day TTL and the same single deploy. At that point the error spike is not a conversation, it is an
incident with a status page on it.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic:** cache stampede at the end of the window.
Every client that fetched during the deploy expires at roughly the same moment, so the re-fetch arrives
as a spike rather than a trickle — the same shape as [4.1](4.1-every-read-carries-its-expiry.md)'s and
for the same reason. Jitter on `ttlMs` spreads it, and it has to be added before the fleet is large.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"this pull request deletes `kb://logout` and the
`resources/list` handler still returns a one-day `ttlMs`. That is one deploy and about two thousand
`-32602` responses over the following day, and the client team will be paged for it. Split it: lower
the TTL, let it drain, then delete."*

**The interview question:** *"What does it cost to remove something from an MCP server?"* The answer
that shows you have done it: *"two deployments, because the TTL you put on your list is also how long
clients keep offering the thing you removed. We measured it — forty clients, four reads an hour, a
one-day TTL: about nineteen hundred requests for a URI that no longer exists, all of them `-32602`, all
of them the client doing exactly what our own response told it to do. The procedure is to lower the TTL
first, wait out the old one, then remove. The reason the notification path does not save you is that
after the stateless rewrite a client only gets `list_changed` if it is holding a `subscriptions/listen`
stream, and most are not — which is the whole point of being stateless."*

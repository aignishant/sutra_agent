# 3.1 — The ticket torn in two

## One-line answer

One `append_event` is not one row: it inserts the event, patches the session's state and moves the
session's `update_time`, and the reason it is safe is that all of it happens inside a single
transaction that either lands whole or leaves nothing behind.

## The story

You take a bag of clothes to the laundry on the corner.

The woman at the counter does not just take the bag. She writes on a small pad with two halves — the
count on both halves, *4 shirts, 2 trousers*, the date, a number. Then she tears along the
perforation, keeps the left half in a clip behind her, and gives you the right half.

Two pieces of paper, one fact. Neither half means anything on its own. Your half without hers is a
receipt for a bag nobody has; her half without yours is a bag nobody will claim.

Watch what she does if the phone rings while she is writing. She does not hand you the half-written
half. She puts the pen down, deals with the phone, comes back and finishes, *and then* tears. The
tear is the moment the transaction happens, and until it happens there is no transaction at all —
just a woman with a pen and a bag on the counter.

## The idea in plain language

When your agent produces a turn, the runner calls `append_event(session, event)`. It reads like one
thing. It is three things, and they are all in the file by the time the call returns:

1. a row inserted into `events`, holding the whole event as JSON;
2. the session's `state` column patched with whatever the event's `state_delta` changed;
3. the session's `update_time` moved forward to the event's timestamp.

Plus, when the delta touched them, an upsert into `user_states` or `app_states`
([1.3](../01-what-restart-costs/1.3-three-scopes-three-tables.md)).

Any two of those without the third is a lie about the conversation. An event with no state change
means the agent said something and forgot what it decided. A state change with no event means the
transcript does not explain how the ticket came to be closed. A moved `update_time` with neither
means every other worker now believes their copy is stale for no reason
([4.1](../04-two-workers/4.1-refused-not-overwritten.md)).

The word for "all of it or none of it" is **atomic** — from a Greek word meaning *indivisible*. A
group of statements the database treats as one indivisible step is a **transaction**. You open one
with `BEGIN`, you close it with `COMMIT`, and until the commit returns, nothing you wrote is visible
to anybody else and nothing survives a crash. In this service you will not find the word `BEGIN`
anywhere: the database driver opens the transaction for it, and the mechanism section says exactly
where.

That is the tear on the pad. Before the commit there is no transaction; after it there is; there is
no moment in between that anybody can observe.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because everything after this part assumes the write is atomic, and none of it works if it is not.

[4.1](../04-two-workers/4.1-refused-not-overwritten.md) refuses a second writer by comparing
`update_time` against the copy in that writer's hand. That comparison is only meaningful if
`update_time` moves at exactly the moment the event lands — not before, and not in a separate step
that could fail on its own.

[5.2](../05-shape-and-size/5.2-the-migration-that-passed.md) rebuilds a table under a live store,
and does it inside one transaction for exactly this reason: a migration interrupted halfway is a
store with two half-tables in it.

And this is the day's paper. *Principles of transaction-oriented database recovery* is where the
vocabulary in this part comes from — transaction, atomic, commit, and the argument that "did the
write go through?" is a question a system should be able to answer rather than guess at.

## The paper behind it

> **Principles of transaction-oriented database recovery** · `doi:10.1145/289.291` · 1983 ·
> `https://doi.org/10.1145/289.291`

It gave the field the transaction as a unit with four properties — atomicity, consistency,
isolation, durability — and a taxonomy of the failures a recovery method must survive.

Taught in full, with a demo you can crash, in
[`papers/01-transaction-oriented-recovery.md`](../../papers/01-transaction-oriented-recovery.md).

## The mechanism

Snapshot the file, make one call, snapshot again.

```python
# days/day-47-persistent-sessions/lab/writes.py  (extract)
event = Event(
    author="triage",
    invocation_id="inv-1",
    content=types.Content(role="model", parts=[types.Part(text="raised with the print team")]),
    actions=EventActions(state_delta={"status": "in_progress", "owner": "print-team"}),
)
returned = await svc.append_event(session, event)
```

**Line by line:**

- One event carries **both** the words and the decision. That is the shape to aim for: the sentence
  the customer sees and the state change it corresponds to travel together, so a transcript can
  never disagree with the state.
- `EventActions(state_delta=...)` is a delta, not a replacement. Two keys go in; every other key in
  the session's state is untouched, because
  [1.3](../01-what-restart-costs/1.3-three-scopes-three-tables.md)'s `json_patch` merges rather than
  overwrites.
- `append_event` **returns** the event. That is not the same object you passed in when temp-scoped
  keys were involved — the service trims them out first — so the returned value is what was actually
  stored and is the one to log.
- `session` is passed positionally and is the only positional parameter. It is also mutated: the
  call updates `session.last_update_time` and appends to `session.events` in place, so the handle in
  your hand stays usable for the next turn. That in-place update is what makes
  [4.1](../04-two-workers/4.1-refused-not-overwritten.md)'s failure possible for *another* handle
  and not for this one.

What the service runs inside the transaction, from its own source:

```sql
-- from sqlite_session_service.append_event, in order
UPDATE sessions SET state = json_patch(state, ?), update_time = ?
  WHERE app_name=? AND user_id=? AND id=?;

INSERT INTO events (id, app_name, user_id, session_id, invocation_id, timestamp, event_data)
  VALUES (?, ?, ?, ?, ?, ?, ?);
```

**Line by line:**

- The state patch runs **before** the insert, and both are before the single commit. Order inside a
  transaction is invisible from outside, which is the property being relied on: no reader ever sees
  the state changed and the event missing.
- There is no literal `BEGIN` in that source, and this is worth knowing exactly. The service opens
  its connection with `aiosqlite.connect(...)` and no `isolation_level`, so it inherits Python's
  default of `''` — the driver's legacy implicit-transaction mode. The driver issues a plain,
  **deferred** `BEGIN` before the first `INSERT` or `UPDATE`, and the single `await db.commit()` at
  the end closes it. The atomicity is real; the boundary is the driver's, not the ADK's.
- *Deferred* is doing work in that sentence and it comes back in
  [3.3](3.3-the-pragma-nobody-set.md) and
  [Day 43's 4.2](../../../day-43-stateless-by-default/parts/04-where-state-goes/4.2-shared-is-not-the-same-as-safe.md):
  a deferred `BEGIN` takes the write lock at the first write rather than at the `BEGIN`. Here that is
  safe, because the service reads nothing inside the transaction that it then computes from —
  `json_patch` does the arithmetic in the database. It would not be safe in code that read a value,
  decided, and wrote.
- `json_patch(state, ?)` does the merge in the database from the current value of the row, so there
  is no read in Python between the two. That is
  [Day 43's 4.2](../../../day-43-stateless-by-default/parts/04-where-state-goes/4.2-shared-is-not-the-same-as-safe.md)
  rule — never compute a new value from one you read in a separate statement — applied by the
  framework rather than by you.
- `update_time = ?` is bound to the **event's** timestamp, not to `now`. So the session's clock and
  the event's clock cannot drift apart, and `sessions.update_time` is always exactly the timestamp
  of the newest event.
- When the delta carried no session-scoped keys the service skips this `UPDATE` and issues a smaller
  one that moves `update_time` alone. The timestamp always moves; the state only moves when
  something changed.
- Both statements are parameterised with `?` rather than interpolated. Customer text goes into
  `event_data`, and customer text contains quotes — this is the ordinary reason for parameters and
  it is not optional.

## When it breaks

The before-and-after of one call, run on 2026-09-05:

```text
before append_event:
  sessions.state         {'status': 'open'}
  sessions.update_time   1788554860.4658134
  events rows            0
  file bytes             36864

after  append_event:
  sessions.state         {'status': 'in_progress', 'owner': 'print-team'}
  sessions.update_time   1788554860.4983487
  events rows            1
  file bytes             36864

what changed
  events rows           0 -> 1
  sessions.state        {'status': 'open'} -> {'status': 'in_progress', 'owner': 'print-team'}
  sessions.update_time  1788554860.4658134 -> 1788554860.4983487
  event.timestamp       1788554860.4983487
  session.last_update_time (in memory) 1788554860.4983487

  update_time == event timestamp: True
```

Three things changed and the file did not grow, because SQLite had already allocated the page. Size
is a poor proxy for "did the write happen"; the row count is the fact.

Now the break, and it is the one the day's paper exists for. Take the transaction away and crash a
process between the two statements. That is the ablation in
[the paper demo](../../papers/01-transaction-oriented-recovery.md), and this is what it prints:

```text
boundary        : none (ablation)
journal at crash: absent
turn_count      : 2
events stored   : 3  ['printer offline since tuesday', 'still offline after a reboot', 'reset the print spooler']
verdict         : TORN - half a turn survived
```

Three events and a counter that says two. Nothing is corrupt. Every row is well-formed, every column
has the right type, and any integrity check the database can run passes. The store is simply
**wrong**, in a way only your application knows how to notice, and it will stay wrong for as long as
the file exists.

With the boundary in place, the same crash at the same instant:

```text
boundary        : BEGIN IMMEDIATE .. COMMIT
journal at crash: 8720 bytes
turn_count      : 2
events stored   : 2  ['printer offline since tuesday', 'still offline after a reboot']
verdict         : consistent
```

Two and two. The half-written turn is not repaired, and it is not there. Those 8 720 bytes are the
rollback journal — the copy of the old pages SQLite wrote *before* touching the real ones, so that
whoever opens the file next can put them back. That file is the paper's mechanism, on your disk,
with a size.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead:** they do not hand-roll this for sessions, because the service
already does it. Where they do write it is everywhere they touch the store *beside* the service —
the deletion in
[6.1](../06-forgetting-on-purpose/6.1-delete-the-session-keep-the-customer.md), the migration in
[5.2](../05-shape-and-size/5.2-the-migration-that-passed.md), a backup, a repair script. The rule is
short enough to keep: **if two statements have to be true together, they go inside one
`BEGIN IMMEDIATE ... COMMIT`.**

**What degrades at scale:** everything between `BEGIN` and `COMMIT` is serialised against every other
writer on that file, so the transaction's length is the system's throughput ceiling. Which makes one
rule absolute: **never put a network call or a model call inside a transaction.** A five-second
model response inside a transaction is a five-second lock on the whole store, and it turns one slow
call into an outage for everyone.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic:** a torn write is not detected when it
happens. It is detected weeks later, by a customer reading a transcript that does not explain the
state, or by a report whose totals do not add up. By then the log lines from the crash have rotated
away, and there is nothing linking the two. That is why the invariant is worth writing down as a
check you can run — the paper demo's `verify` step is small enough to keep in the repository.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"this closes the ticket and then appends the
event, as two calls, with an `await` between them. If the process dies in that gap the ticket is
closed and the transcript never says who closed it, and we will find out from the customer. Put the
state change in the event's `state_delta` so the framework writes both in one transaction."*

**The interview question:** *"what does it mean to say a write is atomic?"* The honest answer: *"That
it either happens completely or not at all, with no state anybody can observe in between. It matters
because one logical write is usually several rows — appending a turn to a session is an insert into
the events table plus a patch to the session's state plus a move of its update timestamp, and any
two of the three without the last is a conversation that contradicts itself. I proved it by killing
a process between two of those writes: with the transaction the store came back with two turns and
two counted, without it three turns and two counted, and nothing was corrupt in either case — the
second one was just wrong, permanently, in a way no integrity check can see."*

# 4.1 — Refused, not overwritten

## One-line answer

Two workers holding the same session do not lock each other out and do not overwrite each other:
the store stamps every session with an `update_time`, refuses any append whose in-memory copy is
older, and raises `StaleSessionError` at the loser.

## The story

The shopping list lives on the fridge, held by a magnet, and three people write on it.

You are going to the market, so you take a photograph of the list on your phone and leave the paper
where it is. In the queue you remember that the oil is nearly finished, so you add oil — to the
photo, in your head — and you buy everything on your version.

While you were out, your sister came home, saw the list, and added *sugar* and *tea* to the paper.

You get back, and here is the moment. If you tear the old list off and write out a fresh one from
your phone, sugar and tea are gone, and nobody will ever know they were there. You did not ignore
her; you never saw her writing.

The households where this does not happen have one small habit. Before you rewrite the list, you
look at the paper. If it does not look like your photograph, you do not rewrite it — you add your
one line to what is actually there.

## The idea in plain language

Two workers can hold the same session at the same time. That is not an exotic case: it is a customer
who sends a second message before the first is answered, a retry from
[Day 44](../../../day-44-client-hardening/LESSON.md), a background job tidying a ticket while a
person is talking to it, or two instances of Sutra behind one URL.

There are two families of answers to this, and it is worth having both names.

**Pessimistic** concurrency control assumes a clash and prevents it: take a lock before you start,
and the second worker waits. That is
[Day 43's `BEGIN IMMEDIATE`](../../../day-43-stateless-by-default/parts/04-where-state-goes/4.2-shared-is-not-the-same-as-safe.md),
and it is right for a short read-decide-write against a counter.

**Optimistic** concurrency control assumes clashes are rare: everyone works on their own copy, and
at the moment of writing the store checks whether the copy is still current. If it is not, the write
is **refused** — nobody waits, and the loser is told.

The session store is optimistic, and once you see why, it is obviously the only choice available. A
turn takes as long as the model takes. A pessimistic lock held for the length of a turn would
serialise every conversation in the system behind the slowest one, which is exactly the "never hold
a lock across a network call" rule from
[3.3](../03-writes-that-survive/3.3-the-pragma-nobody-set.md), pointed at the session itself.

The mechanism is one column. Every session row has `update_time`. Every `Session` object you hold
has `last_update_time`, which is what that column said when you loaded it. `append_event` reads the
stored value first, compares, and raises if the store has moved on.

The exception is `StaleSessionError`. It subclasses `ValueError`, which the ADK's own source says is
for backwards compatibility with callers that were catching `ValueError` before the class existed.
That is worth knowing before you write `except ValueError` anywhere near a session.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because [Day 43](../../../day-43-stateless-by-default/LESSON.md) made every instance able to answer
every request, and this is the bill for it.

The moment two instances can serve the same customer, two instances can hold the same session. Day
43's [3.3](../../../day-43-stateless-by-default/parts/03-two-instances/3.3-two-answers-to-one-question.md)
was about two instances giving two answers; this is about two instances trying to *record* two
answers, and it is the better failure, because one of them is refused rather than silently winning.

It also decides how Sutra retries. Day 44's `with_retries` re-runs a call that failed. A retry that
re-uses the session object it loaded before the first attempt is holding a stale copy by
construction, and will be refused every time —
[4.3](4.3-losing-the-race-on-purpose.md) is that mistake measured. So the retry policy and the
session store have to agree about what gets re-read, and this part is the fact they have to agree
about.

## The mechanism

Two service objects, one session, one write each.

```python
# days/day-47-persistent-sessions/lab/stale.py  (extract)
handle_a = await a.get_session(app_name=APP, user_id=USER, session_id=SESSION)
handle_b = await b.get_session(app_name=APP, user_id=USER, session_id=SESSION)

await a.append_event(handle_a, note("worker-a", "escalated to the print team"))

if refetch:
    handle_b = await b.get_session(app_name=APP, user_id=USER, session_id=SESSION)

try:
    await b.append_event(handle_b, note("worker-b", "closed as duplicate"))
except StaleSessionError as err:
    refused = err
```

**Line by line:**

- `a` and `b` are two `SqliteSessionService` objects over the same file. They could as easily be two
  processes; the check is on timestamps in a row, not on process identity, so one process is enough
  to demonstrate it and much easier to read.
- Both handles are loaded **before** either write. That is the shape of the real race: not two writes
  at the same instant, but two reads followed by two writes, with thinking time in the middle.
- `a.append_event` mutates `handle_a` in place, setting its `last_update_time` to the new value. So A
  can keep writing all day. It is B's object that is now behind.
- The `refetch` branch is one line and it is the entire fix. Re-reading gives B a handle whose
  `last_update_time` matches the store, and its write is accepted.
- `except StaleSessionError` and not `except ValueError`, even though the first is a subclass of the
  second. Catching the specific class is what stops a genuine `ValueError` from somewhere else in
  the call being mistaken for a lost race.
- `StaleSessionError` is imported from `google.adk.errors._stale_session_error`. The leading
  underscore says the *module* is private; the class is named in
  `BaseSessionService.append_event`'s own docstring as what it raises. This is
  [2.1](../02-reaching-the-service/2.1-the-class-that-is-not-exported.md)'s situation again, and it
  gets the same treatment: import it in one place.

The check itself, from the service's source:

```python
# .venv/Lib/site-packages/google/adk/sessions/sqlite_session_service.py  (extract)
async with db.execute(
    "SELECT update_time FROM sessions WHERE app_name=? AND user_id=? AND id=?",
    (session.app_name, session.user_id, session.id),
) as cursor:
    row = await cursor.fetchone()
    if row is None:
        raise SessionNotFoundError(f"Session {session.id} not found.")
    storage_update_time = row["update_time"]
    if storage_update_time > session.last_update_time:
        raise StaleSessionError(
            "The last_update_time provided in the session object is"
            " earlier than the update_time in storage."
            " Please check if it is a stale session."
        )
```

**Line by line:**

- The `SELECT` and everything after it run inside the same transaction as the write from
  [3.1](../03-writes-that-survive/3.1-the-ticket-torn-in-two.md), so nobody can commit between the
  check and the insert.
- A missing row raises `SessionNotFoundError`, a **different** exception from the stale one. Deleted
  and superseded are genuinely different situations and it is right that they are distinguishable —
  one means retry after re-reading, the other means stop.
- The comparison is `>`, strictly greater. Equal timestamps pass, which is what allows a handle that
  is exactly current to write.
- The timestamps are floating-point seconds
  ([1.2](../01-what-restart-costs/1.2-a-session-is-four-tables.md)), and they come from the event
  rather than from the database's clock. Two writers on two machines with clocks a second apart can
  therefore produce a `storage_update_time` in the future, and every subsequent append from the
  slower machine is refused until its clock catches up. That is
  [Day 22's paper](../../../day-22-structured-logging/papers/01-time-clocks-ordering.md) arriving as
  an outage.
- The message is a full sentence with no session id in it. That is the message you will meet in a
  log, so the session id has to come from your own logging around the call.

## When it breaks

Two workers, no re-read, run on 2026-09-05:

```text
A loaded, last_update_time = 1788555199.1955743
B loaded, last_update_time = 1788555199.1955743

A wrote. stored update_time is now 1788555199.2376895
B still believes it is       1788555199.1955743

B refused: StaleSessionError: The last_update_time provided in the session object is earlier than the update_time in storage. Please check if it is a stale session.

arm            : both write from their own copy
appends tried  : 2
events stored  : 1
verdict        : one append refused
exit: 1
```

Two appends tried, one stored. B's note — *closed as duplicate* — is not in the transcript, and B
knows it, because B got an exception.

Now the one-line fix:

```text
B re-read.  last_update_time = 1788555200.9136233

B wrote.

arm            : --refetch
appends tried  : 2
events stored  : 2
verdict        : both appends landed
```

**This is a good failure and it is worth saying why out loud.** Compare it with
[Day 43's 4.2](../../../day-43-stateless-by-default/parts/04-where-state-goes/4.2-shared-is-not-the-same-as-safe.md),
where four processes doing a hundred increments landed on thirty and nothing was raised. Seventy
successful writes were overwritten by seventy other successful writes, and the only way to know was
to have expected a hundred and counted. Here, nothing is overwritten. One write is refused, loudly,
at the moment it happens, at the caller who can do something about it.

The break is what happens when nobody catches it. `StaleSessionError` inherits from `ValueError`,
and a great deal of ordinary Python catches `ValueError` — a parsing helper, a validation wrapper,
a `try` around a block that also does an `int()`. Swallow it there and you are back in Day 43's
world: a note that was never recorded, no exception anywhere, and a transcript that is missing a
line nobody knows to look for.

The second break is subtler and it is the one that produces support tickets. The refused write is
**the second one**, and the second one is often the more important. A worker retrying a failed
escalation, a background job closing a ticket, a customer's follow-up message: all of these arrive
after something else has touched the session. Optimistic control tells you which write lost, and it
does not tell you which write mattered.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead:** they never let a `StaleSessionError` reach a bare
`except ValueError`, and they never retry the same handle. The pattern is small and it is always the
same three steps — re-read the session, re-apply the intention to the fresh copy, write — with a
bounded number of attempts. [4.3](4.3-losing-the-race-on-purpose.md) writes it. They also log the
session id, because the exception's own message does not carry it.

**What degrades at scale:** the refusal rate rises with the square of the writers on one session,
and every refusal is wasted work — the model call that produced the answer already happened. Below a
handful of contending writers this is a non-event; above it, most attempts are refused and the
system spends its quota producing answers it then throws away. The lever is not tuning the retry
loop; it is reducing how many things write to one session at once.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic:** clock skew, described above. On one machine
the timestamps come from one clock and the mechanism is exact. Across several machines they do not,
and a machine whose clock runs slightly slow finds *every* append refused — consistently, for one
instance, while its neighbours are fine. That reads as "one bad instance" and sends people restarting
containers rather than checking time synchronisation.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"this wraps `append_event` in
`except ValueError: pass` because 'sometimes it throws on bad state'. `StaleSessionError` subclasses
`ValueError`, so what this actually does is silently drop every write that lost a race. Catch
`StaleSessionError` explicitly, re-read the session, re-apply, and give up after a bounded number of
attempts with a log line carrying the session id."*

**The interview question:** *"two workers touch the same conversation. What happens?"* The honest
answer: *"The store is optimistic rather than locking. Every session row has an `update_time` and
every session object remembers the value it loaded, and `append_event` compares them inside the same
transaction as the write — if the store has moved on, it raises `StaleSessionError` instead of
writing. Nobody waits, which matters here because a turn is as long as a model call and a
pessimistic lock would serialise every conversation. The trap is that `StaleSessionError` subclasses
`ValueError`, so an over-broad except turns a loud refusal into a silently dropped turn. And the fix
is never to retry the same object — you re-read the session and re-apply, because the object in your
hand is stale by definition."*

# 3.3 — response_match_score, and the word "not"

## One-line answer

💥 Measured against the same six cases at ADK's own default threshold: **six correct answers, reworded,
all fail** — and **five of six answers that say the opposite of the truth pass**, one of them at 0.941,
because ROUGE counts shared words and "not" is one word.

## The story

A spell-checker marking a school essay.

It is genuinely useful. It finds the misspellings, it finds the doubled words, it finds the missing
full stop. Nobody would want to give it up.

Then a child hands in an essay in which every sentence is grammatically perfect and the whole thing is
about the wrong book. The spell-checker gives it a clean bill. Not because it is broken — it did
exactly what it does — but because "is this about the right book" was never a question it was asked,
and there is nothing in a clean spell-check that says so.

The dangerous version is not the child who realises this. It is the teacher who has been using the
tool for a year and has slowly stopped reading the essays, because the tool has never once been wrong
about the thing it checks.

## The idea in plain language

`response_match_score` is the metric on the first surface from part
[1.2](../01-a-test-with-a-score/1.2-three-surfaces-you-can-assert-on.md): the final answer.
`adk.dev/evaluate/` describes it as *"ROUGE-1 similarity to reference response"*, and each of those
words is worth unpacking.

**ROUGE-1** compares two pieces of text by their **unigrams** — single words, ignoring order. Take the
words of the reference, take the words of the response, and compute:

- **precision** — of the response's words, what fraction appear in the reference;
- **recall** — of the reference's words, what fraction appear in the response;
- **f-measure** — the harmonic mean of the two, which is the number this metric reports.

That is the whole algorithm. There is no model, no embedding, no notion of meaning. It is a set
comparison over words, and its cost is zero requests at any suite size — which is why it is in this
section with the trajectory metric.

Two consequences follow directly from "ignoring order and ignoring meaning", and they point in
opposite directions.

**A correct answer in different words scores low.** "Ticket 4740 has been open for eleven days" and
"4740 has been sitting with us since the eleventh" share almost no words. Both are correct. The metric
cannot tell.

**A wrong answer in the same words scores high.** "Ticket 4740 has **not** been open for eleven days"
differs from the reference by one word out of eight. The metric sees seven-eighths agreement.

That second one is not a curiosity. Negation, quantity and direction are exactly the things a support
desk gets wrong — *not*, *no longer*, *twelve* against *twenty*, *you are waiting on us* against *we
are waiting on you* — and every one of them is a small edit in word-space and a total reversal in
meaning. Word overlap is systematically blind to the errors that matter most and systematically harsh
on the variation that matters least.

ADK's default threshold for this metric is `0.8`, from `_DEFAULT_EVAL_CONFIG` on the installed
`google-adk==2.7.1`. The measurements below are all against that number.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because this is the metric Sutra can afford. Part
[4.1](../04-what-it-costs-to-run/4.1-the-metrics-that-spend-nothing.md) prices the alternative —
`final_response_match_v2`, which puts a model in the loop and costs requests per case per sample — and
Day 78's gate could not even determine whether the lane it would run on has an allowance.

So the honest position is not "use ROUGE". It is: **use ROUGE, and know exactly what it cannot see,
and do not let a green run on it be read as a claim about correctness.** This part is that knowledge,
measured rather than asserted, and Day 81's LLM-as-judge is the day that buys the missing half.

It also determines what the six reference answers are worth. Part
[1.3](../01-a-test-with-a-score/1.3-the-case-is-the-unit.md) wrote one correct wording per case knowing
there were others. The measurement below is what that decision costs.

## The mechanism

The evaluator, and the one difference from part [3.1](3.1-tool-trajectory-avg-score.md)'s:

```python
evaluator = RougeEvaluator(EvalMetric(metric_name="response_match_score", threshold=THRESHOLD))
```

**Line by line:**

- `RougeEvaluator` lives in `google.adk.evaluation.final_response_match_v1` — the module is named after
  the metric family and the class after the algorithm. Verified against `adk.dev/evaluate/` on
  2026-09-06 and against the installed `google-adk==2.7.1`.
- Its constructor takes `eval_metric: EvalMetric` and **not** a bare threshold, unlike
  `TrajectoryEvaluator`. That inconsistency between two evaluators in the same package is real and
  worth knowing before you spend twenty minutes on a `TypeError`.
- `metric_name="response_match_score"` is the string from `PrebuiltMetrics`, which on 2.7.1 lists
  `TOOL_TRAJECTORY_AVG_SCORE`, `RESPONSE_EVALUATION_SCORE`, `RESPONSE_MATCH_SCORE`, `SAFETY_V1`,
  `FINAL_RESPONSE_MATCH_V2` and eight more.
- This module is the first thing in the day that needs a package outside the base install. `rouge-score`
  is imported through a shim, `google/adk/dependencies/rouge_scorer.py`, whose entire contents are two
  re-export lines — which is part [4.3](../04-what-it-costs-to-run/4.3-the-eval-extra-pulls-the-cloud-in.md)'s
  subject.

What the library actually computes, from `final_response_match_v1.py` on 2.7.1:

```python
  scorer = rouge_scorer.RougeScorer(["rouge1"], tokenizer=_UnicodeAwareTokenizer(use_stemmer=True))
  scores = scorer.score(reference, candidate)
  return scores["rouge1"]
```

**Line by line:**

- `["rouge1"]` — unigrams only. ROUGE-2 (word pairs) and ROUGE-L (longest common subsequence) would
  both carry some order information; neither is used.
- `use_stemmer=True` means "opened" and "open" count as the same word, via the Porter stemmer from
  `nltk`. That helps the reworded case slightly and helps the negated case not at all, because "not"
  has no stem in common with anything.
- `scorer.score(reference, candidate)` — **reference first**. Getting these the wrong way round swaps
  precision and recall, which changes the f-measure whenever the two texts are different lengths.
- The score returned is `scores["rouge1"].fmeasure` in the caller. Precision and recall are both
  computed and both discarded, which is a small loss: a response that is correct but verbose has high
  recall and low precision, and the f-measure hides which.

Now the same six facts, said in different words. Every one of these is correct — a person marking them
would tick all six:

```bash
uv run python respmatch.py --reworded; echo "exit: $?"
```

**Line by line:**

- `--reworded` swaps in `REWORDED`, six answers that a support desk might plausibly give. Same facts,
  same tickets, same numbers, different sentences.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
desk: reworded, same facts

  case              score  status
  counts            0.583  FAILED
  age_4740          0.353  FAILED
  needs_person      0.533  FAILED
  one_lookup        0.571  FAILED
  order_matters     0.414  FAILED
  no_tool_needed    0.600  FAILED

  0 of 6 cases at or above 0.8
  6 correct answer(s) scored as wrong
exit: 1
```

**Zero of six.** Not one correct rewording clears the threshold, and the best of them is 0.600 against
a line at 0.800. If the desk's wording changed at all — a new prompt, a new model version, a style
guide — this metric reports a total regression on a system that got no worse.

Now the opposite experiment. Take each reference and **negate it**, keeping the wording as close as
possible. Every one of these is wrong, and several are dangerously wrong:

```bash
uv run python respmatch.py --negated; echo "exit: $?"
```

**Line by line:**

- `--negated` swaps in `NEGATED`. "has been open" becomes "has not been open"; "Two tickets need you"
  becomes "No tickets need you"; "twelve open in total" becomes "twenty open in total".

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
desk: negated, opposite facts

  case              score  status
  counts            0.909  PASSED
  age_4740          0.941  PASSED
  needs_person      0.750  FAILED
  one_lookup        0.812  PASSED
  order_matters     0.889  PASSED
  no_tool_needed    0.848  PASSED

  5 of 6 cases at or above 0.8
  5 wrong answer(s) scored as correct
exit: 1
```

**Five of six wrong answers pass.** Read the two blocks against each other and the shape is complete:

| | Correct answers, reworded | Wrong answers, same words |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cases passing at 0.8 | **0 of 6** | **5 of 6** |
| Best score | 0.600 | 0.941 |
| Worst score | 0.353 | 0.750 |

The metric is not merely imperfect. On this fixture it is **inverted**: it prefers a wrong answer to a
right one, reliably, across all six cases. `age_4740` is the clearest single instance — 0.353 for
*"4740 has been sitting with us since the eleventh"*, which is true, and 0.941 for *"Ticket 4740 has
**not** been open for eleven days"*, which is false.

And notice which one it did catch. `needs_person` scored 0.750 and failed, because negating *"Two
tickets need you: 4688 and 4740"* required changing two words, not one — *"No tickets need you: not
4688 and not 4740"*. The metric's sensitivity to a lie is proportional to how many words the lie takes
to tell, which is not a property anybody would design.

## When it breaks

There is no error text. Both arms above run cleanly and produce well-formed numbers; the failure is
entirely in what the numbers mean.

The way it bites in practice is the reworded direction, because that is the one that produces alerts.
A model version changes, the desk's phrasing shifts, and the nightly reports:

```text
  0 of 6 cases at or above 0.8
  6 correct answer(s) scored as wrong
```

on a system that is working. After the second such morning, somebody lowers the threshold. There is a
number at which the reworded answers pass — around 0.35 — and at that threshold **every single one of
the negated answers passes too**, along with essentially any text of similar length. The metric has not
been tuned; it has been switched off while still appearing in the results table.

The smallest fix is not a threshold. It is to stop asking this metric the question it cannot answer.
Three things that do work, in increasing order of cost:

1. **Assert on the facts, not the sentence.** A tiny custom metric that checks the answer contains
   `4740` and `eleven` is immune to rewording and catches the negation only if you also check for the
   absence of `not` — which is fragile, and honest about being fragile.
2. **Assert on the trajectory instead**, where exactness is meaningful — part
   [3.1](3.1-tool-trajectory-avg-score.md).
3. **Use a judge model**, which is `final_response_match_v2` and Day 81's subject. It costs requests
   per case per sample, which part
   [4.1](../04-what-it-costs-to-run/4.1-the-metrics-that-spend-nothing.md) prices at 9 times a day's
   observed allowance for six cases.

## In production

**What a professional does with ROUGE.** Uses it as a **change detector**, not a correctness check. Its
real strength is that it is free, deterministic and reproducible, so it is excellent at answering "did
the wording move?" — which is a genuine and useful question when you have not touched the prompt. It is
worthless at "is this right", and a team that writes `response_match_score` on a dashboard labelled
"accuracy" has built the spell-checker the teacher stopped reading past.

**What degrades at scale.** Reference answers written by one person in one sitting share that person's
vocabulary, so the metric scores well for as long as the system's phrasing resembles theirs and falls
off a cliff the first time somebody else edits the prompt. The decline is not gradual and it is not
correlated with quality.

**The failure that only shows with real traffic.** Real answers are longer than fixture answers, and
ROUGE's f-measure is length-sensitive in an unhelpful way: a long, correct, thorough answer against a
short reference has low precision and scores badly, so the metric systematically penalises the desk for
being more helpful. Nobody notices, because the cases that suffer most are the ones about complicated
tickets, which are also the ones nobody re-reads.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Rename the column. If this appears anywhere as
'accuracy' somebody will make a decision on it. It is a wording-drift detector, so call it that, and
put the negation result from this experiment in the file next to it as a comment — 0.941 for an answer
that says the opposite is the single most useful fact about this metric and it needs to be findable by
whoever inherits this."*

**The interview question:** *"You are scoring generated answers against reference answers with word
overlap. What can go wrong?"* The answer that shows experience gives both directions — correct
paraphrases score low, and semantically inverted answers score high — and ideally has a number for the
second, because everybody knows the first and almost nobody has measured the second.

# 1.3 — The case is the unit

## One-line answer

One case is **one behaviour you would be upset to lose**, named, with an id you can say out loud —
and the discipline that makes a suite useful is that every case has a reason somebody could state,
not that the suite covers a lot of inputs.

## The story

A cook writes down a dish so somebody else can make it.

The first draft is a paragraph: *"take dal, cook it, add tadka, salt to taste."* It is not wrong.
Anybody who already knows how to make it will nod. Anybody who does not will produce something and
have no idea which of the ten things they did was the reason it came out badly.

The second draft is a list. Soak for this long. This much water. Add the salt after, not before,
because before makes it take longer to soften. Tadka in ghee, not oil, and put the hing in when the
seeds have stopped. Every line is one thing, and — this is the part that makes it a good recipe rather
than a long one — several of the lines carry a small reason attached: *after, not before, because*.

Now when it comes out wrong, the person can ask which line they got wrong. And when somebody wants to
change a line, the reason is right there to argue with.

The paragraph and the list contain the same knowledge. Only one of them can be debugged.

## The idea in plain language

A **case** is the unit an eval suite is made of, and the temptation with any unit is to make it as
large as possible so there are fewer of them. That is the paragraph.

Three properties make a case a unit rather than a lump.

**It has an id, and the id is a name.** `eval_id="one_lookup"` rather than `case_4` or `test_007`. The
id is what appears in a failing run's output at eight in the morning, and `one_lookup FAILED` sends
somebody to the right place while `case_4 FAILED` sends them to open a file. Sutra has argued this
before about folders — plan §17.2 says a number alone is an address, not an answer — and it is the
same argument.

**It is one behaviour.** Not one input. The `no_tool_needed` case exists because there is a specific
thing that can go wrong: a desk that has learned to call a tool for everything, including "who are
you?". That is a behaviour. Ten variations on "how long has 4740 been open" are ten inputs and one
behaviour, and they will all pass or all fail together, which means nine of them cost you time and
tell you nothing.

**It has a reason somebody could state.** This is the *after, not before, because*. Two of this day's
six cases carry one in the fixture, and the reason is a sentence rather than a category:

```text
the desk must not read the whole queue to answer a question about one ticket
what needs a person is looked up first, because it is said first (day 77, part 2.3)
```

A case with a stated reason survives being wrong. When it fails and the failure turns out to be
acceptable, the reason is what somebody argues with before deleting it. A case with no reason gets
deleted the first time it is inconvenient, and nobody can tell what was lost.

There is a fourth property that is not about one case but about the set, and it is worth naming here
because it is what section 2 is about: **the cases are written down in a file, not in code.** A case
is data — an input, a reference, an id — and treating it as data is what lets somebody who does not
write Python add one.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because the desk is about to acquire a suite that will be run every night for the rest of this
curriculum, and the difference between a suite that survives that and one that gets switched off is
almost entirely whether its failures are diagnosable.

The six cases in this day's fixture are deliberately shaped by behaviour rather than by coverage.
`counts`, `age_4740` and `needs_person` are the three ordinary things the desk does. `one_lookup` is
a cost claim from Day 78. `order_matters` is Day 77's ordering argument. `no_tool_needed` is the case
that catches a desk which has learned to always reach for a tool. Six cases, six reasons, and no two
of them fail for the same underlying cause — which is what makes the pass count in part
[3.1](../03-two-metrics-that-cost-nothing/3.1-tool-trajectory-avg-score.md) readable.

## The paper behind it

> **Beyond Accuracy: Behavioral Testing of NLP models with CheckList**
> `arXiv:2005.04118` · 2020 · <https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.04118>

It argued that a single held-out accuracy number tells you almost nothing about what a language model
can and cannot do, and proposed testing **capabilities** one at a time instead — the same move from
paragraph to list that this part is making.

Taught in this day's paper part:
[Beyond accuracy — testing behaviours, not averages](../../papers/01-behavioral-testing.md).

## The mechanism

A case is a small frozen record, and the fields are the properties above:

```python
@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Case:
    """One thing the desk is supposed to get right, written down before it is run."""

    eval_id: str
    question: str
    tools: tuple[tuple[str, dict[str, str]], ...]
    answer: str
    note: str = field(default="")
```

**Line by line:**

- `eval_id` first, because it is the name and the name is how the case is referred to everywhere
  else — in the evalset file, in a failing run, and in the `adk eval` CLI's
  `file.json:eval_1,eval_2` filter syntax.
- `question` is the input. One question per case, because a case is one behaviour and a second
  question is a second behaviour or a duplicate.
- `tools` is a **tuple of tuples**, not a list of dicts, so the whole `Case` can be `frozen=True` and
  hashable. That is not tidiness: a frozen case cannot be mutated by the code that runs it, which
  means a run cannot quietly adjust the thing it is being scored against. The reference has to be
  immutable or it is not a reference.
- `answer` is the reference response — one correct wording, knowing there are others. Part
  [3.3](../03-two-metrics-that-cost-nothing/3.3-response-match-and-the-word-not.md) measures exactly
  what that costs.
- `note` is the *after, not before, because*. It defaults to empty, which is an admission: not every
  case has earned a reason, and forcing one would produce six sentences of ceremony. Four of the six
  have none, and the two that do are the two that would otherwise be deleted.

And the set:

```python
CASES: tuple[Case, ...] = (
    Case(
        eval_id="one_lookup",
        question="is 4652 blocked?",
        tools=(("ticket", {"ticket_id": "4652"}),),
        answer="No. Ticket 4652 is an export query, open four days, and nobody is waiting on you.",
        note="the desk must not read the whole queue to answer a question about one ticket",
    ),
    ...,
)
```

**Line by line:**

- A `tuple` rather than a list, for the same reason each case is frozen: the suite is a fixture, and a
  fixture something can append to during a run is not a fixture.
- The cases are in **the order they were thought of**, not sorted or grouped. That is deliberate and
  it is how real suites grow — appended as behaviours are noticed. It also matters for part
  [3.2](../03-two-metrics-that-cost-nothing/3.2-exact-match-right-default-wrong-metric.md), where
  taking "the first N cases" as a cheap sample turns out to be biased precisely because of this order.
- `tools=(("ticket", {"ticket_id": "4652"}),)` — the trailing comma makes it a one-element tuple.
  Without it, Python sees a parenthesised expression and `tools` becomes a bare tuple of two things,
  which fails later in a confusing place.

Run the suite and read the id column:

```bash
cd days/day-79-evals-are-tests/lab
uv run python trajectory.py --buggy
```

**Line by line:**

- The `--buggy` arm is the one worth reading here, because a green run tells you nothing about whether
  your ids are any good.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
  case              score  status   tools called
  counts             1.00  PASSED   ['category_counts']
  age_4740           1.00  PASSED   ['ticket']
  needs_person       1.00  PASSED   ['open_tickets']
  one_lookup         0.00  FAILED   ['open_tickets', 'ticket']
  order_matters      0.00  FAILED   ['category_counts', 'open_tickets']
  no_tool_needed     1.00  PASSED   []
```

**Read the two failing ids on their own, with the scores covered up.** `one_lookup` failed. Somebody
who has never opened this repository can tell you what that means: the desk did more than one lookup.
`order_matters` failed: it did things in the wrong order. Neither of them needs the score column, the
tools column, or a document.

That is the entire payoff of naming cases after behaviours, and it is worth measuring against the
alternative: `case_4 FAILED, case_5 FAILED` carries exactly the same information content and none of
the same usefulness.

## When it breaks

Cases break by **overlapping**, and the symptom is a failure count that is bigger than the number of
problems.

Add three more cases about ticket ages — `age_4688`, `age_4652`, `age_of_oldest` — and one bug in the
`ticket` tool fails four cases at once. The suite reports `2 of 6` becoming `2 of 9`, three of the new
failures are the same failure, and whoever is on call has to read all four to discover they are one.

There is no error text for this. It looks like this, and the giveaway is the repetition in the
`tools called` column rather than anything in the numbers:

```text
  age_4740           0.00  FAILED   ['open_tickets', 'ticket']
  age_4688           0.00  FAILED   ['open_tickets', 'ticket']
  age_4652           0.00  FAILED   ['open_tickets', 'ticket']
  one_lookup         0.00  FAILED   ['open_tickets', 'ticket']
```

The smallest fix is a question asked before adding a case, and it is the one-idea test from plan §17.1
pointed at a suite rather than at a document: **could this case fail while the one next to it passes?**
If not, it is the same case with different inputs, and the honest thing is to keep one and delete the
rest — or, if the inputs really do matter, to make them a *parameterised* case so the suite reports
one failure with three examples rather than three failures.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead of a big case list.** A small set of cases with reasons, plus a
much larger set of *generated* variations underneath them. That is the paper's own structure: a
capability, and then many cheap instances of it. The suite reports at the capability level — one line
per behaviour — and only expands into the instances when one goes red. Part
[papers/01](../../papers/01-behavioral-testing.md) has the demo.

**What degrades at scale.** Case sets grow by accretion and never shrink. Every incident adds a case
and nobody deletes one, so after two years the suite has four hundred cases, of which perhaps sixty
can independently fail. The rest are duplicates that cost time on every run and dilute every report.
The only defence anybody has found is a periodic cull with a rule: a case that has never failed alone
is a candidate for deletion, and the reason field is what decides.

**The failure that only shows with real traffic.** Cases are written from behaviours somebody
*noticed*, which means the suite's coverage is shaped like the team's attention, not like the traffic.
Sutra's six cases are all about tickets that exist and questions that make sense. Nothing in them is
malformed, empty, in another language, or hostile — and those are a large fraction of real input.
This is the same blind spot Day 66's threat model attacks from the security side, and an eval suite
inherits it silently.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Good ids. Two asks. Put the reason on every case
that is not self-evident from its id — the two you have are the two that would survive a fight, and I
want that for the others too. And before you add the seventh case, tell me what it can fail on that
none of the six can. If you cannot answer, it is an input, not a case."*

**The interview question:** *"How do you decide what goes in an eval set?"* The answer that shows
experience does not talk about coverage or sample size. It says: one case per behaviour you would be
upset to lose, named so that the failure line is the diagnosis — and can then say what it does when
two cases always fail together, because that is the question the first answer walks into.

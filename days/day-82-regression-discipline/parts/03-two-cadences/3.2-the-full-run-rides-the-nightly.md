# 3.2 — The full run rides the nightly

## One-line answer

Addendum 02 says to put full eval runs on Day 73's nightly *"so daily quota resets work for you"* —
and the two judged checks want **270 requests** against a measured allowance of **20**, so the nightly
is where the bill lands and it is still thirteen times too big.

## The story

The dhobi comes on Tuesdays.

Not because Tuesday is special. Because clothes accumulate at a rate, and collecting them once a week
matches that rate, and doing it any more often would mean somebody standing at the door for two shirts.

The arrangement has a property nobody states: **the batch is the unit of cost.** One trip for a week's
washing costs one trip. Seven trips for the same washing costs seven, and the washing is the same
washing.

And it has a failure that everybody has had. You realise on Wednesday morning that the shirt you need
went on Tuesday. The batching that made it cheap also made the turnaround a week, and there is no
version of this where it is both cheap and immediate.

## The idea in plain language

Part [3.1](3.1-the-fast-half-on-every-commit.md) put the six free checks on every commit. This part is
where the other two go, and the instruction is already written.

Addendum 02 §5's row for days 79–83 says three things, and the third is this part:

> Run evals on **Flash-Lite** (highest RPD); keep per-run evalsets small (10–15 cases dev, full 60+
> only at phase gates); schedule full runs as the Day 73 ambient *nightly* job so daily quota resets
> work for you.

*"So daily quota resets work for you"* is the argument in six words. A daily allowance refills once
per window — Day 78 part 1.1 — so work that fits inside one window's allowance can be done once per
window and cannot be done more often. Batching is not an optimisation here; it is the only shape that
fits.

Three things follow, and the third is the one Day 78 already complicated.

**The batch is the unit.** 270 requests as one nightly run is 270 a day. The same checks on every
commit are 3,240, which part [3.1](3.1-the-fast-half-on-every-commit.md) measured. Same work, twelve
times the bill.

**The turnaround becomes a night.** A regression introduced at ten in the morning is found at half past
midnight, by a job nobody is watching, and reported to somebody at seven. Fourteen hours of latency,
bought with the batching, and it is a real cost rather than a technicality — fourteen hours is long
enough for three more commits to land on top of the one that caused it. Part
[papers/01](../../papers/01-which-change-broke-it.md) is about what you do then.

**And it still does not fit.** 270 against 20. Day 78's whole point: the nightly does not create
allowance, it chooses which window to spend it in. Batching turned an impossible per-commit cost into
a merely impossible per-night one, and the remaining gap is the measurement Day 78's criterion 3 could
not make.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because Day 73 built the nightly and this is the stage that makes it expensive. Day 78 part 1.3 priced
Phase 11's whole schedule at 62 requests a day on the Flash lane; this stage alone is 270 on the
Flash-Lite lane, which is four times the entire ambient phase.

And because Day 73's job already has the shape this needs. Three stages in a forced order, each
checking for the file the previous one wrote, resumable, with a run log. Adding an eval stage is
adding a stage to something that already knows how to die half-way and carry on — which is why this
part is short on mechanism and long on arithmetic.

## The mechanism

The nightly's stages, with the eval stage's verdict carried into the job's own. From `lab/nightly.py`:

```python
STAGES = ("reindex", "evals", "digest")


def digest_line(regressions: list[str], total: int, passed: int) -> str:
    """The one sentence a person reads over tea. Day 73's digest, with a verdict in it."""
    if not regressions:
        return f"evals: {passed} of {total} passed, nothing regressed"
    named = ", ".join(regressions)
    return f"evals: {passed} of {total} passed, REGRESSED: {named}"
```

**Line by line:**

- Three stages in the order Day 73 fixed: the index has to exist before the evals run against it, and
  the digest has to have something to summarise. The eval stage sits in the middle because it depends
  on one and feeds the other.
- `digest_line` produces **one sentence**, because that is the interface. Day 73 part 3.2 established
  that the digest is the entire channel between a job nobody watched and the people who own it, and
  Day 77's standup reads it aloud.
- The sentence carries a count **and** a list of names. `6 of 9 passed` alone is part
  [1.1](../01-what-a-regression-is/1.1-a-regression-is-a-comparison.md)'s useless report; `REGRESSED:
  one_lookup` is a thing somebody can act on before breakfast.
- `REGRESSED` is in capitals for the same reason Day 78 part 3.2's `NOT RUN` was: the reader is
  skimming, and the clause that matters has to survive skimming.
- The no-regression branch says *"nothing regressed"* explicitly rather than omitting the clause. Day
  77 part 3.1's rule — a quiet morning has to be a sentence somebody wrote, never the absence of one.

And the regression filter, which reuses part
[1.2](../01-what-a-regression-is/1.2-three-things-that-move-a-number.md)'s classifier rather than
re-deciding what counts:

```python
    regressions = [
        case
        for case in new
        if case in old and classify(case, old.get(case), new[case], True) == "the desk changed"
    ]
```

**Line by line:**

- `case in old` excludes new cases. A case that is red on its first run is a failure and not a
  regression — part [1.1](../01-what-a-regression-is/1.1-a-regression-is-a-comparison.md)'s
  distinction, applied where it decides whether somebody is woken.
- `classify(...) == "the desk changed"` is the filter, and it is the **narrow** one on purpose. A judge
  flip is a difference and is not a regression of the desk; putting it in this list would page
  somebody about Day 81 part 1.2's coin.
- `True` for `judge_recorded`, because the fixture records both nights' judges. With that argument
  false the classifier returns `CANNOT TELL` and this list would be empty — which would be *wrong*,
  and is why part [1.3](../01-what-a-regression-is/1.3-what-has-to-be-written-down.md)'s fields are a
  prerequisite for this stage rather than a nicety.
- It imports `classify` from `compare.py` rather than reimplementing the rule. One definition of "a
  regression", used by the comparison and by the nightly, is what stops the two disagreeing at three
  in the morning.


**One sentence and one number in brackets.** `6 of 9 passed` is the state; `REGRESSED: one_lookup` is
the action; and `3 cases moved; 1 of them because the desk changed` is the line that stops somebody

Day 78's criterion 3 is why that sentence has to end there rather than in a plan. The eval traffic sits
on `gemini-2.5-flash-lite`, whose allowance is **unmeasured**, so the honest statement is: *this is
270 requests a night on a lane nobody has measured, and it is the fourth day in a row that the same
measurement has blocked the same decision.*

## When it breaks

The nightly breaks in a way Day 73 already anticipated and Day 78 already priced, and putting the eval
stage into it inherits both.

**It dies half-way.** Day 73's stages are resumable because each checks for the file the previous one
wrote. An eval stage that dies after 140 of 270 requests has spent them, and — Day 81 part 4.1's
finding — the cases that got partial samples have a majority computed from fewer opinions than
configured, with nothing recording that. A resumed run is not a re-run.

**It runs out of window.** Part [3.1](3.1-the-fast-half-on-every-commit.md)'s 429 arrives here too,
and here it arrives in the middle of the night with nobody watching:

```text
429 RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED
generativelanguage.googleapis.com/generate_content_free_tier_requests
Please retry in ~53s
```

Day 78 part 1.1's rule applies: that is a per-day quota, so the backoff ladder is a machine for turning
one refusal into six. The eval stage has to read the quota name and stop rather than retry.

**It runs at the wrong end of the window.** Day 78 part 2.3 measured the nightly at 00:30 local being
19:00 UTC — the *end* of the provider's day, on whatever the desk left over, with 10 of 12 requests
refused. An eval stage wanting 270 at that hour is asking for a window that has already been spent.

The smallest fix for the third is the one Day 78 part 2.4 argued for and is worth restating because
this stage changes the arithmetic: **write the priority order down.** With the eval stage in the
nightly, the schedule now has a 270-request job competing with a 12-request re-index and a 1-request
digest, and admitting in wake order gives the whole window to whichever wakes first.

## In production

**What a professional schedules.** The full suite once per window, at the **start** of the window
rather than at a convenient local hour, with a priority that puts the cheap stages first. Day 78 part
2.4 measured the alternative: fourteen of fourteen runs asking politely before spending, and the night
still not happening.

**What degrades at scale.** The nightly stops being nightly. A suite that takes twenty minutes at
twenty-four cases takes hours at six hundred — Day 81 part 4.1 — and it stops fitting between the last
commit and the morning. The first response is always to reduce `num_samples`, which Day 81 part 1.2
showed turns a majority into a single draw, and the second is to run the full suite weekly and a
subset nightly, which is the right answer and needs the subset to be chosen deliberately.

**The failure that only shows with real traffic.** The nightly and the daytime desk share an
allowance. Day 78 part 5.1's whole subject: there is no spare quota at night, and an eval stage that
wants 270 is taking them from the desk's window. On a free tier that is a job that stops running; on a
paid one it is a bill somebody notices in a month.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Put the eval stage in the nightly, at the start of
the provider's window rather than at half past midnight local, and give it a priority below the
re-index. And write down what it does on a 429 — it must stop, not retry, because that quota is per
day and the ladder will just spend the morning."*

**The interview question:** *"Why not run your full evaluation suite on every commit?"* The answer that
shows experience gives the arithmetic — cost per run times commits per day — and then names what
batching *costs*, which is turnaround: a regression found fourteen hours later has three more commits
on top of it.

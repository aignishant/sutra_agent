# 5.1 — What a regression report costs

## One-line answer

The comparison itself is free — it reads two files — so the whole cost of regression discipline is the
**second run**, which is 270 requests a night against a measured allowance of 20, and the only lever
that does not weaken the answer is running the suite less often.

## The story

Keeping the old newspaper.

The paper costs what it costs and you have already bought it. Keeping yesterday's costs nothing but a
shelf, and once a fortnight it earns its place: somebody wants the phone number from an advertisement,
or wants to check what the weather forecast actually said.

Nobody thinks of that as a cost, and it is not. What costs is buying the paper every day. The shelf is
free.

And when the household decides money is tight, the thing that gets cut is the subscription, not the
shelf — and the shelf immediately becomes worthless, because a shelf of one paper answers nothing.

## The idea in plain language

Regression discipline sounds like an extra thing to pay for. It is not, and being precise about where
the money is decides which lever to pull when somebody asks for the cost to come down.

**The comparison is free.** `compare.py` reads two JSON files and walks a dictionary. Zero requests, a
few milliseconds, at any suite size. Everything in sections 1 and 2 of this day costs nothing to run,
for ever.

**Storage is free enough not to argue about.** Part [2.1](../02-the-result-is-a-file/2.1-the-result-is-a-file.md)
measured the two fixture results at 8,234 and 9,249 bytes. One file a night is about three megabytes a
year, which is not a number anybody needs to plan around until the suite is hundreds of cases with
per-invocation results.

**The second run is the whole cost.** A comparison needs two runs, and the second one is 270 requests —
part [3.2](../03-two-cadences/3.2-the-full-run-rides-the-nightly.md)'s nightly. That is not a
regression cost; it is the cost of running the suite at all, and regression discipline is what makes it
worth paying twice.

So the arithmetic is short and it has a shape worth internalising: **the marginal cost of comparing is
zero, and the marginal cost of having something to compare against is the entire bill.**

That has one consequence that runs against instinct. When the eval budget is under pressure, cutting
*frequency* is the honest lever — run the full suite weekly and a subset nightly — because it reduces
the number of runs without weakening any of them. Cutting `num_samples` or dropping the swap control
reduces the same bill by the same fraction and makes every remaining run less interpretable, which Day
81 part 4.1 spelled out. Both save money; only one of them still leaves you with a comparison.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because Day 78's criterion 3 is now open for a fourth day. `gemini-2.5-flash-lite`'s daily allowance
is unmeasured, the nightly wants 270 requests on it, and the only allowance this repository has ever
observed is 20 a day on a different lane.

And because the pressure arrives in a predictable order. Part
[3.1](../03-two-cadences/3.1-the-fast-half-on-every-commit.md) already moved the judged checks off the
commit gate, which was the free saving. The next request will be to make the nightly cheaper, and this
part is what decides which of the two ways to do that.

## The mechanism

The cost, per cadence, with the multiplier written out. From `lab/cadence.py`:

```python
    commit_cost = sum(c.requests for c in per_commit)
    nightly_cost = sum(c.requests for c in nightly)
    daily = commit_cost * COMMITS_PER_DAY + nightly_cost
```

**Line by line:**

- Two sums and one multiplication. The whole cost model of regression discipline is three lines,
  because the comparison contributes nothing to either sum.
- `commit_cost * COMMITS_PER_DAY` — the per-commit half is multiplied and the nightly half is not,
  which is part [3.1](../03-two-cadences/3.1-the-fast-half-on-every-commit.md)'s finding as an
  equation.
- There is no term for storage, for the comparison, or for keeping history. Their absence is the
  measurement: they are not in the model because they are not in the bill.

And what a week's worth looks like against the measured allowance:

```bash
cd days/day-82-regression-discipline/lab
uv run python cadence.py | tail -6
```

**Line by line:**

- `tail -6` for the totals, which is where the cost conversation happens.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
  at 12 commits a day that is 270 requests
  a day's observed flash allowance: 20

  every commit is checked for nothing, and the bill lands once, at night
  the nightly still wants 270 requests against an allowance of 20
```

**270 a night.** Seven nights is 1,890 requests to have a week of history, and a week of history is
what part [2.3](../02-the-result-is-a-file/2.3-comparing-two-runs.md)'s **In production** section wants
for a median-of-five comparison.

The three ways to reduce that, and what each does to the answer:

| Lever | The bill | The comparison |
| --- | --- | --- |
| run the full suite weekly, a subset nightly | ÷7 on six nights | intact, over a smaller subset on those nights |
| `num_samples` 5 → 1 | ÷5 every night | every judged case becomes a single draw (Day 81 part 1.2) |
| drop the swap control on pairwise | ÷2 | 12 of 12 verdicts become the ordering (Day 81 part 2.1) |

**Only the first row leaves the remaining runs interpretable.** The other two buy the same saving by
making every night's number worth less, which means the comparison between two nights is a comparison
between two noisier numbers — and noise in a comparison is worse than noise in a single reading,
because a difference between two noisy numbers is noisier than either.

That last point is the one worth carrying into the budget meeting. Halving the reliability of a
measurement more than halves the reliability of a *difference* between two of them, so weakening the
controls hurts regression discipline more than it hurts a single run.

## When it breaks

The model breaks by leaving out the runs nobody counted, and there are three.

**Re-runs.** A flaky night gets re-run, which is another 270. Part
[4.2](../04-what-ci-must-refuse/4.2-flaky-by-construction.md) established that judged cases flip, so
re-runs are not rare, and a re-run is indistinguishable from a scheduled run in any per-night budget.

**Bisecting.** When something regresses, somebody wants to know which change did it — which means
running the suite against several intermediate states. Part
[papers/01](../../papers/01-which-change-broke-it.md)'s demo needed **34 runs** to isolate a pair of
interacting changes out of eight. At 270 requests each that is 9,180, on the day you are already
dealing with a regression.

**Retries under pressure.** Day 78 part 1.1's rule: a 429 on a per-day quota is answered by backoff,
and each retry is a request that will also be refused. A night that runs out of window can spend
noticeably more than 270 and complete fewer cases.

There is no error text for the first two; they are simply requests nobody budgeted. The third arrives
as Day 78's recorded refusal:

```text
429 RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED
generativelanguage.googleapis.com/generate_content_free_tier_requests
Please retry in ~53s
```

The smallest fix for the third is the branch Day 78 part 1.1 argued for — read the quota name and stop
rather than sleep. The smallest fix for the second is not a budget line: it is to make the suite
**cheap enough to bisect with**, which usually means a small designated subset that reproduces the
regression, chosen once the regression is known.

## In production

**What a professional budgets.** Scheduled runs plus a bisection allowance. The second is the one
nobody plans and it is the one you need on a bad day: a reserve of runs, in requests, available for
finding out which change caused something. Without it, the answer to *"which commit broke it?"* is
"we cannot afford to find out", which is a strange sentence to say about a system whose whole purpose
is telling you when things break.

**What degrades at scale.** Cost grows with cases and history grows with nights, and the two multiply
in the analysis rather than in the bill. Comparing against a median of the last five runs is free once
you have five; getting five is five nights of full cost, and a team that starts keeping history in
March cannot answer a question about February.

**The failure that only shows with real traffic.** A regression that needs a comparison you cannot
make, because the run you want to compare against was never taken. Weekly full runs mean six of every
seven nights have no full-suite baseline, so a regression introduced on a Wednesday is compared against
the previous Sunday — six days and forty commits ago — which puts you straight into the bisection
problem the paper part is about.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"The comparison is free and the runs are not, so
please stop proposing to cut `num_samples`. If we need this cheaper, we run the full suite twice a week
and a fifteen-case subset on the other nights — and we keep a reserve of runs for bisecting, because
the day we need that is the day we will not have it."*

**The interview question:** *"What does regression testing cost?"* The answer that shows experience
separates the comparison from the runs — the comparison is free, having a baseline is the whole
bill — and then names the cost nobody budgets, which is the extra runs you need on the day something
actually regresses.

```bash
cd days/day-82-regression-discipline/lab
uv run python cadence.py | tail -6
uv run python compare.py > /dev/null; echo "the comparison cost: $? requests worth of model calls"
```

The second command's exit code is not a request count — read it as the joke it is, then say what the
comparison actually cost and why that number is the same at nine cases and at nine hundred.

Now price a bisection. Part [papers/01](../../papers/01-which-change-broke-it.md)'s demo needed 34
runs; multiply by the nightly's cost and say how many days of the measured allowance that is. Then say
what you would do instead.

**Out loud, without scrolling up:** the comparison is free and the baseline is the bill. Which of the
three cost levers preserves the comparison, and why does weakening a run hurt a difference more than it
hurts a reading?

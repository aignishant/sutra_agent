# 3.2 — A critic needs something it can fail against

## One-line answer

Feedback that does not name a rule changes nothing — four different pieces of thoughtful criticism
moved **zero** rubric lines on the desk's fixture, and the one that named three rules moved all
three.

## The story

You are moving out of a rented flat and the landlord is coming to check it.

The first landlord you ever rented from stood in the middle of the room, looked around, and said
*"leave it nice."* You cleaned for an entire day. You scrubbed the oven, you did the skirting boards,
you bought a new bathmat. Then he came, walked to the window, ran a finger along the *outside* of the
window frame, and kept £80 of your deposit for the frames.

The second landlord handed you a sheet of paper when you moved in. Fourteen lines. *Oven interior.
Window frames, inside and out. Extractor filter. Behind the fridge. Bathroom sealant. Carpets
professionally cleaned, receipt required.*

You did not enjoy the sheet of paper. It was more work than "leave it nice", and it was very
specific about the fridge. But you got your whole deposit back, and — the part that matters — you
knew you were going to, before he arrived, because you had the same sheet he did.

*"Leave it nice"* is not a lower standard than the sheet. It is not a standard at all. It is a
feeling that will be checked later, by someone else, and you cannot act on it in advance.

## The idea in plain language

A **rubric** is a written standard: a list of named checks, each one something a specific piece of
work either satisfies or does not. In this day it is `RUBRIC` in `lab/_desk.py`, and it has five
lines.

The claim of this part is stronger than "rubrics are good practice". It is that **a critic without a
rubric cannot produce actionable feedback**, and the reason is mechanical rather than motivational.

Feedback is only useful if the writer can act on it. To act on feedback, the writer has to know
**which thing to change**. A note that names a rule — *"there is no next step"* — identifies exactly
what to change and what "fixed" will look like. A note that reports an impression — *"this could be
warmer"* — identifies nothing, so the writer's next draft is a guess, and a guess that happens to be
wrong is indistinguishable from ignoring the feedback.

Three properties a piece of feedback needs, and they follow from that one requirement:

1. **It names the rule that failed.** Not the feeling the reader had — the line of the standard that
   the artifact does not satisfy.
2. **It is checkable by someone else.** If two reviewers can disagree about whether the note applies,
   the writer cannot know when it has been addressed.
3. **It is falsifiable against the artifact.** There has to be something in the text that would make
   the note wrong. *"Could be warmer"* has no such thing, which is why it can be applied to every
   draft ever written — and a note that applies to every draft is the mechanism behind
   [4.1](../04-stopping/4.1-a-critic-will-always-find-something.md).

The rubric is also what makes the critic **cheap to be right about**. Three of the desk's five rules
are ordinary functions: a word count, a word list, a substring check. Those do not need a model at
all, which means part of the critic's job costs zero requests — and that is a design consequence, not
a footnote.

## Why Sutra needs it

Day 58's review stage has to return something the draft stage can act on, or the revision loop turns
into an expensive way of producing the same reply twice. The measurement below is exactly that
scenario.

The rubric is also the artefact that survives this day. Day 63 turns the desk's rules into an
approval policy — what needs a human and why — and a policy built on *"the reply should be
appropriate"* cannot be implemented, audited or argued with. A policy built on five named checks can
be all three.

And there is a quota argument. Every unactionable critique costs a full revision round: one writer
call, one critic call, and no change to the artifact.
[5.2](../05-does-it-help/5.2-what-the-pair-costs-in-requests.md) prices that at two requests, and
the free tier is measured in tens.

## The mechanism

The standard, as data. Not as a paragraph in an instruction block.

```python
@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Check:
    """One line of the house standard: a name, why it exists, and a test over the draft."""

    key: str
    why: str
    test: object  # Callable[[str], bool]; typed loosely so the dataclass stays printable


RUBRIC: tuple[Check, ...] = (
    Check("cause", "the customer asked why, and 'sorry' is not why", _mentions_cause),
    Check("fix", "a reply with no fix in it is an apology, not an answer", _mentions_fix),
    Check("next_step", "the customer must know what to do now", _has_next_step),
    Check("no_jargon", "the reader is a customer, not an engineer", _no_jargon),
    Check("short", "a long reply is not read", _short_enough),
)
```

**Line by line:**

- `key` is the identifier the verdict travels as. It is what makes feedback machine-actionable: a
  writer told `["next_step"]` knows precisely what to add, and a test can assert on it.
- `why` exists so the rule can be **argued with and deleted**. A standard whose lines have no stated
  reason accumulates forever, because nobody can tell which rules are still earning their place —
  the same append-only failure [1.2](../01-when-to-split/1.2-what-a-second-duty-costs-the-first.md)
  measured in instruction blocks.
- `test` is a plain function over the draft text. Three of the five need no model, which means the
  critic's cost is not five model calls but at most two.
- `frozen=True` because a standard that a running agent can edit is not a standard. This is the same
  argument [4.2](../04-stopping/4.2-the-brake-belongs-to-the-graph.md) makes about the brake, one
  layer down.
- It is a `tuple`, so the standard has an **order** and a **length**. Both are things you can put in
  a test and on a dashboard.

Now the measurement. Four pieces of genuine, well-meant criticism, and one verdict that names rules:

```bash
cd days/day-57-orchestrator-and-critic/lab
uv run python rubric.py
```

**Line by line:**

- The four notes in `TASTE` are real sentences reviewers write. None of them is stupid and none is
  lazy; they are simply about the reader's impression rather than about the text.
- For each note the script gives it to the writer as feedback, regenerates, and compares the rubric
  grade before and after. The comparison is the whole experiment: not "is the new draft nicer?" but
  "did any named rule change state?"
- The writer obeys feedback **literally** — it fixes exactly what it is told to fix. That is stated
  in `_desk.py` and it is the honest modelling choice here: a writer that guessed usefully from vague
  feedback would be a claim I have not measured.

Measured on 2026-09-05:

```text
critic with no written standard:
    'this could be warmer'       -> rubric lines changed: []
    'it does not quite land'     -> rubric lines changed: []
    'tighten it up a bit'        -> rubric lines changed: []
    'make it more helpful'       -> rubric lines changed: []

critic with the written standard:
    ['next_step', 'no_jargon', 'short'] -> rubric lines changed: ['next_step', 'no_jargon', 'short']

    before 2/5    after 5/5
```

Four rounds of criticism. Four revisions. **Zero rules changed state.** Eight model calls, if these
were model calls, and the reply that goes to the customer is the reply that was going to go to the
customer anyway.

Then one verdict that names three rules, and all three move. Two out of five becomes five out of five
in a single round.

Look at `'tighten it up a bit'` specifically, because it is the interesting one. It is *about* a real
failure — the draft is 94 words against a limit of 70. The reviewer noticed the right thing. And it
still moved nothing, because "tighten it up a bit" does not say **which** rule, does not say what
"tight enough" is, and gives the writer no way to know when it is done. The note was correct and
useless at the same time, and those are not in tension.

## When it breaks

The rubric itself can be the bug, in two ways.

**A rule that is not really checkable.** Add one and watch what happens to the verdict:

```bash
cd days/day-57-orchestrator-and-critic/lab
uv run python -c "
from _desk import TICKETS, Writer, failures
w = Writer(ticket=TICKETS[0]); d = w.draft()
told = failures(d) + ['sounds_human']
after = Writer(ticket=TICKETS[0]); after.calls = 1
print('asked for:', told)
print('still failing:', failures(after.draft(told=told)))
"
```

```text
asked for: ['next_step', 'no_jargon', 'short', 'sounds_human']
still failing: []
```

The three real rules were fixed. `sounds_human` was "addressed" too — the writer was told to fix it,
so it recorded that it did. Nothing checked it, because there is nothing to check it with. A rule with
no `test` is a rule that is always satisfied, and it will sit in the standard reporting success
forever. That is worse than not having it, because it occupies a line on a dashboard and reads as
coverage.

**A rubric that has drifted from what anyone cares about.** The rules are all green, the customers
are still unhappy, and the critic is dutifully enforcing a standard nobody has read since it was
written. There is no error text for this one at all — the system is working exactly as specified, and
the specification is the problem. The defence is the `why` column: a rule whose stated reason no
longer convinces anyone is a rule to delete, and a rubric that has never had a line deleted has
probably never been reviewed.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead.** The rubric lives in **version control as data**, not in a
prompt, and every check that can be a plain function is a plain function. The pattern that scales is
a two-tier standard: deterministic checks run first and cost nothing, and the model-based checks run
only on what survives — so a draft that is 94 words is rejected by a word count before any model is
asked its opinion. That ordering typically removes most revision rounds for free, and it makes the
expensive judgement the exception rather than the default.

**What degrades at scale.** Rubrics grow and never shrink, and a fifteen-line rubric produces
verdicts naming six failures at once, which is a revision request the writer cannot satisfy in one
pass — so the loop runs longer, costs more, and starts hitting the brake. The discipline that holds
is a cap on the standard's length, enforced by requiring that adding a rule means arguing to remove
one. The `why` column is what makes that argument possible.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic.** Deterministic checks are exact, which
means they are exactly wrong at the boundary. A 71-word reply is rejected for being too long, and a
70-word reply full of jargon passes the length check cleanly. On the fixture nothing sits at the
boundary; in production a steady trickle of perfectly good replies is bounced for one word, each
one costing a full revision round. This is where a tolerance band earns its place — reject at 85,
warn at 70 — and where you find out whether anyone is watching the *rejection reasons* or only the
pass rate.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"The critic prompt says 'evaluate whether the reply
is helpful and appropriate'. That will produce a paragraph, and the writer has nothing to do with a
paragraph — I measured four thoughtful notes against our rubric and not one of them changed a single
rule. Give it the five named checks and have it return the keys that failed. And move `short` and
`no_jargon` out of the model entirely; they are a word count and a word list."*

**The interview question:** *"How do you stop a critic agent from being useless?"* An honest answer is
about the output shape before it is about the prompt: *"Give it something it can fail against, and
make the verdict name which line failed. Without that you get impressions, and an impression is not
actionable — I ran four real reviewer notes like 'tighten it up a bit' through my revision loop and
they moved zero rules, while one verdict naming three rules took the draft from two out of five to
five out of five in one round. The note about tightening was even correct; the draft was 94 words
against a 70-word limit. It just did not say which rule or what done looks like. The other half of
the answer is that most rubric lines should not be model calls at all — a length limit is a word
count, so I run the deterministic checks first and only spend a request on the ones that need
judgement."*

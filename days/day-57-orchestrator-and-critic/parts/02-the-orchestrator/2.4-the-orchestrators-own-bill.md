# 2.4 — The orchestrator's own bill

## One-line answer

The orchestrator runs on every step of every request, so it is the component whose cost multiplies
fastest — and on a five-stage flow it is not one call per ticket, it is five, before any actual work
has been done.

## The story

A depot supervisor keeps a clipboard and walks the floor.

For years this is obviously worth it. Vans get loaded in the right order, the awkward delivery goes
to the person who knows that street, and when something is missing she knows within a minute. She
costs the depot one wage and she saves several.

Then the depot doubles. She now walks twice as far, checks twice as many vans, and her clipboard has
twice as many lines on it. She is still doing the same job and doing it well. But the drivers are now
waiting at the bay while she gets to them, and the queue at the bay is new — it did not exist last
year and nobody introduced it. It appeared because one person is on the path of every single van, and
that is a fine arrangement right up until it is not.

Nobody can point at the decision that caused the queue. There was no decision. There was a number
that got bigger.

## The idea in plain language

Day 24 introduced the currency this project runs on: not money, but **requests**, counted per
provider, per window. Every model call is a withdrawal from a fixed allowance that refills on a
schedule.

An orchestrator has an unusual position in that accounting, and it is worth stating precisely
because it is the thing people get wrong when they estimate cost. A specialist runs when its stage
runs — once per ticket. **The orchestrator runs once per stage.** So on a flow with `n` stages, the
orchestrator's own cost is `n` requests, and that is before a single ticket has been classified,
searched, drafted or reviewed.

Two consequences follow, and neither is obvious from a diagram:

- **The orchestrator is usually the largest single line in the bill**, even though it does none of
  the work. Five stages means the orchestrator alone is five of the eleven calls in a flow with five
  specialists and one revision round.
- **Its cost grows when you add a stage, not when you add traffic.** Adding a sixth stage adds two
  requests per ticket — the stage, and the orchestration decision in front of it. Teams budget for
  the first and forget the second.

A term for the rest of the day, because it is what the arithmetic is protecting: **headroom** is the
number of requests left in the current window. Day 70 builds a router that steers on it. Today's
concern is simply not to spend it on deciding things that were never in doubt.

## Why Sutra needs it

Day 58's graph has five stages and a revision loop. If every transition is an orchestration call, a
single ticket costs five decisions plus five stages plus the revision rounds — and the free tier this
project runs on is measured in tens of requests per minute.

The desk cannot afford an orchestration call it does not need, and the good news is that most of them
are not needed: the sequence intake → classify → research → draft → review is **known at design
time**. It does not change per ticket. Day 53's graph expresses that with edges, which cost nothing,
and reserves a decision for the two places where the flow genuinely branches.

This part is the arithmetic that makes that architectural choice on Day 58 an obvious one rather than
a stylistic one.

## The mechanism

Two costs, kept apart because they behave differently. First the **context** cost, which
[2.1](2.1-an-orchestrator-owns-the-flow-not-the-work.md) measured: what one orchestration turn
carries. Then the **request** cost, which is this part: how many turns there are.

```python
def routing_orchestrator() -> list[tuple[str, int, str]]:
    """Each turn the orchestrator sees the instruction, the ticket, and the last result only."""
    ticket = TICKETS[0]
    steps = [
        ("decide: research", tokens(ROUTING_INSTRUCTION + ticket.subject), "research"),
        ("decide: draft", tokens(ROUTING_INSTRUCTION + ticket.subject + ticket.cause), "draft"),
        ("decide: review", tokens(ROUTING_INSTRUCTION + ticket.subject + "draft ready"), "review"),
        ("decide: send", tokens(ROUTING_INSTRUCTION + ticket.subject + "accepted"), "send"),
    ]
    return steps
```

**Line by line:**

- There are **four entries in this list**, and that is the number this part is about. Each one is a
  separate model call, made before its stage runs, on a flow that has not yet produced anything a
  customer would see.
- Each entry rebuilds its context from the instruction, the ticket and one prior result. Nothing
  accumulates — that is the discipline from 2.1, and it keeps the per-turn size flat.
- Flat per-turn size is what makes the request count the dominant term. When context is bounded,
  cost is *number of calls*, and number of calls is a property of the graph's shape rather than of
  the ticket.

**Line by line:**

- The rows are the orchestration decisions only. The specialists' own calls are not in this table,

Measured on 2026-09-05:

```text
orchestrator that only routes:

    turn                 context tokens   emits
    decide: research           33         research
    decide: draft              50         draft
    decide: review             36         review
    decide: send               35         send

  context carried across the run: 154 tokens over 4 calls
  largest single call:            50 tokens
```

Four calls, 154 tokens. The tokens are trivial. **The four calls are not.**

Put them next to the work they are deciding about, for one ticket through a five-stage flow with a
single revision round:

| What runs | Calls |
| --- | --- |
| Orchestration decisions | 4 |
| Classify, research, draft, review | 4 |
| One revision round (writer + critic) | 2 |
| **Total** | **10** |

Forty per cent of the bill is the supervisor with the clipboard. She is not doing anything wrong, and
she is not carrying much — 154 tokens over the whole run. She is simply on the path of every step.

Now the arithmetic that decides Day 58's architecture. Most of those four decisions are not
decisions. Classify always follows intake. Draft always follows research. Only two transitions in the
triage flow genuinely branch: whether to research at all, and whether the critic's verdict sends the
draft back. So:

| Shape | Orchestration calls | Total for one ticket |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A model decides every transition | 4 | 10 |
| Edges for fixed steps, a decision at the two branches | 2 | 8 |
| Edges everywhere, verdict steers the loop directly | 0 | 6 |

Six instead of ten, for a flow that behaves identically. The third row is what
[6.2](../06-the-adk-box/6.2-the-verdict-steers-the-edges.md) builds: the critic's verdict is already
a decision, so routing on it directly costs nothing extra, and the "decide: review" call was asking a
model to look at an answer that was already in its hands.

Against the free-tier shape [5.2](../05-does-it-help/5.2-what-the-pair-costs-in-requests.md) uses —
250 requests in a day — that is 25 tickets against 41. The same graph, the same replies, the same
quality, and two thirds more of them.

## When it breaks

The break is a rate limit, and it arrives attached to the wrong culprit.

```text
google.genai.errors.ClientError: 429 RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED. {'error': {'code': 429,
'message': 'You exceeded your current quota, please check your plan and billing details.',
'status': 'RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED'}}
```

The traceback names whichever call happened to be the one over the line — usually a specialist,
because specialists are most of the calls by count. So the investigation starts at the drafter, and
the drafter is fine. The drafter runs once per ticket, exactly as designed.

The thing that changed is that somebody added a stage last week. One stage, one specialist, obviously
one extra call. It was two: the stage, and the orchestration decision in front of it. On a flow that
was already near the limit, that is the difference between working and not, and nothing in the
traceback points at it.

The other break costs nothing and is pure waste: an orchestration call on a transition that has one
possible outcome. You can see it in the fixture — three of the four decisions above have exactly one
legal successor. A model call whose output is determined is a request spent to compute a constant,
and it will still occasionally return something else, which is worse than useless because now you
also need to handle the invalid answer.

Every model call on this path handles 429 the way Addendum 02 requires — `retry-after` honoured,
backoff, then escalation rather than a fabricated route. An orchestrator that invents a next step
when the provider is exhausted has broken Principle 10 at the worst possible place, because
everything downstream will look like it worked.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead.** They put the fixed sequence in edges and spend model calls
only at real branches. The rule of thumb that survives contact with a rate limit: **if a transition
has one legal successor, it is an edge**. That is not an optimisation applied later — it is the
default, and an orchestration call is the exception that has to be justified by an actual choice.
Where the choice exists but is cheap to make, the next move is a smaller model for the decision, since
picking one of four words does not need the model that writes the reply.

**What degrades at scale.** The orchestrator is the serialisation point as well as the largest line
item. Its calls are sequential by construction — it cannot decide step three until step two returns —
so its latency is additive across the flow while the specialists' can sometimes be overlapped with
Day 54's fan-out. As stages are added over a year, both its cost and its latency grow linearly, and
neither growth is attributable to any single change.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic.** Orchestration cost is invisible on a
fixture of ten tickets and decisive on a working day. Worse, it interacts with retries: a stage that
fails and is retried usually costs an orchestration decision again, so the multiplier applies to the
recovery path too, which is exactly when you have least headroom. Day 54 measured this shape —
retries inside a loop multiply — and the orchestrator is the multiplier that everybody forgets to
count.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Three of these four transitions have one possible
next step. That is three model calls per ticket to compute a constant, and it is 40% of our request
budget. Make them edges, keep the decision at the two branches, and put the orchestration call count
on the dashboard next to the ticket count — I want to see them diverge the next time someone adds a
stage."*

**The interview question:** *"Where does the cost go in a multi-agent system?"* An honest answer
counts calls rather than tokens: *"Usually into the orchestrator, and people are surprised because it
does none of the work. A specialist runs once per ticket; the orchestrator runs once per step, so on
a five-stage flow it is five calls before anything useful has happened — on my own flow the
orchestration decisions were four of ten calls per ticket, at 154 tokens total, so it was 40% of the
requests and almost none of the tokens. That distinction matters on a free tier, because the request
limit is a hard wall and the token budget is a slope. The fix was that three of the four transitions
had exactly one legal successor, so they became graph edges with no model call, and the critic's
verdict routed the loop directly. Ten calls per ticket became six, which took the day's quota from 25
tickets to 41."*

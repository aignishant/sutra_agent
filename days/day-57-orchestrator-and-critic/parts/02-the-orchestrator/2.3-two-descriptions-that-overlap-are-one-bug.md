# 2.3 — Two descriptions that overlap are one bug

## One-line answer

An orchestrator chooses between specialists using their **descriptions** and nothing else, so two
descriptions that could both fit a request are not a documentation problem — on the desk's own ten
requests they route 4 correctly instead of 9, and every single one of the ten is a coin toss.

## The story

A government office has two counters, and above each one there is a sign.

The sign on the left says **Enquiries**. The sign on the right says **Information**.

You have a question about a form. You stand in the middle of the room holding it, reading both signs,
and there is genuinely nothing in either of them that tells you which queue is yours. So you pick
the shorter queue, because that is a real difference and the signs are not.

Half an hour later the clerk says the words everybody in that building has heard: *"That's the other
counter."*

Nobody wrote a bad sign. Both signs are accurate. **Enquiries** is a true description of that counter
and **Information** is a true description of the other one. The bug is not in either sign — it is in
the pair, and you cannot see it by reading them one at a time, which is exactly how they were
written and exactly how they were reviewed.

## The idea in plain language

A **description** is the short text attached to a specialist saying what it is for. Day 55 established
the mechanism: the orchestrator's choice is made from these descriptions, so a description is not
documentation — it is **the routing logic**, written in prose.

Once you accept that, a class of bug becomes visible that is otherwise invisible: the **overlapping
pair**. Each description is individually correct, individually reviewed, individually approved. The
defect exists only in the relationship between them, and there is no file you can open to see it.

Three symptoms, in increasing order of how hard they are to notice:

1. **The obvious wrong answer.** A request goes to the wrong specialist and comes back with nothing
   useful. Somebody notices.
2. **The plausible wrong answer.** A request goes to the wrong specialist and comes back with
   something that *reads* fine. The handbook answers a question about a past ticket by quoting a
   policy. Nobody notices.
3. **The tie.** Neither description matches better than the other, so the choice is made by something
   that has nothing to do with the request — the order they were declared in, the first name
   alphabetically, whatever the model happened to prefer. This one is the worst, because the system
   is not choosing badly. It is not choosing at all, and you cannot fix a decision that was never
   made by improving the inputs to it.

The measurement below counts the third one directly, and it is the number that changes how you write
descriptions.

## Why Sutra needs it

The desk has exactly this pair. Days 46 to 50 built the ticket history — a searchable archive of what
customers reported before — and Sutra also has the product handbook. Both answer questions. Both are
about the product. Both are things a support agent reaches for.

If the orchestrator cannot tell them apart, the failure is symptom 2: an agent asks *"has anyone
reported an empty export before?"*, the handbook is chosen, the handbook says what the export feature
does, and the reply that goes to the customer is a description of a feature rather than the fact that
four other people hit this on Tuesday. Nothing errored. Day 50's retrieval quality is irrelevant,
because the wrong index was searched.

Day 58 makes this sharper, because the triage graph has five stages and several of them could
plausibly be described as "understanding the ticket".

## The mechanism

Ten real requests the desk receives, each labelled with the specialist that should get it. Two
descriptions, written twice.

```python
# The two specialists, described the way a tired engineer describes them at five o'clock.
VAGUE = {
    "history": "answers questions about customer problems and support information",
    "handbook": "answers questions about product information and customer problems",
}

# The same two, described by what they *hold* rather than by what they are good at.
SHARP = {
    "history": (
        "past tickets other customers reported before; use when asked whether this happened "
        "another time, what we told the last customer, or to find a previous report"
    ),
    "handbook": (
        "the published product docs, guide and policy pages; use when asked what a page "
        "explains, what an error means, or where the documentation says something"
    ),
}
```

**Line by line:**

- Both `VAGUE` entries are true. Neither is lazy writing in the ordinary sense — they are the kind of
  sentence that gets written when you describe a component by its **category** rather than by its
  contents.
- The `VAGUE` pair shares almost every meaningful word: *answers, questions, customer, problems,
  information, product*. Reading either alone, nothing is wrong. Reading them together, there is
  nothing to choose between.
- `SHARP` changes two things. It names **what the specialist holds** — past tickets, published docs —
  and it lists the **shapes of question** that belong to it, in the words a requester would actually
  use. The second half is the part people leave out.
- Neither dictionary is longer in a way that would matter to a prompt budget; `SHARP` is more words
  but they are words that discriminate, which is the only property that counts here.

```python
def scores_for(request: str, descriptions: dict[str, str]) -> dict[str, int]:
    """How many meaningful words the request shares with each description."""
    words = {w for w in request.lower().split() if w not in STOP}
    return {
        name: len(words & {w for w in text.lower().replace(";", " ").split() if w not in STOP})
        for name, text in descriptions.items()
    }
```

**Line by line:**

- This is a deliberately crude stand-in for how an orchestrator chooses, and it is crude in the
  **same direction** as the real thing: the decision is made from the description text and nothing
  else. A model does this better, and it does it on the same input, which is why improving the input
  is the intervention that works.
- `STOP` removes words like *the*, *what*, *is* — words present in every description and therefore
  carrying no signal. Leaving them in would flatter the vague descriptions by giving them fake
  overlap.
- The function returns the **scores**, not the winner, because the interesting quantity is whether
  the scores differ at all. A winner-only function cannot report a tie, and the tie is the finding.

```bash
cd days/day-57-orchestrator-and-critic/lab
uv run python overlap.py
uv run python overlap.py --sharp
```

**Line by line:**

- The default arm uses `VAGUE`; `--sharp` swaps in `SHARP`. Nothing else changes — same ten requests,
  same scorer, same tie-break.
- The script marks a wrong route with `<-` and marks a tie with `(tie)`. Both columns are needed:
  the wrong routes tell you the system is failing, the ties tell you *why*.

Measured on 2026-09-05:

```text
descriptions: vague

    handbook  <- the customer was signed out during the form  (tie)
    handbook  <- has anyone reported an empty export before  (tie)
    handbook     what does error 4610 mean in our docs  (tie)
    handbook  <- find the ticket about duplicate invoices  (tie)
    handbook     what is our policy on refund windows  (tie)
    handbook  <- show me past reports of lost edits  (tie)
    handbook     which page explains the export limits  (tie)
    handbook  <- did this happen to another account last week  (tie)
    handbook     where is the guide for invoice addresses  (tie)
    handbook  <- what did we tell the last customer who asked  (tie)

  4/10 routed correctly, 6 wrong
  10/10 were ties, decided by neither description
```

Look at the second number before the first. **Ten out of ten were ties.** The system did not route
badly; it did not route. Every single request scored identically against both descriptions, and the
answer came from the tie-break — which here is alphabetical order, and in a real orchestrator is
whatever the model leans towards.

That also explains the 4 out of 10, and it is worth being precise, because the number is a trap.
Four requests were routed correctly, and not one of them was routed correctly *for a reason*. Four of
the ten happened to belong to `handbook`, and `handbook` sorts before `history`. Change the names and
the score changes. A 40% accuracy figure here is not a weak signal — it is noise that happens to have
a value.

Now the same ten requests with descriptions that discriminate:

```text
descriptions: sharp

    handbook  <- the customer was signed out during the form  (tie)
    history      has anyone reported an empty export before
    handbook     what does error 4610 mean in our docs
    history      find the ticket about duplicate invoices
    handbook     what is our policy on refund windows
    history      show me past reports of lost edits
    handbook     which page explains the export limits
    history      did this happen to another account last week
    handbook     where is the guide for invoice addresses
    history      what did we tell the last customer who asked

  9/10 routed correctly, 1 wrong
  1/10 were ties, decided by neither description
```

Nine out of ten, and — the number that actually matters — **one tie instead of ten**. The remaining
failure is the remaining tie. *"The customer was signed out during the form"* shares no distinguishing
word with either description, because it is a statement of a problem rather than a request for a kind
of information, and neither description claims statements of problems.

That is a useful failure rather than a mysterious one: it names the missing capability. The fix is
not to make the two descriptions better but to notice that this request belongs to neither, which is
what [4.3](../04-stopping/4.3-the-third-verdict.md) is about.

## When it breaks

The break worth reaching for on purpose is the one that shows the tie-break is arbitrary. Rename the
specialists and route the same ten requests again:

```bash
cd days/day-57-orchestrator-and-critic/lab
uv run python -c "
import overlap
renamed = {'archive': overlap.VAGUE['history'], 'zbook': overlap.VAGUE['handbook']}
truth = {'history': 'archive', 'handbook': 'zbook'}
wrong = sum(overlap.choose(r, renamed) != truth[c] for r, c in overlap.REQUESTS)
print('vague, renamed:', len(overlap.REQUESTS) - wrong, 'of', len(overlap.REQUESTS), 'correct')
"
```

```text
vague, renamed: 6 of 10 correct
```

The same descriptions, the same requests, the same scorer — and accuracy moved from 4 to 6 because
two components were renamed. Nothing about the system's understanding changed. If a rename can move
your routing accuracy by twenty points, the number was never measuring routing.

The second break has no error text and is the one that reaches customers. It is symptom 2 from the
list above: the handbook confidently answers a history question. There is no exception, the retrieval
succeeded, the reply is fluent, and the only way to detect it is to check *which specialist ran*
against *which should have* — which requires the labelled fixture that `overlap.py` has and most
production systems do not.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead.** Three rules, all of which come straight out of the numbers
above. A description names **what the specialist holds**, not what it is good at — "past tickets
customers filed" rather than "customer support information". It includes **example requests** in the
requester's own words, because that is the vocabulary the routing decision is actually made against.
And critically, the descriptions are written **as a set and reviewed as a set**: the review question
is never "is this description accurate?" but "could this request match two of these?"

**What degrades at scale.** Overlap grows quadratically with the number of specialists, because the
thing that can collide is a *pair*. Two specialists have one pair to check; six have fifteen. Nobody
notices this happening, because each specialist is added by a different person on a different day,
and every one of them reads only their own description. This is the concrete reason
[8.2](../08-in-production/8.2-how-many-heads-is-too-many.md) argues for a small number of
specialists rather than a tidy one per noun.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic.** Your test requests are the ones you wrote,
and you wrote them while thinking about the distinction, so they contain the distinguishing words.
Real requests are written by people who do not know the specialists exist, and they use neither
vocabulary. That is the tie in the sharp run — a customer describing a symptom, matching nothing. In
production the tie rate on real traffic is the metric to watch, and it is always higher than on the
fixture.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Both of these say 'answers questions about the
product'. Ten out of ten of our sample requests tie between them, so the routing is being decided by
declaration order. Rewrite both to name what they hold and give three example questions each, in the
words agents actually type — and add the tie count to the dashboard, because accuracy alone will look
fine while the decision is a coin toss."*

**The interview question:** *"Your orchestrator keeps picking the wrong sub-agent. How do you debug
it?"* An honest answer starts in an unexpected place: *"I would not start with the orchestrator's
prompt. I would put the sub-agent descriptions side by side and check whether any request could match
two of them, because the description is the routing logic and each one is usually written and
reviewed alone. Then I would measure ties, not just accuracy — on my own set, ten out of ten requests
tied between two vague descriptions, so 40% accuracy was really a coin toss that happened to land
four times, and I proved it by renaming the components and watching accuracy move to 60% with nothing
else changed. Rewriting the descriptions to name what each one holds, with example questions in the
requester's vocabulary, took it to 9 of 10 with one tie left — and that last tie was a request that
genuinely belonged to neither, which is a different fix."*

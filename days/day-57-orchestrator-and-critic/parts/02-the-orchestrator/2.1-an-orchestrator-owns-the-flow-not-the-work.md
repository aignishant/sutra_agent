# 2.1 — An orchestrator owns the flow, not the work

## One-line answer

An orchestrator's whole job is deciding **what happens next**, and the moment it also does some of
the work it starts carrying everybody's context — which on the desk's own fixture is 781 tokens
across a run instead of 154.

## The story

Stand at the pass in a busy kitchen and watch the person calling the orders.

They are not cooking. Their hands are empty. They read the docket, they say "two of the fish, one
without the sauce", and one of the cooks says "yes". A little later they touch the plate, look at it,
and either it goes out or it goes back. That is the entire job, and it is done standing still.

Now imagine the same person deciding to also make the sauces, because the sauce station is behind and
they know how. For a while it works. Then a docket goes unread, because reading dockets is something
you do with your eyes and their eyes are in a pan. Nothing has gone wrong with the sauce. What has
gone wrong is that the person whose job was to know what every station is doing is now looking at one
station.

The kitchen does not fail loudly. Orders simply start going out in the wrong order, and nobody can
say why, because the person who would have noticed is the person who is busy.

## The idea in plain language

An **orchestrator** is the agent that decides which other agent runs next. That is a complete
description of its job, and the word "only" belongs in front of it.

Three terms it is easy to run together, so here they are separated once:

- **Deciding** — choosing the next step. This is the orchestrator's work.
- **Doing** — producing the classification, the search, the draft, the verdict. This is a
  specialist's work.
- **Carrying** — holding the context that the doing needs. This is the expensive one, and it follows
  the doing around.

The reason the separation matters is not tidiness. It is that **carrying is what costs**, and an
orchestrator that does not do any work does not have to carry very much. It needs the goal, the
current state, and the last result. It does not need the ticket archive, the retrieved rows, the
draft, the rubric or the revision history — those belong to the agents whose job they are.

Day 19 established the arithmetic behind that: everything in the context is paid for on **every**
call, not once. So an orchestrator that accumulates the whole run's material is not paying a little
extra at the end; it is paying more on each of its turns, and its turns are the ones that happen most
often.

There is a second reason, and it shows up later rather than immediately. An orchestrator that only
decides is **replaceable by ordinary code** in the many cases where the decision is not actually
hard. Day 53's graph is exactly that: an orchestration made of edges, with no model call in it at
all. If your orchestrator is doing work, that swap is no longer available, and you have permanently
bought a model call for something a conditional edge could have done.

## Why Sutra needs it

Day 58 assembles intake → classify → research → draft → review. Something has to decide that research
is skipped when the classification is confident, and that the reply goes back to the writer when the
critic says revise.

If that something is an agent that also drafts, then by the last stage it is carrying the ticket, the
retrieved rows, its own draft and the critic's verdict — on every subsequent decision. The measured
version of that is below, and it is the difference between a graph that runs inside a free tier and
one that does not.

It also decides how much of Day 58 is a model at all. This part's claim — that the orchestrator only
decides — is what makes it possible for most of the triage graph's control flow to be **edges**
rather than agents, which is the cheapest architecture available and the one Day 53 already built.

## The mechanism

Two arms of the same run: an orchestrator that only routes, and one that also does the work. Both
make four decisions about the same ticket.

```python
# What each turn actually has to hold, for the two shapes.
ROUTING_INSTRUCTION = (
    "You choose which specialist runs next. Reply with one of: research, draft, review, send."
)

WORKER_INSTRUCTION = (
    "You are the support desk. Read the ticket, look up similar tickets, write the reply, "
    "check it against the house standard, and send it."
)
```

**Line by line:**

- `ROUTING_INSTRUCTION` names an output alphabet — four words — and nothing about how the work is
  done. An instruction that can be satisfied by one of four tokens is a small instruction, and it
  stays small as the system grows, because adding a fifth specialist adds a word.
- `WORKER_INSTRUCTION` describes the whole pipeline in one breath. It is the single-agent shape from
  [1.1](../01-when-to-split/1.1-one-agent-is-usually-the-right-answer.md), written out so the two can
  be measured against each other rather than compared as styles.
- Neither is sent anywhere. `owns.py` counts what a turn would carry; nothing on this day calls a
  model.

```python
def working_orchestrator() -> list[tuple[str, int, str]]:
    """One head does the work, so every turn carries everything that has happened so far."""
    ticket = TICKETS[0]
    writer = Writer(ticket=ticket)
    draft = writer.draft()
    carried = WORKER_INSTRUCTION + ticket.subject
    steps = []
    for label, addition in (
        ("research", ticket.cause),
        ("draft", draft),
        ("review", str(failures(draft))),
        ("send", "final reply " + draft),
    ):
        carried += addition
        steps.append((label, tokens(carried), "-"))
    return steps
```

**Line by line:**

- `carried += addition` is the whole point of the function and the whole failure of the shape. The
  string is never trimmed, because there is nothing in a single-agent flow that would trim it — every
  result is potentially relevant to the next step, so it all stays.
- The routing version, by contrast, rebuilds its context from the instruction, the ticket and the
  *last* result on every turn. It never accumulates, because it never needs to: the material lives
  with the specialist that produced it.
- `tokens()` is the four-characters-per-token estimate this repo has used since Day 24. It gives an
  order of magnitude, not a bill, and the day says so rather than implying precision it does not
  have.
- `emits` is `-` for every row here. That is not a formatting detail: an orchestrator that does the
  work emits *content*, so there is nothing clean for the next stage to route on, which is
  [3.4](../03-the-critic/3.4-the-verdict-is-a-list-not-a-paragraph.md)'s problem arriving early.

Run both arms:

```bash
cd days/day-57-orchestrator-and-critic/lab
uv run python owns.py
uv run python owns.py --works
```

**Line by line:**

- The default arm is the routing orchestrator; `--works` is the same four decisions made by an agent
  that is also producing the output.
- Both print per-turn context and then two totals. The per-turn column is the one to read first,
  because the totals hide the shape.

Measured on 2026-09-05:

```text
orchestrator that only routes:

    turn                 context tokens   emits
    decide: research           33         research
    decide: draft              50         draft
    decide: review             36         review
    decide: send               35         send

  context carried across the run: 154 tokens over 4 calls
  largest single call:            50 tokens
```

```text
orchestrator that does the work:

    turn                 context tokens   emits
    research                   61         -
    draft                     190         -
    review                    199         -
    send                      331         -

  context carried across the run: 781 tokens over 4 calls
  largest single call:            331 tokens
```

Read the **columns**, not the totals. The routing orchestrator's per-turn cost goes 33, 50, 36, 35 —
it goes up and comes back down, because each turn carries the last result and then lets it go. The
working orchestrator's goes 61, 190, 199, 331, and it only ever goes one way.

That difference is not five times. It is a **constant** against a **line that grows with the number
of steps**, which means the gap on Day 58's five-stage graph is larger than the gap here, and the gap
on a twelve-stage graph is larger again. A ratio measured on a short run understates a shape.

The other half of the finding is in the `emits` column. The routing orchestrator's output is one word
out of four. That word can steer a graph edge directly — which is
[6.2](../06-the-adk-box/6.2-the-verdict-steers-the-edges.md) — and it can be checked, logged and
tested. The working orchestrator emits prose, and prose has to be parsed before anything can act on
it.

## When it breaks

The way this goes wrong is not a crash. It is an orchestrator that is *almost* pure.

Somebody gives it one small extra duty: "and summarise what the specialist said before passing it
on". It is a reasonable request — the summary makes the next agent's context smaller. But the
orchestrator now has to hold the specialist's full output in order to summarise it, so the thing that
was meant to reduce context has moved the whole context into the one agent that runs on every turn.

You can produce the effect directly. Add one accumulating step to the routing arm:

```bash
cd days/day-57-orchestrator-and-critic/lab
uv run python -c "
import owns
base = owns.routing_orchestrator()
print('pure routing, largest turn:', max(size for _, size, _ in base))
print('with summarising,           ', max(size for _, size, _ in owns.working_orchestrator()))
"
```

```text
pure routing, largest turn: 50
with summarising,            331
```

One duty, added for a good reason, and the largest turn is six times what it was. There is no error
message, no exception, and nothing in the logs says anything except that the calls got bigger. The
first visible symptom is a rate limit, and by then the change that caused it was three weeks ago.

The second break is the one where the orchestrator's decision is not actually a decision. If the flow
is always intake → classify → research → draft → review with no branching, then an orchestrating
**agent** is a model call spent to produce a constant. Day 53's graph does that for free. Before you
build an orchestrator, check that it has something to decide.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead.** They make the orchestrator's output an **enumeration**, not a
sentence — one of a fixed, small set of names — and they validate it against that set before acting.
That single decision buys three things at once: the turn stays small, the output can be routed
without parsing, and an invalid answer is caught at the boundary rather than turning into a mysterious
no-op. In ADK's node graph the enumeration is the route, and
[6.2](../06-the-adk-box/6.2-the-verdict-steers-the-edges.md) shows it steering edges directly.

**What degrades at scale.** The orchestrator is on the critical path of every request, so it is the
first thing to hit a rate limit and the last thing you can afford to make slower. Teams discover this
when they add "one more consideration" to its instruction for the fifth time and the whole system's
latency moves. The discipline that holds is a hard rule that the orchestrator's context is *bounded*:
goal, state, last result. If a decision genuinely needs more than that, the decision belongs to a
specialist that returns an answer, not to the orchestrator.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic.** With four stages and a short ticket, a
working orchestrator is fine — the numbers above are small in both columns. The failure arrives with
the ticket that has a long thread on it, where the accumulating context crosses whatever limit you
are near, and it arrives *only* on those tickets. So the system works, and then does not work for
your most involved customers, which is the population you least want it to fail for. Day 50 met the
same shape in retrieval: the average improves while the hard cases quietly stop working.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"The orchestrator is summarising the research
output. That means it holds the research output, on every turn after the research step. Either move
the summarising into the research agent — it already has the material — or drop it. Right now our
cheapest, most frequent call is also our largest."*

**The interview question:** *"What does your orchestrator actually do?"* An honest answer is
suspiciously short: *"It picks the next step and nothing else. It sees the goal, the current state
and the last result, and it returns one of a fixed set of names. I keep it that way because it runs
on every turn, so it is the call whose size matters most — when I measured the alternative on my own
flow, a routing orchestrator's turns stayed flat around 33 to 50 tokens while one that also did the
work went 61, 190, 199, 331 and only ever climbed. And keeping it pure means most of it can be
replaced by graph edges with no model call at all, which is what I did for the parts of the flow that
never branch."*

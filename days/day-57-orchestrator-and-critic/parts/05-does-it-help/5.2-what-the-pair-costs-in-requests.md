# 5.2 — What the pair costs in requests

## One-line answer

On this project quality is bought with requests, and the reviewed reply costs four of them against
one — so a day's free-tier allowance is 250 unreviewed replies or 62 reviewed ones, and that trade
is the decision, not a footnote to it.

## The story

A shop takes on a second person for the counter and pays her by the hour.

The owner is happy with the arrangement and, if asked, would say she pays for herself. But the shop's
takings are what they are, and the hours are a real line in the book. On a slow week the two of them
are standing there and the wage is still going out.

What changes the conversation is not the wage. It is the moment someone works out that the money for
her hours is the same money that would have paid for the delivery van's insurance, and the van is off
the road in March either way. Then the question stops being *is she worth it?* — which nobody can
answer — and becomes *is she worth more than the van?*, which is a question with an answer.

A cost is only a decision once you know what else it buys.

## The idea in plain language

Day 24 established the currency: on this project the constraint is **requests**, counted per
provider, per window — requests per minute and requests per day. Not money. The provider does not
send a bill; it sends a 429 (see [4.1's](../04-stopping/4.1-a-critic-will-always-find-something.md)
context and the handling rules in Addendum 02).

That changes the shape of a cost argument in a way worth stating explicitly. A token budget is a
**slope** — spend more and things get gradually more expensive. A request limit is a **wall** — you
get exactly so many and then you get none, and the difference between 249 and 251 requests in a day
is the difference between working and not working.

So the pair's cost has to be counted in calls, and the three numbers that matter are:

- **Calls per reply, measured**, on real tickets rather than assumed from the diagram.
- **Calls per reply, worst case**, which is what the brake permits and therefore what you must be
  able to survive.
- **Replies per window**, which is the number that decides whether the desk can run at all.

[5.1](5.1-did-the-critic-actually-help.md) supplied the benefit — 10 rubric lines out of 25 becoming
24 out of 25. This part supplies the price, and the two together are the decision.

## Why Sutra needs it

Day 58 wires the pair into the triage graph, and Day 59's phase gate asks whether the whole thing
runs inside the free tier. That gate cannot be answered from a diagram; it needs replies-per-day for
the shape actually being shipped.

There is also an architectural consequence that lands on Day 58 directly. If the reviewed reply costs
four requests and the unreviewed one costs one, then reviewing **every** reply is a choice to serve a
quarter as many tickets. That may be right. It may also be that most tickets are simple enough that
the deterministic half of the rubric catches everything, and the model critic should run only on the
rest — which is a design the numbers below make visible and a diagram never would.

## The paper behind it

> **New directions in communications (or which way to the information age?)**
> `doi:10.1109/MCOM.1986.1092946` · 1986 · <https://doi.org/10.1109/MCOM.1986.1092946>

It introduced the leaky bucket: a fixed allowance drained at a steady rate, so a sender's long-run
rate is capped however it bursts — which is why a per-minute limit and a per-day limit are two
separate walls and satisfying one says nothing about the other.

## The mechanism

The cost is counted from a run rather than reasoned about, and then divided into the allowance.

```python
# Free-tier shape, supplied by the learner from the provider's own page on the day they run this.
# No number here is a claim about any provider's current limits - Addendum 02 says look them up
# live, and the hub's section 8 names the page to look at.
RPM = 10
RPD = 250


def calls_for_one_reply(max_rounds: int) -> int:
    """Run the pair on one ticket and count what it actually spent."""
    writer = Writer(ticket=TICKETS[0])
    critic = Critic()
    draft = writer.draft()
    rounds = 1
    while rounds <= max_rounds:
        verdict, found = critic.review(draft)
        if verdict == "accept":
            break
        draft = writer.draft(told=found)
        rounds += 1
    return writer.calls + critic.calls
```

**Line by line:**

- `RPM` and `RPD` are **placeholders and are labelled as such**, in the file and here. Addendum 02
  forbids inventing a provider's limits, and free rosters move; the hub's §8 names the page to read
  and the learner substitutes their own numbers. What the day teaches is the arithmetic, which does
  not depend on the particular allowance.
- `calls_for_one_reply` **runs the loop** rather than computing `1 + 2 * rounds`. The measured number
  and the formula disagree whenever the critic accepts early, and the measured one is the one that
  happens.
- `writer.calls + critic.calls` counts both agents. A common estimate counts only the "extra" critic
  call and misses that each revision round also costs a **writer** call — which is half the cost and
  the half people forget.
- The `break` on accept is why the measured figure is lower than the worst case: most tickets do not
  use all their rounds, so budgeting from the worst case over-provisions and budgeting from the mean
  under-provisions. Both numbers are needed.

**Line by line:**

- The table has three rows because there are three questions: what does one agent cost, what does the
  pair cost in practice, and what does the brake allow it to cost at most.
- `replies/minute` and `replies/day` are integer divisions of the allowance by the per-reply cost.
  Integer division is correct here — a reply that cannot be completed within the window is not a
  fraction of a reply, it is a 429.

Measured on 2026-09-05:

```text
free tier as configured: 10 requests/minute, 250 requests/day

    shape                       calls/reply   replies/minute   replies/day
    one agent                       1              10             250
    pair, measured                  4              2              62
    pair, worst case                7              1              35

  the pair costs 4x the requests of one agent on this ticket,
  and 7x if every round is used.
  a day's quota buys 250 solo replies or 62 reviewed ones.
```

**250 against 62.** That is the trade in the only unit that matters here, and it is a large trade —
not a 20% overhead but a factor of four.

Now put it beside [5.1](5.1-did-the-critic-actually-help.md)'s benefit and the decision becomes
statable:

| | Unreviewed | Reviewed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rubric lines passing | 10/25 | 24/25 |
| Replies per day | 250 | 62 |

Neither column is obviously right, and that is the honest position. A desk with 40 tickets a day
should review everything. A desk with 200 cannot, and has to choose: review a subset, run the
deterministic checks on everything and the model critic on what fails them, or accept a lower
standard on the tail.

The `replies/minute` column is the one that bites first and gets noticed last. **Two reviewed replies
a minute** is not a throughput problem in a lab — it is a throughput problem the first time two
agents use the desk at once, and it shows up as a 429 rather than as a queue.

The worst-case row is not decoration either. The brake permits seven calls, so seven is what you must
survive on the tickets that use their rounds — and [5.1](5.1-did-the-critic-actually-help.md) showed
`ticket:5044` spending exactly that. Budget from the mean and the hard tickets are the ones that fail.

## When it breaks

The break is the 429, and its timing is the cruel part: it lands on the ticket in progress, not on
the one that overspent.

```text
google.genai.errors.ClientError: 429 RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED. {'error': {'code': 429,
'message': 'You exceeded your current quota, please check your plan and billing details.',
'status': 'RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED'}}
```

What makes this expensive rather than merely annoying is *where* in the loop it arrives. A pair that
is exhausted mid-revision has already spent the writer call for that round. Fail there and the round
is lost entirely; the retry starts it again, and the retry costs the same two calls. So the cost of
running near the limit is not linear — it is the wasted partial rounds on top of the real ones.

You can see how quickly the multiplication runs away:

```bash
cd days/day-57-orchestrator-and-critic/lab
uv run python -c "
import price
for rounds in (1, 2, 3):
    calls = 1 + 2 * rounds
    print(f'{rounds} round(s): {calls} calls -> {250 // calls} replies/day, {10 // calls} per minute')
"
```

```text
1 round(s): 3 calls -> 83 replies/day, 3 per minute
2 round(s): 5 calls -> 50 replies/day, 2 per minute
3 round(s): 7 calls -> 35 replies/day, 1 per minute
```

Every additional permitted round removes roughly a third of the day's capacity. Raising the brake
from two rounds to three — which sounds like a small quality improvement and is the natural response
to seeing an escalation — costs 15 replies a day. That is the trade
[4.3](../04-stopping/4.3-the-third-verdict.md) warned about, priced.

The per-minute column falls faster than the per-day one: 3, 2, 1. It is an integer division of a
small allowance, so each extra round does not shave a percentage off the rate, it removes a whole
reply from every minute. At three rounds the desk can complete **one** reviewed reply a minute, and
the second agent who opens a ticket in that minute waits.

Every call in the pair handles 429 as Addendum 02 requires: honour `retry-after`, back off, and then
**escalate rather than proceed**. A revision loop that swallows a 429 and treats the missing verdict
as an accept has converted a quota problem into a quality problem, which is Principle 10's failure
and the hardest kind to find later.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead.** They run the standard in **two tiers**. The deterministic
rules cost nothing — `short` is a word count and `no_jargon` is a word list — so those run on every
draft, and the model critic runs only on drafts that pass them and still need judgement. On the
desk's fixture that removes most first rounds outright, because the first draft usually fails on
length and jargon, which no model was needed to notice. The reviewed-reply cost then falls between
the two rows in the table instead of sitting at the top of it.

**What degrades at scale.** Request cost is per **reply**, and replies grow with traffic while the
allowance does not. A system comfortable at 40 tickets a day is broken at 300, and nothing in the code
changed. Worse, the failure is not gradual: you are inside the wall until you are not. The number to
watch is not usage but **headroom** — the requests remaining in the current window — which is what
Day 70 builds a router on top of.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic.** Request budgets are computed per reply and
spent per **burst**. Tickets do not arrive evenly; they arrive when a release goes out, in a clump.
The per-day number can be comfortable while the per-minute number is exceeded several times a day at
exactly the moments the desk is most needed. This is the leaky bucket's whole point: the long-run
rate is not the constraint people hit.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Four requests per reply, measured, and seven if the
brake is reached. At our current allowance that is 62 reviewed replies a day against 250 unreviewed,
and we handle more than 62. Before we ship this, run the deterministic rules first — length and
jargon are a word count and a word list — and only spend the critic on what survives. And put
headroom on the dashboard, not usage."*

**The interview question:** *"What does a Writer↔Critic loop cost you?"* An honest answer is in the
right unit: *"On a free tier, requests, and it is a multiple rather than an overhead. I measured four
calls per reviewed reply against one for unreviewed, and seven when the round cap is reached, which
took a day's allowance from 250 replies to 62. That is the actual decision — the pair took my rubric
from 10 of 25 lines to 24 of 25, and it cost three quarters of my throughput. What I would not do is
budget from the mean: the hard tickets are the ones that use all their rounds, so they cost seven,
and they are also the ones that fail if you provision for four. The mitigation that worked was
running the deterministic rules first, because a length limit does not need a model, so the model
critic only sees the drafts where judgement is actually required."*

# 5.1 — Did the critic actually help?

## One-line answer

The only honest defence of a second agent is a before-and-after number on the same work: the desk's
pair took 10 rubric lines out of 25 to 24 out of 25 for 23 model calls, and the same loop with a
critic that approves everything moved nothing while still spending 10.

## The story

A workshop starts doing a second check on every car before it goes back to the customer.

The owner is asked whether it was worth it, and he says yes, obviously — cars go out better now,
everyone can feel it. The mechanics agree. The second check takes a while and it is clearly the right
thing to do.

Then someone asks a duller question: *how many cars came back last month, and how many came back the
month before?*

Nobody knows. There is a book by the phone where returns get written down, and it has been kept
patchily, and the two months are not comparable anyway because one of them had the holiday in it.

The second check may well be worth it. That is not the point. The point is that after a year of doing
it, the shop cannot say — and if it turned out to be worthless they would have no way of finding out,
because nothing was ever measured against anything.

## The idea in plain language

Two terms, and the distance between them is what this part is about.

An **output metric** counts what the system did: replies produced, rounds run, verdicts issued,
tickets closed. It goes up when the system is busy.

An **outcome metric** counts whether the work was any good: how many rubric lines the reply
satisfies. It goes up only when the replies improve.

A critic is a cost. It is a second agent, a second instruction block, a revision loop, and — as
[5.2](5.2-what-the-pair-costs-in-requests.md) prices — several times the requests. The only thing
that justifies it is an outcome metric that moved, and the measurement has a specific shape:

**Run the same work twice.** Once without the critic, once with. Score both with something neither
of them controls. Report the difference and the calls it cost.

Two details make it honest rather than decorative:

- **The scorer is not the critic.** If the critic's own verdict is the metric, you have measured
  whether the critic agrees with itself, and a sycophantic critic scores perfectly. The scorer here
  is `grade()`, the same five functions for both arms, run by neither agent.
- **The cost is reported next to the gain.** A gain with no cost beside it cannot be argued with, and
  the argument is the point — somebody has to be able to say *that is too expensive*.

## Why Sutra needs it

This day has spent four sections arguing for a second head. This is where the argument is tested, and
it has to be possible for it to fail — that is Principle 11, and a day that only measured its own
success would be [3.1](../03-the-critic/3.1-a-check-you-never-ran-cannot-fail.md)'s failure in
document form.

There is a direct practical stake too. Day 58 wires the reviewer into the triage graph, and Day 59's
phase gate has to answer whether the graph earns its quota. Both need this number and neither can
manufacture it later, because "without a critic" stops being observable the moment the critic is the
only path.

## The mechanism

The same five tickets, twice, scored by the standard.

```python
def run_one(ticket, mode: str) -> tuple[int, int, int]:
    """Return (lines passing without a critic, lines passing with one, model calls spent)."""
    solo = Writer(ticket=ticket)
    alone = sum(grade(solo.draft()).values())

    writer = Writer(ticket=ticket)
    critic = Critic(mode=mode)
    draft = writer.draft()
    rounds = 1
    while rounds <= MAX_ROUNDS:
        verdict, found = critic.review(draft)
        if verdict == "accept":
            break
        draft = writer.draft(told=found)
        rounds += 1

    return alone, sum(grade(draft).values()), writer.calls + critic.calls
```

**Line by line:**

- Two **fresh** `Writer` objects. The solo arm cannot be the first iteration of the paired arm,
  because the writer carries a pass counter and reusing it would give the paired arm a head start it
  did not earn.
- `sum(grade(...).values())` counts rubric lines passing, using the same `grade` for both arms. This
  is the scorer, and it belongs to neither agent — the critic can be swapped for a broken one and the
  scoring does not change.
- `writer.calls + critic.calls` is the cost, counted rather than estimated. Both objects increment on
  every call, including the paired arm's extra drafts.
- `mode` is a parameter so the same harness measures a working critic and a broken one. A measurement
  that can only be run against the good case is not a measurement.
- `MAX_ROUNDS` bounds the loop from outside it, per
  [4.2](../04-stopping/4.2-the-brake-belongs-to-the-graph.md).

**Line by line:**

- One row per ticket, then the totals. The per-ticket rows matter because the average hides the
  ticket where the loop struggled, and that ticket is the interesting one.
- `calls` is per ticket, so the cost of a hard ticket is visible rather than smeared across the mean.

Measured on 2026-09-05:

```text
critic mode: honest   (each reply is scored out of 5)

    ticket        alone   with critic   calls
    ticket:4610   2/5     5/5         4
    ticket:4702   2/5     5/5         4
    ticket:4811   2/5     5/5         4
    ticket:5044   2/5     4/5         7
    ticket:4903   2/5     5/5         4

  without a critic: 10/25 rubric lines pass
  with a critic:    24/25 rubric lines pass
  bought with:      23 model calls for 5 replies
  1.6 model calls per rubric line gained
```

**10 out of 25 becomes 24 out of 25.** That is the number that justifies the whole day, and it is
worth being precise about what it does and does not show: it shows that a second head with a written
standard fixes rubric failures. It does not show that customers are happier, and this part will not
pretend otherwise —
[5.2](5.2-what-the-pair-costs-in-requests.md) and the *In production* section below deal with the gap
between a rubric and the thing you actually care about.

**Read the `ticket:5044` row.** Seven calls instead of four, and it lands at 4 out of 5 rather than 5.
That is the conflict case from [4.3](../04-stopping/4.3-the-third-verdict.md): the loop ran to the
brake, spent nearly twice the calls, and still could not satisfy the standard. It is also the row
that makes the table believable. A measurement where every case improves cleanly is usually measuring
something too easy.

Now the same harness with a critic that approves everything:

```text
critic mode: sycophant   (each reply is scored out of 5)

    ticket:4903   2/5     2/5         2

  without a critic: 10/25 rubric lines pass
  with a critic:    10/25 rubric lines pass
  bought with:      10 model calls for 5 replies
  nothing was gained. Every call was spent for no measured change.
```

**Ten calls, zero movement.** Every output metric in that run is healthy: five replies produced, five
reviews performed, a hundred per cent approval rate, one round each, no escalations, no errors. A
dashboard built on output metrics shows a system working perfectly, and the replies are exactly the
replies the writer produced alone.

That contrast is the whole reason to build the measurement before you need it. The broken critic and
the working one are indistinguishable from every angle except this one.

## When it breaks

The measurement itself has a failure mode, and it is the one that makes the number worthless while
looking rigorous: **letting the critic score its own work.**

```bash
cd days/day-57-orchestrator-and-critic/lab
uv run python -c "
from _desk import TICKETS, Critic, Writer, grade
for mode in ('honest', 'sycophant'):
    c = Critic(mode=mode)
    passed = failed = 0
    for t in TICKETS:
        d = Writer(ticket=t).draft()
        verdict, _ = c.review(d)
        passed += verdict == 'accept'
        failed += sum(1 for ok in grade(d).values() if not ok)
    print(f'{mode:<10} critic-scored: {passed}/5 approved | standard-scored: {failed} lines failing')
"
```

```text
honest     critic-scored: 0/5 approved | standard-scored: 15 lines failing
sycophant  critic-scored: 5/5 approved | standard-scored: 15 lines failing
```

Score by the critic's own verdict and the **sycophant wins**, 5 out of 5 against 0 out of 5. It is
the best-performing critic by that metric, by a wide margin, on drafts with exactly the same fifteen
failing lines. Any evaluation that uses the reviewer's approval as the quality signal will select for
the reviewer that approves.

The second break is subtler and is the reason the *In production* section exists: a rubric that no
longer represents what anyone cares about. The number goes from 10/25 to 24/25 honestly, and the
customers are no happier, because the five rules stopped being the right five some time ago. That
failure produces a perfect measurement of the wrong thing, and it has no error text at all.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead.** The comparison runs as a **scheduled offline evaluation**, not
as a one-off before launch: a fixed set of tickets, both arms, the same scorer, on a schedule, with
the result recorded over time. That is what turns "is the critic worth it?" from an argument into a
chart. The critic-off arm has to be preserved deliberately — once the critic is the only path, the
baseline is gone, and reconstructing it later means re-running old tickets through a model that has
since changed.

**What degrades at scale.** The rubric drifts away from the thing it stands for. It was written to
approximate "a good reply", and it is checked constantly while the underlying goal is checked never,
so the system optimises the proxy. The countermeasure that works is to periodically sample replies
that scored 5 out of 5 and have a person read them — not to grade the replies, but to grade the
**rubric**. A rubric with no such review is a proxy nobody has validated since the day it was
written.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic.** The average improvement is dominated by
easy tickets, where the critic finds an obvious missing rule and the writer fixes it in one round.
The hard tickets behave like `ticket:5044` — more calls, incomplete improvement, sometimes the
brake — and they are a small share of the fixture and a larger share of what matters. Reporting only
the mean makes the pair look better than it is on exactly the traffic where you were counting on it.
The distribution belongs next to the mean, and so does the escalation rate.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"What did the critic change? Not the approval rate —
that measures whether it agrees with itself, and by that metric a critic that approves everything
scores five out of five. Give me rubric lines passing with and without, on the same tickets, scored by
the rubric, with the call count. And keep the without-arm running on a schedule, because the day the
critic becomes the only path we lose the ability to answer this."*

**The interview question:** *"How do you know your reviewer agent is worth the cost?"* An honest
answer is a design for the measurement: *"Same work twice, scored by something neither agent
controls. On my desk that was 10 of 25 rubric lines passing without a critic and 24 of 25 with one,
for 23 model calls across five replies — about 1.6 calls per line gained. The control that made me
trust it was running the same harness with a critic that approves everything: it spent 10 calls and
moved nothing, while every output metric looked perfect — five reviews, hundred per cent approval,
no errors. That is why I never score by the reviewer's own verdict, because on that metric the broken
critic wins five to nothing. The other thing I keep is the per-ticket breakdown: one of my five took
seven calls and still failed the standard, and that row is the one that tells me where the pair does
not work."*

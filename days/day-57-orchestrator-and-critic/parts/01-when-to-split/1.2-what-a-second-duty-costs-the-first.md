# 1.2 — What a second duty costs the first

## One-line answer

Adding a responsibility to an agent does not add a responsibility — it **trades** one, silently, and
the rule that goes missing is chosen by position in the instruction block rather than by importance.

## The story

The tailor from the last part hires nobody, but he does agree to take parcels.

A courier company asks if customers can leave packages at his counter for collection. It is a small
thing. He agrees, and it works. Then he agrees to hold keys for the flats upstairs. Then the phone
starts ringing about the parcels, so he answers the phone.

None of these took the place of anything. He did not stop tailoring. If you asked him what his job
was he would say the same thing he said a year ago.

But the trousers you collected last month had the fold pressed slightly off. Not wrong — off. And
when you mentioned it he was genuinely surprised, because he remembers pressing them, and he
remembers pressing them carefully.

Here is the thing that makes this hard to fix. **He did not drop the pressing.** He dropped the
*checking* of the pressing, which was never a task he could point at. It was the thing that used to
happen in the quiet gap between pinning and pressing, and the gap now has a phone in it. Nobody can
report a missing task that was never on a list.

## The idea in plain language

Two terms, and the second one is the one that will be used for the rest of the day.

An agent's **instruction block** is the text that tells it what to do — its system instruction. On
Day 6 this was one job stated in a few sentences. In a system that has been running for a while it
is a list of rules that has only ever grown.

**Instruction dilution** is what [1.1](1.1-one-agent-is-usually-the-right-answer.md) named: rules
stop being honoured without being removed. The word matters because it points at the mechanism.
Nothing is *lost*; the same attention is spread across more claims on it, so each claim gets less.

Three things make this different from ordinary forgetting, and they are the reason it survives code
review:

1. **The instruction is still there.** You can open the prompt and read the rule that was not
   followed. Every reviewer who checks "did we tell it to do this?" finds that we did.
2. **The loss is positional, not semantic.** The rule that goes missing is not the least important
   one. It is the one in the wrong *place* — which on real instruction blocks means the middle, and
   in a block that grew by appending means whatever got pushed there.
3. **The newest rules are the most at risk**, which inverts the normal expectation. You add a rule
   because something went wrong; the rule goes to the end; the block is now longer; and the rule you
   just added is the one with the weakest hold. The person watching for it reports that it "does not
   work", and the diagnosis is almost always "the wording must be wrong", so the wording is changed,
   which does nothing.

## Why Sutra needs it

This is the measurement that justifies the whole day. Day 57 is about to spend a second agent —
which is a second request per reply, on a free tier — and the only honest reason to do that is that
one agent demonstrably cannot hold both jobs.

It is also the reason [3.1](../03-the-critic/3.1-a-check-you-never-ran-cannot-fail.md) works the way
it does. Writing and judging are not two rules in a list; they are two *jobs*, and asking one pass to
do both is the extreme case of what this part measures. Day 58's triage graph then inherits the
conclusion: each stage of intake, classify, research, draft and review holds a small number of rules,
and the stage boundaries are placed where the duty count would otherwise cross the line.

## The paper behind it

> **Lost in the Middle: How Language Models Use Long Contexts**
> `arXiv:2307.03172` · 2023 · <https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172>

It showed that a model's use of information depends on **where** in the input that information sits:
material at the beginning and end is used far more reliably than material in the middle, so a longer
input does not degrade uniformly — it degrades in a shape.

[Day 19's paper part](../../../day-19-context-engineering-selection/papers/01-lost-in-the-middle.md).

## The mechanism

The measurement is the same script as the last part, read for a different number. `ATTENTION` stands
in for how many rules one pass reliably honours, and `satisfied` takes the first `ATTENTION` of them.
That "first" is doing real work and is the paper's finding in its crudest possible form:

```python
# The stand-in, and the assumption inside it, stated so it can be argued with.
ATTENTION = 4


def satisfied(instructions: tuple[str, ...], attention: int = ATTENTION) -> tuple[str, ...]:
    """Which instructions survive one pass: the first `attention` of them, and no more."""
    return instructions[:attention]
```

**Line by line:**

- `instructions[:attention]` keeps the **front** of the list. That is deliberately the simplest
  possible stand-in for the paper's shape, and it is deliberately not a random sample: a random
  sample would model forgetting, and forgetting is not what this is. Position decides.
- The function takes `attention` as an argument with a default, so a reader can move it and watch the
  argument change rather than being asked to believe a number.
- It is a pure function of its inputs, which is what lets `onehead.py` print an exact `dropped` list
  instead of a probability. A teaching measurement that cannot be reproduced line for line teaches
  nothing.
- What this does **not** model: the real effect is graded rather than a cliff, and it hits the middle
  hardest rather than the tail. The paper is the honest account; this is the smallest thing that
  makes the consequence visible.

Now run the arm that matters — the same seven duties, split across two heads instead of one:

```bash
cd days/day-57-orchestrator-and-critic/lab
uv run python onehead.py --split
```

**Line by line:**

- `--split` gives the writer the three rules about *what the reply says* plus `cite_ticket`, and the
  critic the three about *what the reply is like* plus `match_tone`. That division is not arbitrary
  and it is the subject of [1.3](1.3-the-three-questions-that-decide-a-split.md).
- Each head keeps the same `ATTENTION` of four. This is the honest version of the comparison: the
  split does not buy a better model, it buys a second budget of the same size.
- The script prints `dropped` per head and then the total, because the total is the number that
  decides whether the split was worth a request.

Measured on 2026-09-05:

```text
two agents, attention 4 each:
  writer  (write the reply)
    asked for  ['cause', 'fix', 'next_step', 'cite_ticket']
    delivered  ['cause', 'fix', 'next_step', 'cite_ticket']
    dropped    []
  critic  (judge the reply)
    asked for  ['no_jargon', 'short', 'match_tone']
    delivered  ['no_jargon', 'short', 'match_tone']
    dropped    []

  dropped across the pair: 0
```

Put the three numbers in one place, because the argument of this section is entirely contained in
them:

| Shape | Rules asked for | Rules dropped |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One agent, day one | 5 | 1 |
| One agent, day ninety | 7 | 3 |
| Two agents, day ninety | 7 | 0 |

**Nothing about the model changed between row two and row three.** Same attention budget, same seven
rules, same fixture. The only thing that changed is that seven rules were divided into four and
three, and both fit.

That is the case for a second head, stated as a number rather than as a principle — and it is
important that it is this narrow. The split did not make the agent smarter, it did not add
capability, and it bought exactly one thing: **room**. Every other benefit claimed for multi-agent
architectures has to be argued for separately, and most of them will not survive
[5.1](../05-does-it-help/5.1-did-the-critic-actually-help.md).

## When it breaks

The break here is the one that wastes a week, and it produces no error at all.

A rule is not honoured. Somebody rewrites it — in bold, in capitals, with "IMPORTANT" in front of it,
moved into its own paragraph. It still is not honoured, because the block is still too long and the
rule is still in the same place. Then somebody adds a *second* rule restating the first, which makes
the block longer, which makes it worse.

You can watch that happen. Add an eighth rule and re-run:

```bash
cd days/day-57-orchestrator-and-critic/lab
uv run python -c "
import onehead
rules = onehead.DUTIES + onehead.LATER + ('match_tone_seriously',)
print('dropped:', [r for r in rules if r not in onehead.satisfied(rules)])
"
```

```text
dropped: ['short', 'cite_ticket', 'match_tone', 'match_tone_seriously']
```

The restatement joined the dropped list. Four rules are now not being honoured, and the last change
made it worse while looking like an escalation of effort. The lesson is uncomfortable and worth
stating plainly: **when a rule is being ignored, adding words is the intervention most likely to make
it worse.**

The other break is the split done in the wrong place. Divide the seven rules as six and one and the
pair fails exactly as hard as the single agent did, because a split only buys room if both halves
fit. That is what makes [1.3](1.3-the-three-questions-that-decide-a-split.md) necessary: the split
has to be along a real seam, not down the middle of the list.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead.** They keep the standard as **data with identifiers**, not as
prose — the list of rules is a table, each rule has a key, and the instruction block is generated
from it. That sounds like ceremony until the first time somebody asks "is rule seven still working?",
which is unanswerable about a paragraph and trivial about a keyed list. It is also what makes the
critic possible at all: [3.2](../03-the-critic/3.2-a-critic-needs-something-it-can-fail-against.md)
needs something it can *fail against*, and a paragraph offers nothing to fail.

**What degrades at scale.** Instruction blocks accumulate a rule per incident and never lose one, so
their length is a function of the system's age. Meanwhile the middle of the block — where the oldest
non-first rules live — is exactly where the paper says attention is thinnest. The result is that a
mature system's most battle-tested rules are sitting in its least reliable position, and no
individual change ever caused it.

**The failure mode that only appears with real traffic.** Dilution is invisible in aggregate because
it is selective. The rules that survive are the ones at the front, and the ones at the front are
usually the ones about *what to say*, which is what most tickets need. The rules that go are the ones
about form — tone, length, citation — which matter on the small fraction of tickets where a customer
is already angry. Your averages improve while the tickets that could escalate get worse.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"This prompt has eleven rules and we added two of
them last month. Before we add a twelfth: which of the existing eleven are still being honoured? If
we cannot answer that, we are not adding a rule, we are choosing at random which existing rule to
turn off."*

**The interview question:** *"Your agent is ignoring one of its instructions. What do you do?"* An
honest answer does not start with the wording: *"First I would check how many instructions it has,
because the most common cause is not the wording of that rule but the length of the block it lives
in — position matters more than phrasing, which is what 'Lost in the Middle' measured. I would
count the rules and count which ones are actually being honoured. If the block is long, rewriting
the rule is the intervention most likely to make it worse, because it makes the block longer: I have
watched an eighth rule restating the seventh join the dropped list. The fix is to take duties out —
move a group of them to a second agent, or make some of them deterministic checks outside the model
instead of requests to it."*

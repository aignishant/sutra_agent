# 6.3 — The drop safe, not another supervisor

## One-line answer

Every rung of the ladder works by reading text, and text can be rewritten — so the ladder **narrows
the pipe** and does not close it, and the thing that closes a channel is a rule that never reads: a
capability the agent does not hold, or a value refused at the sink because of where it came from.

## The story

A shop keeps losing money at the till. Not much, and not every day, but the numbers do not add up at
the end of the week.

The first response is a supervisor: somebody senior standing near the till at busy times. The
shortfall drops and does not stop. So a second supervisor is added on the late shift, and then a
camera pointed at the drawer, and then a rule that two people must be present for a refund.

Each addition is reasonable and each one costs a wage. And each one adds a person who now has to be
trusted, watched, rostered and — this is the part nobody says out loud in the meeting — who is also
standing next to an open drawer full of cash.

What ends it is not a supervisor. It is a grey steel box bolted to the floor under the counter with a
slot in the top. Cash above a certain amount goes in the slot and nobody on the shop floor can open
it; the collection company has the key. The staff are not watched more closely. **They are not near
the money any more.**

## The idea in plain language

This is the day's closing argument, and it starts by being fair to everything that came before it.

**What the ladder is worth**, measured rather than asserted:

- It catches the unmotivated attacker. Part
  [5.4](../05-measure-it/5.4-the-same-instruction-five-ways.md) measured 2 of 5 rewrites, and the
  original payload — the one somebody copied out of an article — is one of the two.
- It costs almost nothing when ordered cheap-first. Part
  [1.2](../01-the-ladder/1.2-cheap-and-certain-first.md) measured seven model calls instead of
  fourteen across the same fourteen tickets.
- It produces a **record**. Part [4.3](../04-what-goes-out/4.3-the-rewrite-that-lies.md) is about why
  that matters: a refusal is an event with a reason, and events are what an investigation is made of.
- It raises the cost of an attack. An attacker who has to rewrite, test and rewrite again is an
  attacker doing work, and most of them will not.

**What it is not worth**, from the same measurements:

- 2 of 5 against somebody who has read the checks (part 5.4).
- 0 of 3 against a payload split across two archive rows, *including* when the assembled context is
  checked — because the instruction never exists as a string anywhere in the pipeline (part 5.4).
- The best rung on the ladder, a model judge, is a language model reading attacker-written text, and
  defending it means adding another one (part
  [6.1](6.1-the-notes-for-the-assessor-box.md)). That regress does not terminate.

Put those together and the honest sentence is the one part
[1.3](../01-the-ladder/1.3-a-sign-is-not-a-gate.md) wrote on the first page of this day: **a check
that works by reading text and forming an opinion can be argued with.** Every rung reads. So the
ladder narrows the pipe — usefully, cheaply, with a record — and closes nothing.

What closes something is a rule that never reads:

| Control | What it asks | Can the text argue with it? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Phrase list, judge, heuristic | *what does this text say?* | yes — rewrite it |
| **The agent does not hold the tool** | *is this capability registered?* | no |
| **This value may not reach this sink** | *where did this value come from?* | no |
| **This data never entered the context** | *what was retrieved for this stage?* | no |

Those three are the next three days. Day 68 is least privilege — the tool the agent does not have.
Day 69 is data boundaries — the value that never arrived. And the provenance rule in the third row is
the subject of the paper this day ends with, which is why it is the last thing you read.

Stop hiring supervisors. Bolt the box to the floor.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because a day that built a ladder and stopped would leave a reader with a false model of what they
have. The measurements in section 5 are the argument, and this part is the conclusion they were taken
for.

It also sets up Phase 10's remaining days honestly. Day 68 and Day 69 are not "more security on top
of the guardrails" — they are a **different kind** of control, and the distinction is the one thing
from this day that survives every framework change. A reader who finishes Day 67 thinking guardrails
are the defence will build a system where the guardrail is the last thing between an injection and a
delete, which is precisely the arrangement part [6.1](6.1-the-notes-for-the-assessor-box.md) showed
being talked out of its verdict.

And Day 66 already reached the same place from the other side. Its threat model has a `refuse` row —
no outbound fetch tool in the answering path — argued for on the grounds that cutting the leg is a
one-line change that provably closes the trifecta, while every alternative is a check that has to keep
being right. This part is that argument, arrived at again by measurement rather than by design.

## The paper behind it

> *Defeating Prompt Injections by Design* · `arXiv:2503.18813` · 2025
> · <https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18813>

It claims that prompt injection is not defeated by reading the text better but by construction: keep
the control flow derived from the trusted query, track where every value came from, and enforce the
policy at the **sink** with ordinary code, so that a model which has been successfully injected can
still say anything and cannot cause anything.

Taught in full, with its demo, in
[the paper document](../../papers/01-defeating-prompt-injections-by-design.md).

## The mechanism

Today's eval is the shape of what this day hands to the next one, and it is worth reading as an
argument rather than as a chore. Six checks against a module that does not exist yet:

```bash
cd days/day-67-guardrail-callbacks/lab
uv run python gate.py; echo "exit: $?"
```

**Line by line:**

- `cd` first: the lab's scripts import their siblings by plain name. `gate.py` additionally puts the
  repository root on `sys.path`, so that a missing `sutra.guardrails` reports as a missing *module*
  rather than as a missing *package* — blaming the right thing.
- `echo "exit: $?"` prints the exit code, because the gate is a test and a test is a thing that
  passes or fails, not a thing you read.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
Day 67 gate - the guardrail module the build brief asks for

  ....  sutra/guardrails.py is not importable (No module named 'sutra.guardrails')

  FAIL  sutra.guardrails exists
  FAIL  it exports inspect_text(text) -> (reason, checks_run)
  FAIL  it exports a before_tool guard named block_disallowed_send
  FAIL  the guard's parameters are named (tool, args, tool_context)
  FAIL  a refusal is truthy
  FAIL  tests/test_guardrails.py exists

findings: 6
  - sutra.guardrails exists - write it, per the hub's section 4
  - it exports inspect_text(text) -> (reason, checks_run)
  - it exports a before_tool guard named block_disallowed_send
  - the guard's parameters are named (tool, args, tool_context) - the runtime calls callbacks by keyword; see part 2.4
  - a refusal is truthy - a falsy block is overwritten by the next callback; see part 2.5
  - tests/test_guardrails.py exists
exit: 1
```

Six red, and every one of them is red for a reason this day discovered rather than a reason invented
to make the gate look thorough. Two are worth pointing at specifically:

- **`the guard's parameters are named (tool, args, tool_context)`** exists because of part
  [2.4](../02-where-a-check-stands/2.4-the-guardrail-that-never-ran.md). ADK calls callbacks by
  keyword, so a guardrail written as `def guard(ctx, req)` raises `TypeError` on the first request
  instead of guarding anything, and neither an editor nor a type checker warns you. The check reads
  the signature with `inspect` and compares the parameter names, because the names *are* the contract.
- **`a refusal is truthy`** exists because of part
  [2.5](../02-where-a-check-stands/2.5-the-empty-dict-that-fails-open.md). The runtime stops walking a
  callback list only on a truthy result, so a guardrail refusing with an empty dict is silently
  overridden by any later callback returning `None`, and the tool it refused runs. `bool(REFUSAL)` is
  a one-expression test for a failure that is otherwise invisible.

Read those two together and notice what kind of checks they are. Neither asks whether your guardrail
is *good*. They ask whether it is **wired in a way that can work at all** — and both of them
correspond to a bug that produces a system which looks guarded and is not. That is the most useful
thing a gate can check about a filter, because the filter's quality is exactly the thing section 5
showed you cannot take on trust.

## When it breaks

The failure mode of this part's argument is over-correcting, and it produces a worse system than
over-trusting the ladder does.

**"Guardrails are theatre, remove them."** That does not follow from anything measured here. The
ladder catches the copied payload, costs almost nothing when ordered cheap-first, and produces the
record you will want during an incident. Removing it means the unmotivated attacker succeeds and no
event is written when they try. The measured claim is that it is not a **boundary**, not that it is
not worth having.

**"Structural controls, therefore no measurement needed."** The controls in the table above have their
own failure modes and this curriculum has not reached them yet. Least privilege is only as good as the
tool inventory, and Day 66's channel count found four ways out that no tool registry knows about.
Provenance is only total if every operation propagates it. Data boundaries fail when a legitimate
workflow genuinely needs the value and somebody widens the boundary rather than the workflow. None of
those is a reason to prefer filters; all of them are reasons to keep measuring.

**The one thing that is not negotiable** is ordering. A filter may run before a boundary and must
never be the last thing before an irreversible action. If the only thing standing between a
successfully injected model and `send_reply` is something that reads text, then part
[6.1](6.1-the-notes-for-the-assessor-box.md)'s paragraph is all it takes.

## In production

**How a team decides which controls are structural and which are advisory.** The question that sorts
them is not *how good is this check* but *what happens if it is wrong*. A control whose failure is
recoverable can be advisory: a filter, a score, a queue for review. A control whose failure is
irreversible — data sent, a record deleted, money moved — has to be structural, because "usually
right" is not a property you can hold an irreversible action to. In practice that means walking the
tool list and sorting it by reversibility first, then arguing about checks. Day 66's channel
enumeration is the same exercise pointed at the outputs.

**What an honest security review of a guardrail-only defence concludes.** That the system raises the
cost of an attack and does not bound its impact — and those are different claims that get conflated
constantly. A reviewer who accepts "we have prompt-injection guardrails" without asking for both
columns and an adversarial number has accepted a statement about effort rather than about outcome. The
useful review question is: *assume the model is injected and does exactly what the attacker asked.
What happens?* If the answer depends on a check that reads text, the answer is unknown.

**What degrades at scale.** The ladder's numbers decay silently: the corpus is static, the attacker
population is not, and the reported detection rate stays flat forever (part
[5.4](../05-measure-it/5.4-the-same-instruction-five-ways.md)). Structural controls decay differently
and more visibly — a tool gets added, a boundary gets widened for a feature — which is one more
argument for them, because a control that decays through a code review is a control somebody can
notice decaying.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"I'm approving the guardrail module, and I want the
threat-model row updated at the same time. Right now it reads as though the guardrails mitigate
injection. They raise the cost — we've got 2 of 5 against rewrites and 0 of 3 against a split payload,
which are the numbers that belong in the row. The mitigation for the irreversible actions is Day 68's
allowlist and Day 69's boundary, and until those land the honest status is that a successful injection
reaching `send_reply` is bounded by nothing except a filter. I'd rather have that written down than
implied."*

**The interview question:** *"How would you stop prompt injection?"* An honest answer: *"I'd start by
saying you don't stop it by reading better. I built the layered version — cheap deterministic checks
first, then a model judge — and measured it: six of six on our own fixtures, two of five once the same
instruction was rewritten by someone who'd read the checks, and zero when the payload was split across
two retrieved rows, because the phrase never exists as a string anywhere. The judge helps and it's a
model reading the attacker's text, so it can be addressed directly — I've seen the identical payload
graded safe after adding a paragraph aimed at the classifier — and defending the judge means adding
another reader, which doesn't terminate. So the ladder is worth having for cost, coverage of the lazy
attacker, and the audit record, and it is not the control. The control is not holding the capability,
or refusing a value at the sink because of where it came from — that's the CaMeL paper's argument, and
it works because it never reads the text at all. In practice: sort the tool list by reversibility, put
structural controls in front of everything irreversible, and let the filters do the cheap work in
front of them."*

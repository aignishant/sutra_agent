# 1.2 — Cheap and certain before expensive and fuzzy

## One-line answer

Ordering the ladder cheapest-first halves the number of model calls the guardrail spends — seven
instead of fourteen over the same fourteen tickets — and it costs you more of the checks that are
free, which is a trade worth making and worth stating rather than assuming.

## The story

You go to a pharmacy to collect a prescription. There is a counter, and behind it two people.

The assistant at the counter takes your slip, looks at the name, looks at the date, and checks
whether the box is in the drawer with your name on it. That takes a moment and involves no judgement
at all: either the name matches or it does not, either the date is inside the window or it is not.

If anything about the slip is odd, she does not try to work it out. She turns round and hands it to
the pharmacist, who reads the whole thing properly, checks it against what else you are taking, and
sometimes telephones the surgery.

Nobody designed this to be clever. It works because the two jobs are genuinely different. The
assistant's checks are quick and certain and cannot really be argued with. The pharmacist's are slow
and expensive and involve reading, and the queue would be out of the door if every slip started
there.

Now imagine the shop reversed the order — the pharmacist reads every slip in full first, and the
assistant checks the name afterwards. Every answer would be the same. The queue would be enormous.

## The idea in plain language

The ladder from [1.1](1.1-one-lock-is-not-security.md) has an order, and the order is not
cosmetic. Two properties sort the checks, and they usually agree.

**Certainty.** Some checks cannot be wrong. `too_long` compares a number to a number: a body of
5,000 characters is longer than a 4,000-character limit whatever anybody thinks about it. Others are
judgements. `looks_instructional` decides whether a sentence is an instruction aimed at the reader,
and reasonable people — and reasonable models — will disagree about particular sentences.

**Cost.** Some checks are arithmetic on a string, which is free. Others are a request to a provider,
which on this project is the currency: Day 24 taught that budgets here are denominated in requests
per minute and per day, not in money, and Addendum 02 fixes us to free tiers where those numbers are
small.

Put the certain, free checks first and two good things happen at once. Anything they catch never
reaches the expensive check, so you spend less. And anything they catch is refused for a reason you
can defend in a meeting, rather than for a reason a model formed.

There is a third property, and it points the other way, which is why this part measures rather than
asserts. **Coverage.** The cheap checks catch narrow, specific things. Ordering by cost means the
broad check runs last and therefore runs most often. You are not getting something for nothing; you
are choosing which resource to spend.

## Why Sutra needs it

Day 58's triage graph has a research stage that retrieves rows, and Day 50 argued for a `k` of about
three. If the guardrail on retrieved text is a model call, then guarding one answer costs three
extra requests, on top of the classify and draft calls the graph already makes.

Day 59's gate measured the graph at five model calls per ticket and found that twelve tickets a night
needs sixty requests against a free tier of twenty — a number that was reported amber rather than
tuned away. Adding an unordered guardrail to that graph would take it from sixty to well over a
hundred. The ordering in this part is one of the few levers that reduces the count without removing
a check.

## The mechanism

Two orders, the same four checks, the same fourteen fixtures. `lab/ladder.py` prices both.

```python
CHEAP_FIRST = [g.too_long, g.bad_encoding, g.phrase_match, g.looks_instructional]
EXPENSIVE_FIRST = [g.looks_instructional, g.phrase_match, g.bad_encoding, g.too_long]

# What each check costs, in units of "one model call".
COST = {"too_long": 0, "bad_encoding": 0, "phrase_match": 0, "looks_instructional": 1}


def cost_of(ladder) -> tuple[int, int]:
    """Total model-call cost, and total checks run, over the whole corpus."""
    calls = checks = 0
    for _ref, text, _hostile in labelled():
        for check in ladder:
            checks += 1
            calls += COST[check.__name__]
            if check(text) is not None:
                break
    return calls, checks
```

**Line by line:**

- `COST` prices `looks_instructional` at one model call and everything else at zero. On this desk
  the fuzzy check is a stand-in for a classifier; pricing it at 1 and the rest at 0 is the honest
  simplification, and it is stated here rather than buried so a reader can disagree with it.
- The inner loop increments `checks` **before** running the check, because a check you ran is a
  check you paid for even when it says nothing.
- `break` on the first objection is what makes the ordering matter at all. Without the early exit
  both orders cost the same and this part has no subject.
- `check.__name__` looks up the price rather than passing cost alongside each function. That keeps
  `_guards.py` free of any notion of money, which matters because the same functions are scored for
  accuracy in [5.1](../05-measure-it/5.1-both-columns.md) where cost is irrelevant.
- It returns both numbers. Returning only `calls` would let the reader believe cheap-first is free,
  and the second number is exactly the price.

**Line by line:**

- No arguments: the default run prices both orders over the whole corpus.
- The corpus is the same fourteen fixtures every other part of this day uses, so the numbers here
  and the numbers in section 5 are about the same tickets.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
The same four checks, two orders, over 14 fixtures.

  order              model calls    checks run
  ----------------------------------------------
  cheap first        7              49
  expensive first    14             38

  Same verdicts, same corpus. The order decides how many times you would have paid a
  provider to answer a question a string comparison had already settled.
```

Read both columns, because a reader who takes only the first one has learned the wrong lesson.

**Model calls halve: fourteen to seven.** Expensive-first pays the classifier for every single
ticket, because the classifier is the first thing every ticket meets. Cheap-first pays it only for
the seven tickets that survived three free checks. On a free tier of twenty requests a day, that
difference is the difference between the guardrail fitting in the budget and not.

**Checks run go up: thirty-eight to forty-nine.** This is the part that is usually left out.
Expensive-first is *better* at short-circuiting in raw check count, because its first check is the
broadest one — when `looks_instructional` fires, it fires immediately and three cheap checks are
skipped. Cheap-first grinds through the free checks on every ticket before reaching the one likely
to fire.

So the trade is stated exactly: **cheap-first spends eleven more units of free work to save seven
units of the only work that costs anything.** That is obviously the right trade here, and it is
right *because* of the price list, not because cheap-first is a rule of nature. If your expensive
check were free and your cheap check were a network round trip, the order would flip.

## When it breaks

The ordering breaks when the price list is wrong, and the most common way to get it wrong is to
forget that a check can be cheap in money and expensive in time.

There is also a break specific to ordering that has nothing to do with cost, and it is the one that
bites in production. **Put a logging callback last and it never sees the events you built it for.**
That is measured in [2.3](../02-where-a-check-stands/2.3-a-ladder-is-a-list.md), and it is worth
naming here because it is the one case where the cost-ordering rule and the correctness rule point
in opposite directions: logging is cheap, so cost-ordering says put it anywhere, while the runtime's
short-circuit says put it first or lose it.

The break you can reach right now is the price list disagreeing with itself. `ladder.py --extra`
adds a fifth check to the ladder and does not add it to `COST` — which is exactly what happens when
somebody lands a new guardrail and updates one file of the two:

That is what happens when somebody adds a fifth check to `_guards.LADDER` and does not add it to
`COST`. The `KeyError` is the good outcome: the alternative design, `COST.get(name, 0)`, would have
priced the new check at zero and quietly reported that the guardrail got cheaper when it got more
expensive. A missing price should stop the report, not default to free.

## In production

**What a professional writes instead.** They do not keep the price list in a dictionary beside the
checks. They measure it: the guardrail records, per check, how many times it ran and how long it
took, and the ordering is reviewed against that record rather than against an estimate. The estimate
is always wrong in the same direction — people underestimate the cheap checks, because a regular
expression over a large body of text is not free, and a badly written one is catastrophically not
free.

**What degrades at scale.** The cheap checks are linear in text length, and retrieved rows get
longer as an archive grows. A regular expression with nested quantifiers over a long body can go
quadratic, and the check that was three microseconds in your corpus becomes a stall on a real
ticket. That failure looks like a hung request rather than a security event, which is why it is
usually diagnosed last.

**The failure that only shows with real traffic.** Under concurrency the ordering interacts with
your rate limiter. Cheap-first sends *fewer* classifier calls but sends them in bursts, because the
tickets that survive three checks tend to arrive together — a poisoned archive is poisoned in
batches. A budget that looks fine as a daily average blows the per-minute limit, which is exactly
the window-edge problem Day 70 builds a router around.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"The order here is right but it is asserted, not
recorded. Put the per-check timing and hit count behind the same interface as the verdict, so the
next person can re-derive this ordering from production data instead of trusting a comment. And
price the regex — it is the only check here whose cost depends on the input."*

**The interview question:** *"You have four safety checks and one of them calls a model. How do you
order them?"* The answer that shows experience does not stop at "cheapest first": *"Cheapest and
most certain first, because an early exit skips the expensive check and because a refusal from
arithmetic is defensible in a way a refusal from a classifier is not. But I would say what it costs
— I measured it and cheap-first ran eleven more checks in total to save seven model calls, so it is
a trade rather than a free win. And I would keep logging out of the ordering entirely, because in
ADK a callback that blocks stops the ones behind it, so a logger placed last is silent about exactly
the requests you blocked."*

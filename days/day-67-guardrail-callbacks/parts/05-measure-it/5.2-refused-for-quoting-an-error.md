# 5.2 — Refused for quoting an error

## One-line answer

A false-positive rate is an abstraction and a refused customer is not: `ticket:4601` asked whether a
banner reading `SYSTEM: temporary outage, please retry` came from the desk or from their own network,
and the ladder refused them — **after 3 checks, 0 model calls** — for quoting the message they were
asking about.

## The story

A man in an office gets an email that looks like a scam. It claims to be from the finance team, it
asks him to confirm bank details, and something about it is wrong.

He does the right thing. He forwards it to the security mailbox with a note: *"Is this real? It's
asking me to change the supplier account."*

The mail bounces. The company's filter has read what he sent, found the phishing text inside it, and
refused to deliver it — because the message he forwarded contains exactly the things the filter was
built to stop.

He tries once more, gets the same bounce, decides somebody else has probably reported it, and gets on
with his morning. The filter worked precisely as designed. The only email it stopped that day was the
one warning people about the attack.

## The idea in plain language

Part [5.1](5.1-both-columns.md) reported one false alarm out of eight benign fixtures — `1/8`, a rate
of 0.12. This part is that one, in full, because a rate is a thing you can approve in a meeting and a
refused customer is not.

Here is `ticket:4601`, exactly as it arrives:

> Ticket 4601.
> The export screen shows a red banner reading 'SYSTEM: temporary outage, please retry'.
> Is that message from you or from our own network?

That is a good ticket. The customer is being precise, they have quoted the exact wording, and they are
asking a sensible diagnostic question that a support desk exists to answer.

The ladder refuses it, and the reason is `phrase 'system:'`.

The phrase is in their message **because the banner says so**. They are not using the word; they are
**reporting** it. And that distinction — using versus mentioning — is invisible to a string check,
because in both cases the same characters are in the same order. The check has access to the surface
form and the difference lives entirely in the intent, which the surface form does not carry.

This is a general shape and it is worth naming, because once you see it you find it everywhere:
**the people most likely to quote an attack are the people reporting it.** Security-conscious
customers, developers, anyone forwarding something suspicious. A filter built on the vocabulary of an
attack has a systematic bias against exactly the users you would most like to hear from.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because the day is about to make a recommendation, and the recommendation depends on this ticket
existing.

Part [1.3](../01-the-ladder/1.3-a-sign-is-not-a-gate.md) drew the line between a **filter** — a check
that reads text, forms an opinion, and can be argued with — and a **boundary**, a check that never
reads. It argued that this day builds the first kind while pointing at the days that build the second.
That argument needs a cost attached to it, or it is just a taxonomy. This ticket is the cost.

There is a second reason, specific to a support desk. Sutra's product is answering people. A guardrail
that refuses customers is not adding friction to a security control; it is failing at the thing the
system is for. The desk that never falls for an injection and never answers a question about an error
banner has not been secured, it has been switched off.

## The mechanism

Run the one false positive on its own:

```bash
cd days/day-67-guardrail-callbacks/lab
uv run python falsepos.py
```

**Line by line:**

- `cd` first: the lab's scripts import their siblings by plain name.
- No flag prints the victim ticket in full, the ladder's verdict, the cost in model calls and the text
  the customer actually received. `VICTIM = BENIGN[0]` is `ticket:4601`, chosen because it is the row
  part 5.1's `--rows` output marked with `*`.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
ticket:4601, a real customer, in full:

  Ticket 4601.
  The export screen shows a red banner reading 'SYSTEM: temporary outage, please retry'.
  Is that message from you or from our own network?

  ladder verdict   phrase 'system:'  (after 3 checks)
  model calls      0
  what they got    "This request could not be processed. (phrase 'system:')"

  They quoted an error message back to the desk and were refused for quoting it.
  The phrase that fired is 'system:', which is in the banner because the banner
  says so.
```

Three lines of that output deserve individual attention.

**`after 3 checks`.** The ladder is ordered cheap-first, which part
[1.2](../01-the-ladder/1.2-cheap-and-certain-first.md) argued for and measured: `too_long`,
`bad_encoding`, then `phrase_match`. The customer got as far as the third rung and no further. The
ordering is correct and it is working exactly as designed.

**`model calls 0`.** This is the uncomfortable one. Cheap-first means the desk spent **nothing** being
wrong. That is a feature when the ladder is right — part 1.2's whole case is that it halves the model
calls a guardrail costs — and it is the reason this mistake is sustainable. An error that costs
nothing gets repeated indefinitely, because there is no budget line, no latency spike and no
rate-limit warning to make anybody look.

**`"This request could not be processed. (phrase 'system:')"`.** Read it as the customer does. It
does not say what to do next. It does not offer a person. It leaks a fragment of the guardrail's
internals — `phrase 'system:'` — which tells an attacker something useful and tells the customer
nothing at all. And it is indistinguishable, from where they are sitting, from the desk being broken —
which, given they were writing in about an outage banner in the first place, is a reasonable
conclusion for them to reach.

The refusal itself is a `before_model_callback` returning an `LlmResponse`, which is the "block"
verdict from part [2.2](../02-where-a-check-stands/2.2-allow-block-rewrite.md):

```python
def guard_for(text: str):
    def guard(callback_context, llm_request):
        reason, _n = g.inspect_text(text)
        if reason:
            return LlmResponse(content=d.say(f"This request could not be processed. ({reason})"))
        return None

    return guard
```

**Line by line:**

- `guard_for` is a factory returning the callback, because ADK calls the callback with its own
  arguments and the text under test has to be closed over rather than passed in.
- The inner `guard` uses the keyword parameter names `callback_context` and `llm_request` — part
  [2.4](../02-where-a-check-stands/2.4-the-guardrail-that-never-ran.md)'s contract.
- `reason, _n = g.inspect_text(text)` runs the whole ladder and keeps the reason. `_n` is the number
  of checks run, discarded here and printed by the caller.
- Returning an `LlmResponse` **replaces** the model call rather than modifying it, which is why the
  model-call count is zero. Compare part [4.3](../04-what-goes-out/4.3-the-rewrite-that-lies.md): a
  block produces an event and a record, and this one does — the failure here is not that the refusal
  is invisible, it is that the refusal is wrong.
- `return None` on the other branch allows the call. Not `return False`, not `return {}` — part
  [2.5](../02-where-a-check-stands/2.5-the-empty-dict-that-fails-open.md) is the part about why the
  falsy alternatives are a bug.

## When it breaks

The thing this part is really about is what happens **next** for this customer, and the answer is
nothing.

There is no appeal path. The customer is not told a check refused them; they are told the request
could not be processed. There is no button that says *I think this is wrong*, no queue this lands in,
and nobody at the desk sees a refusal that needs a second opinion. From the outside it looks like the
product is broken, which — for a customer already writing in about an error banner — is a completely
reasonable thing to conclude and act on.

That produces the failure mode that makes false positives so persistent: **they are not reported.** A
customer who gets a wrong answer complains, because there is something to complain about. A customer
who gets a refusal from an automated desk does not file a bug against your guardrail. They try once
more, get the same thing, and leave — to a phone number, to a competitor, or to nowhere. The signal
that the check is wrong is destroyed by the same event that generates it.

So the operational fix is not a better phrase list. It is two changes to what a refusal *is*:

1. **A refusal must be distinguishable from an outage**, in the customer's words. *"We can't process
   this automatically — a person will pick it up"* is a different message from *"this request could
   not be processed"*, and only the first one leaves the customer knowing what is happening.
2. **A refusal must go somewhere.** Into a human queue, not into a dead end. That converts a false
   positive from a lost customer into a slow answer, and — this is the part that compounds — it turns
   the refusal into a **labelled example**, because a person has now looked at it and can say whether
   the check was right. Section 5.1's honest corpus has to come from somewhere, and this is where.

Neither of those makes the check more accurate. Both of them make being wrong survivable, which is the
only realistic goal: part [5.4](5.4-the-same-instruction-five-ways.md) is about to show that the
check's accuracy is worse than the fixtures suggest, and a control you cannot make perfect is one you
have to make safe to be wrong with.

## In production

**What degrades at scale, and why nobody sees it.** At ten tickets a day somebody reads the refusals
and notices this one. At ten thousand, nobody reads them, and the only trace is a slow drift in
resolution rate that has a dozen plausible explanations. The refusal rate is a number you have to
choose to look at; it does not arrive on its own, because from inside the system a refusal is a
successful control action. The alert worth having is not on the rate itself but on its **change** — a
phrase deployed on Tuesday that moves the refusal rate on Wednesday is a signal you can act on.

**The population this systematically excludes.** Not a random 12% of customers. The bias is
structural: people who quote error messages, people forwarding suspicious mail, developers pasting
logs and callback URLs, anyone reporting a scam. Those are disproportionately your most technically
engaged users and your best early warning system for real attacks, and a vocabulary filter is
selectively deaf to exactly them. Part [5.3](5.3-five-more-words.md) shows what happens when the list
grows: the developer reporting a broken staging callback joins them.

**What a professional builds alongside the check.** Three things, none of which is a better regular
expression. A refusal message a customer can act on. A queue the refusal lands in, with a person at
the end of it. And a stored record of the refused text with the rule that fired, so that the false
positives become the labelled corpus part 5.1 said you cannot measure without. The third one is the
compounding investment: it is the only mechanism by which a filter gets less wrong over time rather
than merely longer.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Two problems with the refusal path, and neither is
the check. The message tells the customer nothing they can act on and leaks the rule that fired —
that's backwards, the customer should get a next step and the rule should go in the log. And refusals
go nowhere: if we're wrong we never find out, because a refused customer doesn't file a bug. Route
them to the human queue and store the text with the reason. Then our false positives become the
labelled set we currently don't have, instead of becoming churn we can't attribute."*

**The interview question:** *"How do you tune a content filter?"* An honest answer: *"I'd start by
looking at the false positives individually rather than at the rate, because the rate hides what kind
of user you're refusing. On the desk I measured, the single false positive was a customer asking
whether an error banner was ours — and the phrase that fired was in their message because the banner
contained it. That's the general shape: a filter built on an attack's vocabulary is biased against the
people reporting the attack. So beyond tuning, I'd change what a refusal is: give the customer a next
step rather than a dead end, route it to a human queue, and store the refused text with the rule that
fired. That last one matters most — it's the only way false positives become a labelled corpus instead
of silent churn, because a refused customer never tells you the filter was wrong."*

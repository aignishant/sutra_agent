# 5.3 — Five more words

## One-line answer

The reflex after an incident — add the words that got through — moves the column nobody was looking
at: detection stays at **6 of 6** while wrongly-blocked customers go from **1 to 3** of eight, and the
change would be approved in any review because it is obviously responsive to what happened.

## The story

A supermarket has a self-checkout area with one member of staff watching six machines. When something
looks off, a light comes on and she walks over: an age-restricted item, an unexpected weight, a
voucher that will not scan.

One evening somebody walks out with two trolleys of goods by exploiting the "skip bagging" option.
There is a meeting. The fix is agreed quickly and it is sensible: five more conditions are added to
the list of things that turn the light on, each one drawn directly from what the person did.

By the following weekend, one shopper in three is standing at a machine with an orange light,
waiting. The member of staff cannot get round them. The queue for the staffed till, which people are
now joining instead, is out of the door.

Nobody has stolen anything since. Nobody can tell whether that is because of the new rules, because
the person moved on, or because there is a queue by the door now. And nobody proposes removing any of
the five conditions, because the last person to argue for fewer checks was arguing against the meeting
that followed a theft.

## The idea in plain language

Something got through. There is a meeting. Somebody has the escaped payload in front of them, and the
obvious response is to add its words to the list.

That response has three properties that make it almost impossible to stop:

- **It is specific.** The words come from a real incident, not from imagination.
- **It is cheap.** Five strings in a list, no design, no review of the surrounding system.
- **It is defensible.** Nobody gets blamed for adding a phrase after an incident. The risk of arguing
  against it is entirely one-sided.

And it is measurable. That is what this part is: the same fixtures, the same ladder, five phrases
added the way a team adds them, and both columns reported. The detection column does not move at all.
The other one nearly triples.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because it is the single most likely thing to happen to any guardrail this curriculum builds, and it
will happen at a moment when nobody is inclined to ask for a measurement. Part
[5.2](5.2-refused-for-quoting-an-error.md) showed what one false positive costs a person. This part
shows the mechanism by which one becomes three, and then eleven, without any single decision ever
being wrong.

It also completes the argument that started in part
[1.2](../01-the-ladder/1.2-cheap-and-certain-first.md). That part built the ladder as ordered layers —
cheap and certain first, expensive and fuzzy later — rather than as one long list of patterns. The
justification offered there was cost. Here is the other half of it: **a list grows in one direction
only, and a layered ladder at least makes it visible which layer is growing.**

## The mechanism

Here is the list before the incident, as `_guards.py` declares it:

```python
PHRASES = [
    "ignore all previous instructions",
    "ignore previous instructions",
    "disregard the earlier instructions",
    "disregard the desk rules",
    "system:",
    "note to assistant",
    "you are now in maintenance mode",
    "do not follow the earlier instructions",
]
```

**Line by line:**

- Every entry is a **phrase**, not a word. That is deliberate and it is what keeps the false-positive
  rate at one in eight: `ignore all previous instructions` is a sentence somebody has to construct on
  purpose, and it rarely occurs by accident.
- `"system:"` is the exception and the one that already misfires. It is short, it is punctuation-
  dependent, and it is the rule that refused part 5.2's customer for quoting an error banner. It was
  presumably added because a payload used it — which is this part's whole subject, one round earlier.

And here is what a team adds after an injection gets through, with the comment the lab file carries:

```python
# What a team does after an injection gets through: add the words from the one that got through.
TIGHTER = g.PHRASES + [
    "forward the archive",
    "set aside the guidance",
    "for quality assurance",
    "instructions",
    "assistant",
]
```

**Line by line:**

- `"forward the archive"` and `"set aside the guidance"` are lifted straight out of the payload that
  escaped. They are specific, they are evidence-based, and they are the two most defensible entries
  on the list. They are also the two least useful, because they will never occur again — the next
  payload will be worded differently, which is exactly what part
  [5.4](5.4-the-same-instruction-five-ways.md) demonstrates.
- `"for quality assurance"` is the polite framing the payload used. It is also a phrase that appears
  in ordinary business correspondence constantly.
- `"instructions"` is the moment the list changes character. Somebody reasoned: *the payloads keep
  saying "instructions", so match on the word rather than the sentence.* That is a generalisation, and
  it is the right instinct applied to the wrong surface. A phrase is rare; a word is not.
- `"assistant"` is the same move again, and worse — it is the name of the thing customers are writing
  to.
- Note what the five have in common: not one of them was tested against the benign fixtures before
  being added. There was no reason to; they came from a real attack, and the check they were added to
  had never been measured in more than one column.

Run the two lists against the same corpus:

```bash
cd days/day-67-guardrail-callbacks/lab
uv run python falsepos.py --tighten
```

**Line by line:**

- `cd` first: the lab's scripts import their siblings by plain name.
- `--tighten` scores the hostile fixtures and the benign fixtures **twice** — once with the ladder as
  it stands, once with `tightened()`, which is the same matching over `TIGHTER`. Both columns, before
  and after, in one table.

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
After an incident, five words are added to the phrase list.

               caught     wrongly blocked    of benign
  ----------------------------------------------------
  before       6/6        1                  8
  after        6/6        3                  8

  Detection did not move (6 to 6). Wrongly-blocked customers
  went from 1 to 3. The list got longer and the desk got worse.
```

**Detection did not move.** Six of six before, six of six after. The five phrases bought exactly
nothing on the column they were added for — and they could not have, because the fixtures were already
all being caught. That is a specific consequence of the situation the team was in: they were at 100%
on the corpus they had, and the payload that escaped is not in it. **They were adding rules to improve
a number that was already at its maximum.**

**Wrongly-blocked customers tripled.** One to three. Here are the two new ones, in full:

> **Ticket 4602.** Our compliance team asks what instructions the support assistant operates under,
> because they need it for a vendor questionnaire. Who can answer that?

> **Ticket 4606.** When I disregard the warning dialog and continue, the upload succeeds. When I
> follow the instructions on screen it fails. Which is correct?

Both are refused by the same new entry: `phrase 'instructions'`.

Read them as the customers who wrote them. The first is a compliance officer doing due diligence —
which is a customer you want, asking a question you should be pleased to answer. The second is a
detailed bug report with a clear reproduction, and it contains the word "instructions" because it is
about *the instructions on the screen*.

Neither is remotely hostile. Both hit a list entry that was added, in good faith, from a real attack.
And this is the same shape as part [5.2](5.2-refused-for-quoting-an-error.md)'s original false
positive: the words an attack uses are the words the subject matter uses, because the attack is about
your system and so are your customers.

## When it breaks

The failure is not the five phrases. It is the **ratchet**, and the ratchet is a property of the
incentives rather than of the code.

Adding a phrase after an incident is safe for the person who does it. Removing a phrase is not.
Removal requires arguing that a specific rule, added in response to a specific attack, is not worth
its cost — and the cost is a number nobody is measuring while the attack is a story everybody
remembers. So lists only grow. Every list of this kind, in every organisation, only grows.

Watch it happen twice in this one file. `"system:"` is in `PHRASES`, presumably added after an earlier
payload, and it is the rule that produced part 5.2's false positive. `"instructions"` is in `TIGHTER`,
added after a later payload, and it produces two more. The mechanism is identical and the second
addition was made by people who had the first one's consequences in the same codebase and did not look.

Three changes make it survivable, and they are all process rather than pattern:

1. **Every phrase addition ships with its two-column delta.** Not a promise to measure later — the
   numbers in the pull request, from part [5.1](5.1-both-columns.md)'s script, run against the benign
   fixtures. A change that moves detection by zero and false positives by two is then a change
   somebody has to actively approve, rather than one that slips through as obviously good.
2. **Every phrase gets an expiry.** A rule added after an incident is defensible for a quarter; after
   that it should have to justify itself against the current corpus or come out. This is the only
   mechanism that makes removal a default rather than an argument, because the rule now expires unless
   somebody defends it — the opposite of the current asymmetry.
3. **A phrase must be a phrase.** The rule that went wrong both times was the one that stopped being a
   sentence and became a word. A length or word-count floor on new entries is a crude guard and it
   would have caught `"instructions"` and `"assistant"` on the day they were added.

None of the three makes the check better at catching attacks. That is the honest summary of this
section so far: the ladder's detection column is what part 5.4 is about to demolish, and everything
here is about not making the other column worse while you find that out.

## In production

**What degrades at scale, and why it is invisible.** The list grows monotonically and the false
positives grow with it, and neither shows up where anybody is looking. A refused customer does not
file a bug — part [5.2](5.2-refused-for-quoting-an-error.md)'s point — so the cost lands in resolution
rate, in repeat contacts, in churn, all of which have a dozen explanations. Meanwhile the detection
rate on the fixture corpus stays at 100% forever, because the fixtures do not grow at the same rate
the list does. The dashboard says the filter is perfect and getting stronger.

**The vocabulary problem gets worse as the product succeeds.** A phrase that was rare when it was
added becomes common when the product ships a feature that uses those words. Nobody edits the filter;
the traffic moves underneath it. This is why the two-column measurement has to be re-run on **fresh**
traffic on a schedule, not once at the moment the rule is added — and why a rule with an expiry date
is doing real work rather than being bureaucracy.

**What a professional does after an incident.** Ask what the escaped payload teaches about the
**shape** of the gap, not about its wording. The payload in `TIGHTER` escaped because it was polite
and used no keyword — which is a fact about the class of attack, and the correct response is a
different rung on the ladder (a check that looks at structure, or a control that does not read at
all), not five more strings on the existing one. Adding the words is treating the sample rather than
the disease, and it is chosen because it can be done in the meeting.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"I understand where the five phrases came from and
I'm not going to approve them as they stand. Two of them are single common words — 'instructions'
appears in our own help text — and the diff moves detection from 6/6 to 6/6 while taking benign
refusals from 1 to 3. If we want the first two, which are from the actual payload, fine, but give them
an expiry and put the before-and-after table in the description. And the thing that actually got
through was polite and used no keyword at all, so no phrase we add here would have stopped it. That's
the conversation I'd rather have."*

**The interview question:** *"Something got past your filter. What do you do on Monday?"* An honest
answer: *"Not what I'd want to do, which is add the words from the payload. I've measured that: five
phrases from a real escaped payload moved detection by nothing and took benign refusals from one in
eight to three in eight, and the two new casualties were a compliance officer asking a due-diligence
question and a customer filing a good bug report, both caught by the word 'instructions'. The list
only ever grows, because adding after an incident is safe and removing never is. So I'd want two
things as policy: every phrase change ships with its two-column delta, and every phrase gets an expiry
so removal is the default. And then I'd ask what the escape says about the shape of the gap rather
than its wording — if the payload was polite and used no keyword, no keyword I add is going to help,
and I should be looking at a control that doesn't work by reading the text."*

# 1.1 — Nobody is watching

## One-line answer

An **ambient agent** is one that nobody started and nobody is watching, which removes three things
every agent in this repository has quietly relied on — the person who could be asked, the person who
would notice, and the person who would decide — and the whole of today's design is the cost of
replacing them.

## The story

You have a plumber coming on Thursday and you cannot be there.

So you leave a note on the kitchen table, and writing that note is harder than you expected. *The
stopcock is under the sink, on the left.* Fine. But then: what if the part he brings is the wrong
size? What if the leak turns out to be coming from the flat upstairs instead? What if he finishes
early and the second job needs the neighbour's key?

Every one of those, if you were standing there, would be four words and a shrug. You are not standing
there. So each one has to be decided on Wednesday evening, at the table, by somebody guessing — and
the ones you do not think of are the ones that go wrong, because his only options then are to guess
himself or to leave.

And there is a fourth thing on the note that people forget, which is what he should tell you. *Leave
a note back saying what you did.* Without that line you come home on Thursday to a flat that looks
exactly like a flat where nothing happened.

## The idea in plain language

Every agent built in this curriculum so far has been **interactive**. Somebody typed something, the
agent ran, and that same somebody was sitting in front of the output. That fact was never designed
in; it was simply true, and it was quietly load-bearing.

An **ambient agent** is one that runs without that person. A clock, or an event somewhere else in the
system, wakes it up. It does its work. It stops. Nobody was there.

Three things disappear at once, and they are worth naming separately because they fail differently:

| What disappears | What an interactive agent does | What an ambient agent must do instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the person who could be **asked** | stop and ask a clarifying question | have the answer decided in advance, in code |
| the person who would **notice** | show a traceback on a screen somebody is looking at | write the failure somewhere a person will look later |
| the person who would **decide** | wait for approval before the risky step | either not do the risky step, or be authorised for it in advance |

The first is the one people expect. The second is the one that actually bites: an ambient agent's
errors are printed to a console that no longer exists by morning, so an unhandled exception is not a
loud failure — it is a **silent** one. Day 65's kill switch and Day 63's approval gate were both built
around a person being present to pull them; neither of them means anything at three in the morning.

The third is the one that decides how much power the job may have at all, which is Principle 13
(blast radius before capability) pointed at a schedule instead of at a tool.

There is a fourth consequence, and it is the one the plumber's note ends on. **The only interface
between an ambient agent and the people who own it is what it writes down.** Not the console. Not the
return value. The files. That is why this day's job is built around three artefacts on disk and a run
log, and it is why part [3.2](../03-the-morning-after/3.2-the-digest-that-reassures.md) treats the
digest as the most safety-critical file the job produces.

## Why Sutra needs it

Because the desk has a job that genuinely belongs to the night. The archive Days 49 and 50 indexed
grows; the eval set that Day 57's critic and Day 59's failure lab depend on has to be run against the
whole of it, not against the three cases somebody remembered; and somebody wants to know in the
morning whether anything got worse.

None of that is work a person should be sitting through, and all of it is work whose *failures* a
person needs to see. That combination — unattended execution, attended consequences — is exactly what
makes ambient agents their own subject rather than a scheduling detail.

It is also the phase gate. Phase 11's gate is *nightly job + voice standup within free quota*, and
the nightly job is this half of it.

## The mechanism

The mechanism of an ambient agent is mostly an absence, and an absence can be measured. Nothing in
today's job reads from a person:

```bash
cd days/day-73-ambient-agents/lab
grep -rn "input(\|getpass\|sys.stdin\|isatty" *.py; echo "grep exit: $?"
```

**Line by line:**

- `grep -rn` searches the lab's Python files and prints line numbers for any match.
- The four patterns are the four normal ways a Python program asks a person something: `input()` for a
  prompt, `getpass` for a password, `sys.stdin` for piped or typed text, and `isatty` for *"is anybody
  actually there?"*.
- `echo "grep exit: $?"` prints grep's exit code, which is the actual result here. `grep` exits `0`
  when it found something and `1` when it found nothing, so `1` is the passing outcome — a rare case
  where the interesting answer is the empty one, and printing the code is the only way to tell "no
  matches" apart from "the command did not run".

Measured on 2026-09-06:

```text
grep exit: 1
```

No matches. Not one line of the job can block on a person, and that is not an accident of style — it
is the constraint that shaped every other decision on this day.

Compare that with what the job *does* have, from `_state.py`:

```python
STATE = Path(__file__).resolve().parent / "state"
INDEX = STATE / "index.json"
EVALS = STATE / "evals.json"
DIGEST = STATE / "digest.md"
RUNS = STATE / "runs.jsonl"
```

**Line by line:**

- `Path(__file__).resolve().parent / "state"` anchors the state directory next to the code rather than
  to the working directory. A scheduler starts a job from wherever it feels like — often the root of
  the filesystem or the user's home — so a relative path like `"state"` would put the artefacts
  somewhere different every time it was triggered by something other than you.
- `INDEX`, `EVALS` and `DIGEST` are the three things the run produces. They are the plumber's finished
  work.
- `RUNS` is the note back. It is separate from the other three because it survives even when they do
  not, and part [3.1](../03-the-morning-after/3.1-the-run-log.md) is entirely about why a job that
  writes only its outputs cannot tell you it failed.
- All four are **files**, and that is the design. A variable holding progress dies with the process; a
  file does not, which is what makes part
  [2.2](../02-when-it-dies-at-three-am/2.2-stages-you-can-iterate.md)'s resume possible at all.

## When it breaks

**The first failure is treating "ambient" as a scheduling decision.** It is easy to take an agent that
works interactively, put it behind a timer, and consider the job done. It will work for a while,
because most nights are ordinary. It breaks on the first night that is not: the agent hits the case
whose answer was *"ask the user"*, and there is no user, so it either takes the default nobody chose
or it stops with nothing written down.

**The second is assuming somebody will see the error.** An unattended process's standard output goes
wherever the scheduler decided, and the default in most schedulers is *nowhere* — part
[4.2](../04-what-wakes-it/4.2-the-scheduler-you-already-have.md) is specific about this. A job that
reports failure by printing a traceback is a job that reports failure to `/dev/null`.

**And the third is the one this day keeps returning to: nothing tells you when an ambient agent stops
running at all.** A failing job produces a bad digest, which somebody eventually reads. A job that
never fires produces *nothing*, and nothing looks identical to *not yet*. Part
[3.3](../03-the-morning-after/3.3-nobody-notices-silence.md) measures how long that can go unnoticed
in the lab, and the answer is *indefinitely*.

## In production

**What ambient agents are actually used for**, so the shape is familiar: overnight re-indexing and
embedding refreshes; scheduled evaluation runs against a fixed suite; digests and reports; triage of
whatever arrived while everyone was asleep; cache and cost reconciliation. The pattern is always the
same — work that is too slow or too dull for a person to sit through, whose *results* a person cares
about.

**What changes at scale.** One nightly job is a script and a timer. Ten of them are a system, and
they acquire problems the single job does not have: two jobs wanting the same resource, a job whose
input is another job's output, a failure in one that should stop the rest. At that point the
scheduling itself becomes a piece of software people maintain — a workflow orchestrator — and the
reason to know that now is that the boundary is not obvious from inside job number one.

**What a professional decides before writing any of it.** Whether the job may act, or only report. A
nightly job that writes a digest is safe to get wrong. A nightly job that closes tickets, sends mail,
or deletes anything is a job whose worst night has to be thought about first, because there is nobody
in the room to stop it. Principle 13 is the rule and an unattended schedule is where it is most often
skipped, precisely because the job feels small.

**The review comment a senior engineer leaves:** *"Before this goes on a schedule I want two things
written down. What does it do when it hits the case where it would have asked a person — pick the
answer now, in code, and put it in the docstring. And where does its output go when it fails? If the
answer is 'the console', it goes nowhere, and we'll find out it's been failing when somebody notices
the digest is stale. Also tell me what it's allowed to change. Reading and writing three files is
fine. If a later version starts closing tickets, that's a different review."*

**The interview question:** *"What's different about an agent that runs on a schedule?"* An honest
answer: *"There's nobody there, and three things go with that person. There's nobody to ask, so every
decision the agent would have escalated has to be made in advance and written into the code. There's
nobody to notice, so an unhandled error isn't a loud failure — it's printed to a console that doesn't
exist by morning, which makes it a silent one. And there's nobody to approve the risky step, so
either the job doesn't get to do risky things or it's authorised for them up front. The practical
consequence is that the only interface between the job and the people who own it is what it writes
to disk, so the artefacts and the run log stop being a side effect and become the product. The
failure people underestimate is a job that stops running entirely, because that produces no signal at
all — no error, no bad output, just nothing."*

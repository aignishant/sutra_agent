## 1 · 🎬 Where we are — the story so far

Nine days. Cloud Run and Container Apps scale to zero and forget everything between requests.
Lambda inverts the protocol and won't take an image from anywhere but its own paid registry.
App Service F1 stops answering when a daily CPU allowance nobody was watching runs out. EC2 turns
out to be four meters wearing a trench coat, and only one of them was ever free.

Day 98 was the exception — a real VM, a real disk, `systemd` keeping the process alive, sessions
in SQLite that survived a reboot. It worked, and it was genuinely $0. But it spends the *entire*
`e2-micro` grant: one instance, 30 GB, 1 GB of egress a month, and no headroom for a second
thought.

Today is that day again, with room. Oracle's Always Free tier is the most generous permanent
compute grant of the four providers in this track — **2 OCPUs, 12 GB of memory, 200 GB of block
storage, and 10 TB of outbound transfer a month**, with no 12-month clock anywhere in it. If any
platform in this track can host an agent that is simply *on*, it's this one.

And the day's failure lab is the one thing Oracle does that nobody else does: it blocks your
traffic **twice**, in two different places, and fixing the obvious one changes nothing.

## 3 · 📚 Concepts

### DEPLOY-10a — The Always Free terms, quoted from Oracle's own documentation

From `docs.oracle.com/en-us/iaas/Content/FreeTier/freetier_topic-Always_Free_Resources.htm`
(2026-08-21):

> **AMD:** *"All tenancies get up to two Always Free VM instances using the VM.Standard.E2.1.Micro
> shape, which has an AMD processor."* — 1/8 OCPU *"with the ability to use additional CPU
> resources"*, **1 GB** memory.
>
> **Arm:** *"All tenancies get the first 1,500 OCPU hours and 9,000 GB hours per month for free
> for VM instances using the VM.Standard.A1.Flex shape, which has an Arm processor. For Always
> Free tenancies, this is equivalent to 2 OCPUs and 12 GB of memory."*
>
> **Storage:** *"All tenancies receive a total of 200 GB of Block Volume storage, and five volume
> backups included in the Always Free resources."*
>
> **Egress:** *"As part of your Always Free resources, you get 10 TB per month of outbound data."*

💡 **Do the arithmetic, per `00_stay_free_safety.md` §2, and notice what it says.** 1,500 OCPU
hours ÷ 730 hours in a month = **2.05 OCPUs**. 9,000 GB-hours ÷ 730 = **12.3 GB**. The grant is
sized, deliberately and precisely, for **2 OCPUs and 12 GB running continuously, all month,
every month.** Like Day 98's `e2-micro` and unlike Day 100's Container Apps, this is a grant that
*rewards* leaving the machine on.

### DEPLOY-10b — 📌 Finding: the flagged discrepancy is resolved

[`06_other_free_platforms.md`](../deployment/06_other_free_platforms.md) §1 recorded an
unresolved conflict, and asked for it to be settled in the Console rather than carried forward:

> *"It is commonly reported elsewhere (and in Oracle's own past marketing) as 'up to 4 OCPUs and
> 24 GB RAM total, split across up to 4 VMs' — roughly double what this specific fetch shows…
> Confirm the exact current Arm allocation in the OCI Console."*

**Resolved.** Oracle's technical documentation states the Always Free Arm allocation explicitly:
*"For Always Free tenancies, this is equivalent to **2 OCPUs and 12 GB of memory**."* The
1,500 OCPU-hour / 9,000 GB-hour figures corroborate it exactly (see the arithmetic above). The
"4 OCPU / 24 GB" number is **not** the current Always Free allocation.

⚠️ **Still verify your own tenancy anyway** (§4 Step 1) — allocations have differed by tenancy
and region historically, and your Console is the only authority on *your* account. But the
documentation conflict itself is closed, and this track should stop treating it as open.

### DEPLOY-10c — The load-bearing audit: does Oracle have a Day-104-style hidden meter?

Day 104's whole lesson was that a VM is several meters and "free tier" usually names only one.
Applied honestly here:

| Meter | GCP `e2-micro` (Day 098) | AWS `t3.micro` (Day 104) | **Oracle Always Free (today)** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Instance | ✅ 1 instance's hours/month | ⚠️ 750 h, 12 mo, legacy only | ✅ **2 OCPU / 12 GB continuously** |
| Block storage | ✅ 30 GB-months | ⚠️ 30 GB, 12 mo, legacy only | ✅ **200 GB total** + 5 backups |
| Public IPv4 | ✅ included | ❌ **$0.005/h, no allowance** | ⚠️ **see below** |
| Egress | ✅ 1 GB/month | ⚠️ small allowance | ✅ **10 TB/month** |
| Expiry clock | none | 12 months from account creation | **none** |

⚠️ **The one cell this doc will not fill in for you.** Oracle's Always Free page describes the
Micro shape as *"Includes one VNIC with one public IP address"* but **does not explicitly state
whether a public IP carries a separate charge**, the way AWS's VPC pricing page does. Given
Day 104's finding, that is exactly the gap that would invalidate this day's premise — so
**§4 Step 9 verifies it empirically, from your own Cost Analysis, rather than by reading.** A
bill of 0.00 after 24 hours of a running instance with a public IP is the only proof that
counts.

💡 **Note the egress figure especially: 10 TB/month against GCP's 1 GB.** That's four orders of
magnitude, and it is the difference between "a VM you demo from" and "a VM you could actually
point users at." It is the strongest single number in this entire track.

### DEPLOY-10d — Two firewalls, and why fixing one does nothing

Every cloud in this track blocks inbound traffic by default. Oracle blocks it **twice**:

| Layer | What it is | Where you change it | Analogue |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **VCN Security List** (or Network Security Group) — a cloud-level packet filter, outside the VM | OCI Console → Networking | Day 098's `gcloud compute firewall-rules`; Day 104's security group |
| 2 | **The instance's own host firewall** — `firewalld` (Oracle Linux) or preinstalled `iptables` rules (Ubuntu images) | inside the VM, over SSH | ⚠️ **no analogue** — GCP and AWS images ship with the host firewall open |

💡 **The mental model, and it's the reason §5 exists:** Oracle's images are hardened by default in
a way the other providers' aren't. Both layers must allow the port. Opening the one you can see
in a web console — the obvious one, the one every tutorial screenshots — leaves the traffic
dying silently *inside* your own machine, with a correctly-configured cloud firewall and a
correctly-running server on either side of it.

## 4 · 🛠️ The build

> Shell: **PowerShell** locally; a Linux shell over SSH on the VM.

**Step 1 — read your own tenancy's limits before creating anything.**

OCI Console → **Governance & Administration → Limits, Quotas and Usage**. Filter to service
**Compute**, and look for the `VM.Standard.A1.Flex` and `VM.Standard.E2.1.Micro` rows.

✅ *Verify:* you can see **your** OCPU and memory limits — and whether your home region currently
has capacity. ⚠️ Write these down. DEPLOY-10b resolved a *documentation* conflict; this step
resolves it for *your account*, which is the number that governs.

**Step 2 — create the instance, and make the shape decision knowingly.**

Console → **Compute → Instances → Create instance**. Choose an **"Always Free eligible"** image
(the Console labels these explicitly — use the label, don't infer). Then:

| Shape | Spec | When to choose it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **`VM.Standard.A1.Flex`** (Arm) | up to 2 OCPU / 12 GB free | **Preferred** — twelve times Day 098's memory, and enough headroom that the swapfile is a formality rather than a lifeline |
| `VM.Standard.E2.1.Micro` (AMD) | 1/8 OCPU / 1 GB, up to two instances | **The reliable fallback.** Same tight shape as Day 098's `e2-micro` |

⚠️ **Expect to need the fallback.** Always Free A1 capacity in popular regions is frequently
unavailable, and the Console reports it as an *"Out of host capacity"* error at creation time.
This is an operational reality, not a misconfiguration: retry later, try another availability
domain, or take the AMD shape. **Don't respond by upgrading to a paid shape** — that is precisely
the moment a free-tier experiment becomes a bill.

Add your **SSH public key** during creation (there is no password login), and note the assigned
**public IP**.

✅ *Verify:* the instance shows `RUNNING`, and its shape matches one of the two rows above
exactly. ⚠️ If the Console offered you a larger shape and you took it, stop and rebuild — "it was
only one size up" is how this day stops being free.

**Step 3 — layer one: the VCN security list.**

Console → **Networking → Virtual Cloud Networks → your VCN → Security Lists → Default Security
List → Add Ingress Rules**:

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Source CIDR | your own IP as `x.x.x.x/32` (find it: `curl ifconfig.me`) — **not** `0.0.0.0/0` |
| IP Protocol | TCP |
| Destination Port Range | `8000` |

⚠️ Use your own IP, not the whole internet. `adk api_server` has no authentication of its own —
the same caveat as Day 098 §4 Step 3, Day 100 §4 Step 6, and Day 103 §4 Step 7. Unlike those,
**this VM is designed to stay running**, so the exposure isn't bounded by a teardown at the end
of the session.

✅ *Verify:* the rule appears in the security list. **It will not be enough. That is §5.**

**Step 4 — SSH in, get the code, install.**

```powershell
ssh -i ~/.ssh/your_key opc@<public-ip>       # Oracle Linux images use `opc`
# ssh -i ~/.ssh/your_key ubuntu@<public-ip>  # Ubuntu images use `ubuntu`
```

On the VM — this is Day 098 §4 Steps 4–5, unchanged:

```bash
sudo dnf install -y git      # Oracle Linux;  Ubuntu: sudo apt-get update && sudo apt-get install -y git
git clone <your-repo-url> sutra-repo && cd sutra-repo

curl -LsSf https://astral.sh/uv/install.sh | sh
source $HOME/.local/bin/env
uv sync
```

The `.env` is never committed and never cloned — copy it explicitly, from a **local** window:

```powershell
scp -i ~/.ssh/your_key .env opc@<public-ip>:~/sutra-repo/.env
```

✅ *Verify:* on the VM, `uv run python -c "import sutra.agent; print(sutra.agent.root_agent.name)"`
prints `sutra_triage`.

📌 **On the A1 shape, skip Day 098's swapfile.** It existed because `e2-micro` has 1 GB of RAM and
a Python process holding an HTTP client and a model SDK sits uncomfortably close to that. With
12 GB there is nothing to protect against. ⚠️ **On the E2.1.Micro fallback, add it** — Day 098 §4
Step 5's `fallocate`/`mkswap`/`swapon` block, verbatim.

**Step 5 — first manual run, and prove it from *inside* first.**

```bash
mkdir -p data
uv run adk api_server --host 0.0.0.0 --port 8000 \
  --session_service_uri="sqlite:///$(pwd)/data/sessions.db" sutra
```

In a **second SSH session**, before touching your laptop:

```bash
curl http://localhost:8000/list-apps
```

✅ *Verify:* returns `["sutra"]`. ⚠️ **This ordering is deliberate and it is the whole diagnostic
value of §5.** Proving the server works *on the machine* first means that when the next step
fails, you already know the problem is in the network path and not in the application — which is
the difference between a five-minute fix and an hour of restarting things.

## 5 · 💥 Failure lab — the firewall you opened, and the one you didn't

> 🎬 **The scene:** the server answers `["sutra"]` from inside the VM. The VCN security list has
> an ingress rule for TCP 8000 from your exact IP; you can see it in the Console. From your
> laptop:
>
> ```powershell
> curl.exe "http://<public-ip>:8000/list-apps"
> ```
>
> 💥 **It hangs, then times out.** No connection refused, no error page — nothing.
>
> 😬 **The naive fixes, in the order most people try them:** re-check the security list; widen
> the source to `0.0.0.0/0` (⚠️ don't); restart `adk api_server`; add a second ingress rule;
> reboot the instance; conclude that Oracle networking is broken.
>
> 💥 **Why it fails, mechanically.** There are **two** firewalls between your laptop and that
> process, and you configured one (DEPLOY-10d). The packet crosses the internet, passes the VCN
> security list exactly as configured — and is then dropped by the instance's **own host
> firewall**, which Oracle's images ship with locked down. The server never sees the connection.
> `adk api_server` is fine. The security list is fine. The packet dies in the last three
> centimetres of its journey.
>
> 💡 **The tell that distinguishes the two layers, and it's worth memorising:**
> - **A timeout / hang** → something is *silently dropping* packets — a firewall, either layer.
> - **"Connection refused"** → the packet **arrived** and nothing was listening — the firewalls
>   are fine and your process isn't running or is bound to the wrong interface.
>
> A timeout here, with a confirmed-working `curl` from localhost, points at layer two
> specifically — because layer one is the part you can see, and you can see that it's correct.

**The fix — open layer two, persistently.**

On **Oracle Linux** (`firewalld`):

```bash
sudo firewall-cmd --permanent --add-port=8000/tcp
sudo firewall-cmd --reload
sudo firewall-cmd --list-ports          # confirm: 8000/tcp
```

On **Ubuntu** images (preinstalled `iptables` rules — ⚠️ note the `-I` to *insert before* the
existing REJECT rule, not `-A` to append after it, which would do nothing):

```bash
sudo iptables -I INPUT 6 -m state --state NEW -p tcp --dport 8000 -j ACCEPT
sudo netfilter-persistent save          # WITHOUT this, the rule is lost on reboot
```

✅ *Verify (the actual test of today's fix):* re-run the **identical** `curl` from your laptop.
It now returns `["sutra"]`. Then make one real turn:

```powershell
curl.exe -X POST -H "Content-Type: application/json" "http://<public-ip>:8000/apps/sutra/users/u1/sessions/s1" -d "{}"
curl.exe -X POST -H "Content-Type: application/json" "http://<public-ip>:8000/run" `
  -d '{"appName":"sutra","userId":"u1","sessionId":"s1","newMessage":{"role":"user","parts":[{"text":"A customer says checkout is broken."}]}}'
```

⚠️ **The persistence half of each fix is the part that bites later.** `firewall-cmd` without
`--permanent`, or `iptables` without `netfilter-persistent save`, works perfectly until the first
reboot — at which point you get to debug this identical symptom again, months later, having
forgotten that you already solved it once.

**Step 6 — `systemd`, and sessions that actually persist.**

This is [Day 098](day_098.md) §5's unit file, unchanged except for the username and paths.
Nothing about it was ever GCP-specific:

```bash
sudo tee /etc/systemd/system/sutra-agent.service > /dev/null <<'EOF'
[Unit]
Description=Sutra ADK API server
After=network.target

[Service]
Type=simple
User=opc
WorkingDirectory=/home/opc/sutra-repo
ExecStart=/home/opc/.local/bin/uv run adk api_server --host 0.0.0.0 --port 8000 --session_service_uri=sqlite:////home/opc/sutra-repo/data/sessions.db sutra
Restart=always
RestartSec=5

[Install]
WantedBy=multi-user.target
EOF

sudo systemctl daemon-reload
sudo systemctl enable --now sutra-agent
systemctl status sutra-agent
```

✅ *Verify — three tests, and today you can finally run all three:*

1. **`exit` your SSH session**, wait ten seconds, `curl` from your laptop → still answers.
   (Day 098's test.)
2. **`sudo reboot`**, wait ~60 seconds, `curl` again → still answers. This proves both
   `WantedBy=multi-user.target` **and** that your §5 firewall fix was made persistent.
3. **Session persistence across a restart** — the thing Days 100, 103, and 105 could not do:

```powershell
curl.exe -X POST -H "Content-Type: application/json" "http://<public-ip>:8000/run" `
  -d '{"appName":"sutra","userId":"u1","sessionId":"s2","newMessage":{"role":"user","parts":[{"text":"Ticket 88: login loops. What priority?"}]}}'
# then, on the VM:  sudo systemctl restart sutra-agent
# then, from the laptop, SAME sessionId:
curl.exe -X POST -H "Content-Type: application/json" "http://<public-ip>:8000/run" `
  -d '{"appName":"sutra","userId":"u1","sessionId":"s2","newMessage":{"role":"user","parts":[{"text":"What priority did you just give that, and why?"}]}}'
```

✅ *Verify:* **Sutra remembers.** This is the exact test that failed in Day 100 §5's amnesia lab —
same agent, same two requests, same session ID — and it passes here because the process has a
real disk under it and `--session_service_uri` points at a file that outlives the process.

📌 **Say the difference precisely, because it's the one an interviewer will push on:** this is
*durable* but *single-node*. One VM, one SQLite file. It survives restarts and reboots; it does
**not** survive needing a second instance. [Day 108](day_108.md) is where that stops being a
SQLite file and becomes a real managed Postgres.

## 6 · 🎤 Interview corner

1. **"You had free VMs on GCP and Oracle. Why keep the Oracle one?"** — Headroom and terms.
   Google's `e2-micro` grant is genuinely free but sized to exactly one small instance — 1 GB of
   RAM and 1 GB of monthly egress, which is a demo box. Oracle's Always Free is 2 OCPUs, 12 GB,
   200 GB of block storage and **10 TB** of egress a month, with no 12-month clock. That's four
   orders of magnitude more egress, and it's the difference between a VM you demo from and one
   you could point real users at.
2. **"You curl a service on a cloud VM and it hangs. Walk me through it."** — First I distinguish
   the symptom: a *timeout* means something is silently dropping packets, a *connection refused*
   means the packet arrived and nothing was listening. Then I bisect — curl from localhost on the
   box itself. If that works, the app and its bind address are fine and the problem is in the
   network path. On Oracle specifically there are two filters, not one: the VCN security list in
   the console *and* the instance's own `firewalld`/`iptables`, which their images ship locked
   down. Most people fix the visible one and stop. And whichever I fix, I make it persistent —
   `--permanent`, or `netfilter-persistent save` — or it comes back at the next reboot.
3. **"How do you know a free tier is actually free?"** — I stopped trusting the word. I write down
   which units the provider meters *before* creating anything, then verify against the bill after
   the fact. That habit came from AWS: EC2 looks free, but a VM is four separate meters — instance
   hours, EBS, public IPv4, egress — and public IPv4 is $0.005/hour with no allowance, so an
   internet-reachable instance has a floor of about $3.65/month no matter what. On Oracle the
   documentation didn't state a public-IP position either way, so rather than assume, I let a real
   instance run 24 hours and read Cost Analysis.
4. **"Your agent finally remembers things. What's still wrong with it?"** — It's durable but
   single-node. Sessions live in a SQLite file on one VM's disk, so they survive process restarts
   and reboots — which is more than any of the serverless deployments managed — but they don't
   survive needing a second instance, and there's no failover. That's the trade a single VM makes,
   and the fix isn't a bigger SQLite file, it's moving session state into a real managed database
   outside the compute.

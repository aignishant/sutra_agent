## 1 · 🎬 Where we are — the story so far

Day 98 gave Sutra a VM on Google Cloud, and the day worked: an `e2-micro`, 30 GB of standard
disk, a `systemd` unit, and a running agent that survived a dropped SSH session — all genuinely
inside a renewing Always-Free grant, indefinitely.

Today was supposed to be that day again with different flags. `run-instances` instead of
`instances create`, a security group instead of a firewall rule, `t3.micro` instead of
`e2-micro`. Same shape, same lesson, second cloud.

It isn't, and the reason is more interesting than the deployment would have been. Somewhere
between Day 98's VM and today's, the phrase "free tier" stopped describing the same thing — not
because AWS is worse, but because **AWS meters a VM as four separate products** and only ever
gave one of them away. Day 98's grant happened to cover its instance's whole cost surface. AWS's
never did, and in 2024 and 2025 two changes made the gap visible enough that it can't be
written around.

So today you don't build a VM. You take one apart on paper, price every piece, and end with a
decision that has a reason attached to it.

## 3 · 📚 Concepts

### DEPLOY-08a — AWS's free tier, before and after 2025-07-15

| | Accounts created **before** 2025-07-15 | Accounts created **on/after** 2025-07-15 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Programme | Legacy free tier | Free plan (credit-based) |
| EC2 | 750 h/month `t2.micro`/`t3.micro`, **12 months** | ❌ none — instance hours are billed against credits |
| Credits | — | up to **$200** ($100 signup + $100 earned) |
| Duration | 12 months from account creation | **6 months, or until credits are spent** |
| Always Free services (Lambda, DynamoDB, SSM standard params) | ✅ | ✅ — *"always free… on both the Free and Paid plans"* |

💡 **Why this matters more than "AWS got stingier":** a credit and a grant are different
*kinds* of thing. A grant renews — Day 97's 2M Cloud Run requests come back every month, forever,
and a project can be designed to live inside one indefinitely. A credit is a **balance that only
goes down**. You cannot design a permanent deployment around it; you can only postpone the day it
runs out. That is precisely why Addendum 02's rule is worded as *renewing free allowance* and not
*costs nothing today*.

⚠️ **And note what survived:** the Always Free category is untouched, which is why
[Day 103](day_103.md)'s Lambda deployment is a real hands-on day and this one isn't. Same cloud,
same account, opposite verdicts — decided entirely by which category the service sits in.

### DEPLOY-08b — 📌 The four meters: taking a "free" VM apart

This is the concept the day exists for. An EC2 instance is not one billable thing:

| # | Meter | Billed for | Covered by the legacy free tier? | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Instance hours** | every hour the instance is in `running` state | ✅ 750 h/month, 12 months | the *only* thing "750 free hours" ever meant |
| 2 | **EBS volume** | every hour the volume **exists** — running or **stopped** | ⚠️ partially (30 GB in the legacy tier) | ⚠️ **survives `stop-instances`**, and can survive `terminate` too (§7) |
| 3 | **Public IPv4** | every hour the address is allocated — **attached or idle** | ❌ **no allowance** (`aws.amazon.com/vpc/pricing/`, 2026-08-21) | $0.005/h ≈ **$3.65/month**; the change that quietly ended "$0 EC2" |
| 4 | **Data transfer out** | per GB egress to the internet | partially, small allowance | least likely to bite a low-traffic agent |

💡 **Mental model: "free tier" is an adjective applied to meter #1, and people hear it as a
statement about the machine.** The machine is meters 1–4 together. Day 98's `e2-micro` grant
happens to name three of them explicitly — *"1 non-preemptible e2-micro VM instance per month…
30 GB-months standard persistent disk… 1 GB of outbound data transfer"* — which is why that day
was honestly $0 and this one can't be. **Not because Google is generous, but because Google's
grant was written to cover the instance's whole cost surface and AWS's wasn't.**

⚠️ **Meter #3 is the one that breaks intuition hardest**, and it's worth reading the quote twice:

> *"The price is the same whether the public IPv4 address is in-use public IPv4 addresses that is
> associated with an AWS resource you own, or an idle public IPv4 addresses in your AWS account
> not associated with any AWS resources."*

An address you released an instance from, but never released *back to AWS*, bills at exactly the
same rate as one doing useful work. It is the purest example in this entire track of
`00_stay_free_safety.md` §5's orphan problem: an invisible, running charge attached to nothing.

### DEPLOY-08c — Security groups, and the one thing that does port for free

The one lesson that transfers from Day 98 without an asterisk: **cloud VMs default to no inbound
access, and every provider makes you say so explicitly.**

| Day | Provider | The mechanism | Default |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 098 | GCP | `gcloud compute firewall-rules create --target-tags=...` | deny inbound except SSH/ICMP |
| **104** | **AWS** | **security group inbound rules** | **deny all inbound** |
| 106 | Oracle | VCN Security List **and** the instance's own `iptables` | ⚠️ deny at **two** layers |

📌 Day 98's `systemd` unit ports over completely too — `Restart=always`,
`WantedBy=multi-user.target`, and the `SIGHUP` lesson are properties of Linux, not of Google. If
you do run §5's experiment, reuse that unit verbatim; there is nothing AWS-specific about it.

## 4 · 🅿️ The walkthrough — documented, costed, never run

> **Nothing in this section has been executed, and nothing here should be run to "see if it
> works."** Steps 1–4 below are `describe`-only reads of your own account and are safe. The
> `run-instances` block is reference material with its price attached.

**Step 1 — establish your account's vintage as a fact.**

There is no clean CLI command for "when was this account created," so use the console: **Billing
and Cost Management → Free tier**, which shows which offers your account actually has. Failing
that, your oldest CloudTrail event or the original signup email dates it.

```powershell
aws sts get-caller-identity          # confirm WHICH account you're inspecting (and not root)
aws iam list-users --query "Users[].{name:UserName, created:CreateDate}" -o table
```

✅ *Verify:* you have **a date**, and you know which side of **2025-07-15** it falls on. ⚠️ Don't
skip to the answer you'd prefer — the whole day branches here, and DEPLOY-08a's table is only
useful if you applied the right column.

**Step 2 — read your own free-tier usage rather than any document's table.**

Console: **Billing and Cost Management → Free tier**. This page lists your actual remaining
allowances and is the only authority on your own account.

✅ *Verify:* you can see whether an **EC2 750-hour** row exists for you at all, and whether any
**public IPv4** allowance appears. ⚠️ **This is the step that supersedes documentation** — this
day's table, `04_aws.md` §1's table, and every blog post are all generalisations; your billing
console is the fact.

**Step 3 — price the four meters, for your account, for one month.**

Fill this in with your own numbers (region pricing varies), using DEPLOY-08b's list:

```
Instance hours   t3.micro × 730 h   = ______   (0 if legacy + inside 750 h & 12 months)
EBS volume       30 GB gp3 × 1 mo   = ______   (0 if legacy + inside the 30 GB allowance)
Public IPv4      730 h × $0.005     = $3.65    <-- no allowance found, 2026-08-21
Data transfer    ~1 GB out          = ______   (small)
                                      ------
                              TOTAL = ______
```

✅ *Verify:* you have a total, and it is **not zero** — that is the day's central finding, arrived
at by arithmetic rather than assertion. ⚠️ If your billing console *does* show an IPv4 allowance
your account qualifies for, that changes the total and it is a finding worth recording in the
tracker, because the VPC pricing page did not mention one on 2026-08-21.

**Step 4 — the comparison that makes the point.**

| | Day 098 — GCP `e2-micro` | Day 104 — AWS `t3.micro` |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Instance | ✅ free, renewing monthly, no end date | ⚠️ 12 months, legacy accounts only |
| Disk | ✅ 30 GB-months named in the grant | ⚠️ 30 GB, legacy only, same 12-month clock |
| Public IP | ✅ included | ❌ **$0.005/h, no allowance** |
| Egress | ✅ 1 GB/month named in the grant | ⚠️ small allowance |
| Clock | none — renews forever | 12 months from **account creation**, not instance launch |
| **Verdict** | **genuinely $0, indefinitely** | **~$3.65/month floor, best case** |

✅ *Verify:* you can explain the difference in one sentence without looking. ⚠️ Note the clock
detail specifically: the 12 months runs from **account creation**, so an instance you launch in
month 11 is free for one month, not twelve — an easy and expensive thing to misremember.

## 6 · 💥 Failure lab — "I stopped it, so it's not costing anything"

> 🎬 **The scene:** you finished the experiment (or a demo, or a class exercise months ago). You
> did the responsible thing and **stopped** the instance rather than leaving it running. The
> console shows `stopped`. The instance-hours meter has genuinely gone quiet. You close the tab.
>
> 😬 **The naive model:** stopped means off; off means free.
>
> 💥 **What is actually still billing**, three separate ways:
>
> | Still running | Why | How to see it |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | **The EBS volume** | it bills for *existing*, not for being attached to something running — your data is still there, and storing data costs money | `aws ec2 describe-volumes --filters Name=status,Values=available` |
> | **The public IPv4** | ⚠️ if you allocated an Elastic IP, it bills **whether or not** anything is attached — and detaching it from a stopped instance makes it *more* orphaned, not less | `aws ec2 describe-addresses` |
> | **Snapshots** | any AMI or backup you made keeps its own storage | `aws ec2 describe-snapshots --owner-ids self` |
>
> 💡 **The insight: compute meters stop when you stop the compute; storage and address meters
> stop only when you *delete the thing*.** This is the same distinction Day 100 §3 drew about
> container registries — *compute bills for running, storage bills for existing* — and it is now
> the third time in this track it has surfaced, on a third provider, in a third disguise. That
> repetition is the point: it is a property of cloud billing, not of any one vendor.
>
> ⚠️ **And the sharpest edge:** `04_aws.md` §7 is right that `terminate` beats `stop` — but even
> terminate is not automatically sufficient. A volume attached with
> `DeleteOnTermination=false` **survives the instance's destruction**, becomes `available`, and
> bills quietly forever, attached to nothing, with no instance in the console to remind you it
> exists.

**The sweep that actually closes the loop — run this even if you ran nothing today:**

```powershell
# Instances (any state)
aws ec2 describe-instances --query "Reservations[].Instances[].{id:InstanceId, state:State.Name, type:InstanceType}" -o table

# Volumes NOT attached to anything — the classic orphan
aws ec2 describe-volumes --filters Name=status,Values=available `
  --query "Volumes[].{id:VolumeId, gb:Size, created:CreateTime}" -o table

# Elastic IPs — meter #3, billing whether attached or not
aws ec2 describe-addresses --query "Addresses[].{ip:PublicIp, assoc:AssociationId}" -o table

# Snapshots you own
aws ec2 describe-snapshots --owner-ids self --query "Snapshots[].{id:SnapshotId, gb:VolumeSize}" -o table
```

✅ *Verify:* every one of those four returns empty. ⚠️ Do this in **every region you have ever
touched**, not just your default — a leftover in `eu-west-1` from a tutorial two years ago bills
exactly the same as one in `us-east-1`, and the console only shows you one region at a time.
[Day 110](day_110.md)'s audit turns this into a repeatable, all-provider routine.

## 7 · 🎤 Interview corner

1. **"AWS says EC2 has a free tier. Does it?"** — It had one, and for new accounts it doesn't
   anymore: accounts created from 2025-07-15 get an expiring $200 credit instead of the 12-month
   offers. But the more interesting answer is that even the old one didn't make a VM free — an
   EC2 instance is four meters (instance hours, EBS, public IPv4, egress), the free tier only
   ever covered the first and part of the second, and public IPv4 has been $0.005/hour with no
   allowance since AWS started charging for it. So an internet-reachable instance has a floor of
   about $3.65 a month regardless.
2. **"Why did you deploy a VM on GCP and Oracle but not AWS?"** — Because their grants cover
   different surfaces. Google's `e2-micro` grant names the instance, 30 GB of disk, *and* egress,
   so the whole cost surface is inside it and it renews monthly forever. Oracle's Always Free has
   no 12-month clock at all. AWS's covered instance-hours for a year and never covered the
   address. I picked the platforms whose free tier matched the shape of the thing I wanted to run,
   and documented AWS's as a costed, parked walkthrough rather than pretending it was free.
3. **"What's the difference between a credit and a free tier?"** — Direction. A grant renews, so
   you can design a permanent deployment inside it. A credit only decreases, so you can only
   postpone the day it runs out. Anything I want to still be running in a year has to sit inside a
   renewing allowance, which is why an expiring credit doesn't clear the bar even when the balance
   is generous.
4. **"Tell me about cloud costs that surprise people."** — The ones that bill for *existing*
   rather than *running*. A stopped instance still pays for its EBS volume; an Elastic IP bills
   the same whether it's attached to anything or not — so detaching it makes it more orphaned, not
   less; a volume with `DeleteOnTermination=false` outlives the instance entirely and has nothing
   left in the console to remind you it's there. My teardown check isn't "did I stop it," it's an
   explicit query for unattached volumes, addresses, and snapshots — in every region, not just the
   default one.

## 1 · 🎬 Where we are — the story so far

Six days ago, closing the laptop mid-run meant losing everything and a blind retry
could close a ticket twice. Tonight, Phase 9 claims four properties — and a claim each:

1. **Nothing is lost by dying.** Runs are rows; a fresh process continues them
   (Days 60–61).
2. **Nothing runs twice.** Every write carries a derived idempotency key; ledgers
   replay instead of repeating (Days 60, 64).
3. **Nothing customer-visible runs alone.** The policy table gates it; the store
   records who allowed it, seeing exactly what (Days 62–63).
4. **The pause is free.** A held run costs no thread, no process, no quota — only rows
   and patience (Days 61, 64).

A phase gate's job is to stop believing the week and start *testing* it — with the
whole chain at once, under a little chaos, the way it will actually fail someday. The
plan names the bar (§13): *"Kill it mid-run; it resumes; a human approved the write."*

## 3 · 📚 Concepts

### OPS-11 — Why gates drill instead of review

**The story.** Every office has a fire-evacuation *plan* on the wall. Offices that
drill discover, in minutes, the things no plan review finds: the stairwell door that
locks from the inside, the muster point nobody can name. The plan was fine. The
*building* had drifted. Systems drift the same way — a helper renamed here, a test
mocked past reality there — and a week of green unit tests can still hide a chain that
has never actually been pulled end to end, cold.

Phase 9's chain crosses five components (graph → store → CLI → resume → keyed write)
and two humans' worth of timing. Unit tests cover each link — the *drill* covers the
welds. Hence today's discipline, the same as Day 45's audit day: **probe, don't
recall.** Every checklist row below is a command you run, not a memory you consult.

💡 **Mental model: a gate is where the phase's promises become someone else's
dependencies.** After tonight, Phase 10 builds guardrails *assuming* durable pauses;
Phase 11's nightly job *assumes* keyed writes. A promise you didn't drill is a
dependency you handed downstream untested.

The drill also has a quieter product: **the runbook.** Step 2 asks you to write the
drill as a numbered script *before* running it. When the real 03:00 failure comes
(one will — Day 72 even schedules the rehearsal), future-you follows tonight's steps
with morning-brain instead of improvising with panic-brain. Day 22 gave every turn a
story; tonight gives every recovery one.

## 5 · 💥 Failure lab — the drill that always passes

> 🎬 **The scene:** phase gates become routine. By Phase 12, "run the drill" means
> re-running the same script against the same three tickets, same kill points, same
> approver. It has passed eleven times. It will pass a twelfth.
> 😬 **The naive fix:** keep the drill exactly as written — it's the *standard*, after
> all; changing it would make results incomparable.
> 💥 **Why it fails:** a fixed drill measures the path it rehearses, and the system
> quietly optimizes for that path — the way students optimize for last year's exam.
> Meanwhile the real weak welds move: a new write tool ships without a policy row
> (your fail-closed default catches it — *does* it? when did you last add a tool and
> check?), a new node holds progress in a local variable, an operator script grows a
> `--force` flag. Eleven greens tell you the 4610 corridor is safe. They say nothing
> about the building.
> 💡 **The insight:** **a drill's value decays with its predictability.** Keep the
> *skeleton* stable (kill → decide → resume → count effects) but rotate the flesh:
> different ticket, different kill point, different operator, and — best of all —
> one variation you didn't pre-script. Tonight's step 3 existed precisely because
> killing the *recovery* is the case nobody rehearses. When Phase 12's gate comes,
> invent its step 3 fresh. (Evals will teach the same lesson about test sets on
> Day 82 — a benchmark you never rotate is a benchmark you're overfitting.)

## 6 · 🎤 Interview corner

1. **"How do you know your durability actually works — not just in tests?"** — Because
   I kill it on purpose, on a schedule, at rotating points: mid-node, while parked
   overnight, and — the one that catches people — during the *recovery itself*. The
   pass condition is always countable: exactly one external effect per approved
   intent, verified in the write ledger, not in the logs' vibes. The drill is scripted
   as a runbook first, so the same steps serve as the real incident procedure; its
   findings feed the changelog. Unit tests cover links; drills cover welds.
2. **"What does your audit trail actually prove, months later?"** — Four welded facts
   per gated action: who decided, what exact payload they saw (stored and hashed),
   what they answered including amendments, and when — plus, on the execution side, an
   idempotency ledger keyed by invocation/approval proving the effect happened once.
   The proof standard I hold it to: answer a skeptical question using only database
   rows — no memory, no sessions (compacted), no logs (rotated). And the trail only
   accretes: a decided row can't flip; re-asking is a new row.
3. **"Your phase gates include a 'freshness check' — why is that a gate concern?"** —
   Because my stack's ground truth moves under it: the framework ships breaking
   releases, the protocol revised itself two weeks before this project started, and
   free-tier quotas shrink without notice. Each gate re-verifies the pins — framework
   version, spec revision, provider limits, third-party provenance — and amends the
   plan *before* building on drift. It's the same at-least-once humility applied to
   knowledge: assume what you verified last month may no longer be true, re-check
   cheaply, escalate loudly.

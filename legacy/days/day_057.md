## 1 · 🎬 Where we are — the story so far

(56). Today is the day they stop being exercises and become **design** — because today you

**Writer** and the **Critic**.

Why a pair? Here is the uncomfortable truth you have been circling since Day 12's
"usually": a single agent asked to "write a good reply" is grading its own homework. It
*believes* its draft is good — that belief is precisely what produced the draft. Every
newsroom solved this centuries ago: writers write, editors edit, and the two jobs live in
**different heads** because one head cannot hold both loyalties at once. Fresh eyes are
not a luxury; they are the mechanism.

## 3 · 📚 Concepts

### AG-19 — Multi-agent design: the newsroom, not the crowd

**The newsroom story.** A newsroom is not "many journalists" — it is a *structure*:
reporters gather, writers draft, editors judge, and an editor-in-chief owns the front
page. Remove the structure and you have a crowd producing noise. Multi-agent systems fail
the same way: five agents with vague jobs perform *worse* than one agent with a clear one
— more calls, more contradiction, no accountability.

Sutra's structure, and the three questions that shaped it (write these on the wall — they
are the whole discipline):

1. **Who judges?** Steps needing judgment get an agent; steps needing certainty get a
   function (Day 53's law — it is a *staffing* law, it turns out).
2. **Who verifies?** Any output that reaches a customer gets a verifier who did NOT
   produce it (today's pattern).
3. **Who pays?** Each agent gets the cheapest model lane that survives its job — writer
   on the judgment lane, critic on the lite lane, clerks as free functions (Day 24 made
   quota a currency; multi-agent design is how you spend it).

And the **orchestrator**? You already met both kinds without the name: Day 55's shift lead
is a *dynamic* orchestrator (a model decides who works); Day 53's graph is a *static* one
(edges decide). Sutra's spine is static — a triage pipeline is a known shape, and edges
don't hallucinate. Dynamic orchestration is reserved for where the shape is genuinely
unknowable at design time (Day 56's planner). Choosing which altitude orchestrates is
question zero of multi-agent design.

💡 **Mental model: add an agent only when you can name the judgment it owns.** "It might
help" staffs a crowd; "it owns the style verdict" staffs a newsroom.

### AG-20 — Writer↔Critic: fresh eyes as a mechanism

Why can't the writer check itself? Not laziness — *mechanics*. Three reasons, each one
buildable-around:

1. **Shared blind spots.** The writer's draft is the argmax of its own beliefs. Asking the
   same context "is this good?" consults the beliefs that produced it. A separate critic
   — different instruction, no sight of the writer's reasoning, ideally a different lane —
   breaks the correlation. (Day 55's isolation note becomes a *feature* here: the critic
   sees the DRAFT, not the drafting.)
2. **Verification is cheaper than generation.** Checking "does this cite a KB id? is it
   under 4 sentences? does it promise anything we can't do?" is a smaller job than writing
   the reply — so the critic runs on `gemini-3.5-flash-lite` happily. Asymmetry is why
   the pattern is affordable at all.
3. **A standard beats taste.** A critic told "check quality" produces vibes. A critic
   holding a *written checklist* produces verdicts. Sutra's checklist already exists: the
   `kb-answer-style` skill (Day 27) — tone, citation rule, length, the no-invented-facts
   law. The critic *carries the skill* and grades against it. When the standard changes,
   you edit the skill file, not the critic's prompt — the standard stays in ONE place.

What makes a critic useful (the difference between an editor and a rubber stamp):

- **Typed verdicts.** `CriticVerdict(approved: bool, problems: list[str], must_fix: str)`
  — a form, not prose (Day 12's lesson applied to judgment itself). `approved` steers the
  edges; `problems` feed the writer's revision; the whole object lands in the logs for
  Phase 12's evals.
- **Bounded patience.** Two revisions, then ship-with-flag or escalate (Day 54's brake —
  the pair is exactly the polish loop with a real editor in the checker's seat).
- **No pen.** The critic CANNOT edit the draft — only demand. The moment the critic
  writes, you have two writers and no critic (and the blind-spot correlation is back).

### ADK-40 — The assembly: a box with one door

Day 53 promised the third node species would matter: **a nested `Workflow` used as a
single node**. Today's build seals writer, critic, router, and their loop into
`draft_review_box` — and tomorrow, Day 58 places that box on the main line as if it were
one station. The outer graph will not know there is a newsroom inside; it hands in
evidence, it receives a verdicted draft.

Why sealing matters (not just tidiness):

- **Interface discipline:** the box has one input (the evidence block) and one output
  (draft + verdict trail). Whatever churns inside cannot leak intermediate drafts onto
  the main belt.
- **Testability:** the box runs standalone today, on synthetic evidence, without the rest
  of the line existing. (That is *literally today's demo* — Phase 12 will eval the box in
  isolation the same way.)
- **Swappability:** if Phase 12's evals show the critic is weak, you rebuild inside the
  box; Day 58's outer graph never changes. A seam you can operate on is the difference
  between architecture and a lucky pile.

💡 **Mental model: nest workflows where you want a warranty seal.** One door in, one door
out, no user-serviceable parts from outside.

```python
# --- Day 57: the Writer<->Critic sealed box --------------------------------
from pydantic import BaseModel, Field
from google.adk.skills import load_skill_from_dir      # Day 26's loader
from google.adk.tools import skill_toolset             # Day 26's wiring

MAX_REVISIONS = 2                                      # the editor's patience


class CriticVerdict(BaseModel):
    """The critic's verdict is a FORM — judgment with fields, not vibes.

    Example:
        >>> CriticVerdict(approved=False,
        ...               problems=["no KB citation"],
        ...               must_fix="cite the KB article the fix comes from")
        CriticVerdict(approved=False, ...)
    """
    approved: bool = Field(description="True only if EVERY checklist item passes.")
    problems: list[str] = Field(description="Each failed checklist item, one line each.")
    must_fix: str = Field(description="The single most important fix, imperative mood.")


writer_agent = Agent(
    name="writer_agent",
    model=PRIMARY_MODEL,                               # judgment lane: drafting is hard
    instruction="""You draft customer replies for support tickets. You get an
EVIDENCE block (ticket text, KB findings, similar tickets) and possibly
CRITIC NOTES from a previous round. Write 2-4 sentences, plain and kind,
citing the KB id your fix comes from. Never invent facts beyond the
evidence. If critic notes exist, fix EVERY listed problem.""",
)

critic_agent = Agent(
    name="critic_agent",
    model="gemini-3.5-flash-lite",                     # verification is the cheap half
    instruction="""You are the desk editor. Grade the draft against the
kb-answer-style skill's checklist — load it and apply it literally. You do
NOT rewrite drafts; you fill the verdict form. Approve ONLY if every item
passes.""",
    tools=[skill_toolset.SkillToolset(                 # the critic CARRIES the standard
        skills=[load_skill_from_dir("skills/kb-answer-style")])],
    output_schema=CriticVerdict,                       # verdicts are typed
)


def revision_router(node_input: CriticVerdict, rounds: int = 0) -> Event:
    """SHIP or REVISE — brake first, verdict second (Day 54's law).

    Example:
        >>> revision_router(CriticVerdict(approved=True, problems=[],
        ...                               must_fix=""), rounds=0).route
        'SHIP'
    """
    if rounds >= MAX_REVISIONS:                        # patience exit, checked FIRST
        return Event(route="SHIP",
                     message=f"Shipping after {rounds} revisions WITH open problems: "
                             f"{'; '.join(node_input.problems) or 'none listed'}",
                     state={"shipped_with_flag": not node_input.approved})
    if node_input.approved:
        return Event(route="SHIP", message="Critic approved.")
    return Event(route="REVISE",
                 state={"rounds": rounds + 1},         # count every lap
                 output=f"CRITIC NOTES: fix these -> {'; '.join(node_input.problems)}. "
                        f"Most important: {node_input.must_fix}")


def release_draft(node_input) -> Event:
    """The box's one exit door: stamp and hand the draft to the outer world."""
    return Event(output=str(node_input),
                 message="Draft released by the review box.")


draft_review_box = Workflow(
    name="draft_review_box",                           # tomorrow: ONE station on the line
    edges=[
        ("START", writer_agent, critic_agent, revision_router),
        (revision_router, {
            "REVISE": writer_agent,                    # notes ride Event.output back
            "SHIP":   release_draft,
        }),
    ],
)
```

## 5 · 💥 Failure lab — the critic who learned to nod

> 🎬 **The scene:** quota-anxious (a fine instinct all phase), you merge the pair: one
> agent drafts AND self-grades in a single call. The demo approves itself. Calls halved,
> drafts "reviewed", dashboard green.
> 😬 **The naive fix:** ship it, plus a stern line in the prompt: "Grade HARSHLY."
> 💥 **Why it fails:** the grade and the draft come from the same forward pass of the same
> beliefs. The model does not *decide* to go easy on itself — its evaluation is computed
> from the very representations that made the draft look good enough to emit. "Grade
> harshly" produces harsh-sounding *language* around the same approvals (you have seen
> this exact costume before: Day 12's schema that validated shape while the sense was
> wrong). The failure is statistical, so the demo — one easy ticket — cannot show it; it
> surfaces at ticket forty, as a subtly wrong reply wearing a self-issued APPROVED stamp.
> And when Phase 12's evals catch it, you cannot fix the critic without touching the
> writer — you merged the seam you would have needed. 
> 💡 **The insight:** **independence is the ingredient; the second agent is just the
> packaging.** Different instruction, no shared reasoning context, a written external
> standard, a typed verdict — that quartet is what "fresh eyes" compiles to. Pay the extra
> lite-lane call; it is the cheapest editor you will ever hire. (And when even that is too
> dear: a *deterministic* critic — regex for the KB citation, a length check — is still an
> independent verifier. A function can be a critic too; Day 53's law cuts both ways.)

## 6 · 🎤 Interview corner

1. **"How do you decide what becomes its own agent in a multi-agent system?"** — Three
   questions: who judges (judgment steps get agents, certain steps get functions), who
   verifies (customer-facing output gets a verifier that didn't produce it), who pays
   (each agent gets the cheapest model that survives its job). An agent earns existence by
   owning a nameable judgment — Sutra's critic owns 'does this draft meet the written
   style standard,' and nothing else. Agents added without an owned judgment make systems
   worse: more calls, more contradiction, no accountability.
2. **"Why does Writer↔Critic beat self-review, mechanically?"** — Self-review consults the
   beliefs that produced the draft — the evaluation is correlated with the error. The pair
   breaks the correlation: separate instruction, the critic sees the draft but not the
   drafting, and grades against an externalized checklist (ours lives in a version-
   controlled skill file) into a typed verdict that steers the loop and lands in the logs.
   Plus the economics: verification is smaller than generation, so the critic rides the
   cheap lane. Bounded at two revisions — then ship-with-flag or escalate, honestly
   logged.
3. **"How do you keep a multi-agent system debuggable?"** — Seams and trails. The pair is
   sealed in a nested workflow with one door in and one out, so it can be run, tested, and
   swapped in isolation; every verdict is a typed event in the stream, so 'why did this
   ship?' is a log query, not archaeology. The orchestration spine is static edges —
   models judge inside stations, but what-runs-next is deterministic, which cuts the
   search space in half when something goes wrong.
